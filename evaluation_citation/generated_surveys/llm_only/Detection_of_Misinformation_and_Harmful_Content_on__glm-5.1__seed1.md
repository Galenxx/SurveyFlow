# 1 Abstract
The explosive growth of short-form video platforms has transformed content consumption, but it has also introduced unprecedented challenges in moderating misinformation and harmful content. Unlike traditional text or image-based social media, short-form videos combine fast-paced audio, visual, and textual modalities, making robust detection extremely complex. This survey provides a comprehensive review of the current landscape in detecting misinformation and harmful content on short-form video platforms, focusing on methods, datasets, and multimodal approaches. We begin by formalizing the problem space and highlighting the unique characteristics of short-form videos that complicate traditional moderation techniques. We then systematically examine state-of-the-art detection methods, categorizing them into unimodal baselines and advanced multimodal architectures that leverage the interplay between transcribed speech, visual frames, and on-screen text. Furthermore, we critically assess existing datasets and evaluation benchmarks, identifying a significant gap in resources tailored specifically for short-form video moderation. We also discuss the challenges of cross-platform generalization and the evolving nature of multimedia manipulation. By synthesizing findings from diverse studies, this survey outlines the limitations of current paradigms and emphasizes the urgent need for large-scale, multimodal datasets and real-time detection frameworks. Ultimately, this work aims to serve as a foundational resource for researchers and practitioners dedicated to fostering safer and more trustworthy short-form video ecosystems.

# 2 Introduction
Over the past decade, social media has undergone a profound paradigm shift from text-centric platforms to ecosystems dominated by visual and multimedia content. At the forefront of this transformation are short-form video platforms, such as TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts. These platforms have captivated billions of users globally through highly engaging, algorithmically curated feeds that deliver bite-sized, rapid-fire content. While this format has democratized content creation and fostered new forms of digital community, it has also created a fertile ground for the proliferation of misinformation and harmful content. Malicious actors exploit the multimodal nature and viral mechanics of these platforms to spread deepfakes, doctored media, hate speech, and coordinated disinformation campaigns at an alarming scale (Davidson et al., 2017). 

The moderation of short-form video content presents unique and formidable challenges that distinguish it from traditional text or image-based moderation. First, the multimodal composition of short-form videos—simultaneously encompassing moving visuals, auditory tracks, on-screen text, and user interactions—creates a complex signal space where harmful intent can be obfuscated across modalities. A video featuring innocuous visual content might propagate harmful narratives through its audio or overlaid text, evading unimodal detection systems. Second, the ephemeral and fast-paced nature of these videos, often lasting mere seconds, requires real-time or near-real-time processing to mitigate viral spread before it causes significant harm. Third, the sheer volume of content uploaded daily overwhelms human moderation teams, necessitating automated, scalable solutions.

Despite the growing urgency of this issue, the research community lacks a unified understanding of the current state of automated detection in this domain. Existing surveys often focus on text-based fake news or general multimedia analysis, leaving a critical gap in the systematic evaluation of methods specifically designed for short-form video ecosystems (Guo et al., 2022). This survey aims to bridge that gap by providing a comprehensive overview of the detection of misinformation and harmful content on short-form video platforms.

The scope of this survey encompasses the algorithmic methods, available datasets, multimodal strategies, and persistent challenges defining this research frontier. We systematically examine the transition from traditional unimodal baselines to sophisticated multimodal architectures that seek to fuse heterogeneous signals. Additionally, we review the datasets currently used to train and evaluate these models, highlighting the severe scarcity of resources tailored to the nuances of short-form video. By synthesizing these disparate strands of research, we provide a holistic view of the field's progress and its remaining bottlenecks.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section 3 explores the evolution of detection methods, from unimodal to multimodal approaches. Section 4 reviews existing datasets and evaluation benchmarks. Section 5 discusses the specific challenges inherent in short-form video analysis. Section 6 examines the critical issue of cross-platform and cross-lingual generalization. Section 7 concludes the survey, and Section 8 outlines promising future directions for the field.

# 3 Unimodal and Multimodal Detection Methods
The detection of misinformation and harmful content has historically relied on unimodal approaches, primarily focusing on textual analysis. Early methods applied natural language processing techniques to user comments, video descriptions, or automatically transcribed audio to identify linguistic markers of deception or toxicity. While computationally efficient, these methods are fundamentally limited in short-form video contexts, where critical context is often embedded in visual or auditory cues rather than spoken text. Similarly, purely visual approaches analyzing individual video frames can detect manipulated media or explicit imagery but fail to capture the narrative context provided by the audio or overlaid text.

To overcome these limitations, the field has increasingly shifted toward multimodal detection methods, which integrate information across textual, visual, and auditory modalities. Multimodal architectures are designed to capture the complex interplays and potential inconsistencies that are hallmarks of multimedia misinformation, such as a manipulated visual track that contradicts the audio narrative. Early multimodal efforts utilized feature-level or decision-level fusion, simply concatenating embeddings from separate unimodal encoders. However, these shallow fusion strategies often fail to model the fine-grained cross-modal interactions necessary for nuanced detection.

More recently, advanced neural architectures have been introduced to better capture cross-modal dynamics. For instance, event adversarial neural networks have been proposed to extract multimodal features that are robust to event-specific biases, allowing models to generalize better to unseen misinformation events (Wang et al., 2018). Furthermore, frameworks leveraging pre-trained language and vision models, such as those utilizing BERT and ViT, have demonstrated significant improvements by aligning visual and textual representations in a shared semantic space. Approaches like SpotFake have utilized these pre-trained representations to bridge the semantic gap between modalities, achieving state-of-the-art performance by deeply integrating contextual text and visual features (Singhal et al., 2020). Despite these advancements, effectively modeling the temporal alignment and asynchronous nature of audio-visual-textual data in short videos remains a complex algorithmic challenge.

# 4 Datasets and Evaluation Benchmarks
The development and evaluation of robust detection models are fundamentally constrained by the availability of high-quality, large-scale datasets. In the domain of misinformation and harmful content detection, there is a pronounced scarcity of datasets specifically curated for short-form video platforms. Most prominent benchmarks have been constructed for text-based social media or static image-text combinations. For example, widely used resources like FakeNewsNet provide rich social context and multimodal news content, but they are primarily centered around traditional news articles and their associated images, lacking the temporal and auditory dimensions of video (Shu et al., 2018).

Similarly, in the domain of harmful content, the Hateful Memes Challenge introduced a multimodal dataset requiring models to reason over both text and images to identify hate speech. While this dataset successfully highlighted the necessity of multimodal reasoning—as the harmful intent often relies on the interplay between the two modalities—it remains limited to static memes rather than dynamic video content (Kiela et al., 2020). The few existing video-based datasets for deepfake detection focus primarily on facial manipulation rather than broader misinformation narratives or harmful audio-visual combinations found on platforms like TikTok.

This gap in datasets presents a significant bottleneck for the research community. Short-form video datasets must capture not only the multimodal elements but also the platform-specific metadata, such as hashtags, music tracks, and engagement metrics, which are crucial for understanding virality and context. Furthermore, evaluation benchmarks for short-form video must account for the temporal nature of the content, requiring metrics that assess the precision of detection over time, rather than simple binary classification. Until the community invests in creating comprehensive, annotated short-form video datasets, models will continue to be trained and evaluated on data distributions that do not reflect the true complexity of the moderation challenge.

# 5 Challenges in Short-Form Video Analysis
Analyzing short-form videos for misinformation and harmful content introduces a unique set of technical and logistical challenges that differentiate it from traditional media analysis. One of the primary hurdles is the sheer volume and velocity of content generation. Platforms process millions of uploads daily, necessitating highly efficient, low-latency models capable of operating at scale. However, the computational cost of processing high-dimensional video data, extracting audio features, and performing multimodal fusion is substantial, often making real-time analysis infeasible with current architectures.

A further challenge lies in the evolving sophistication of the harmful content itself. The rise of deepfakes and advanced generative AI has made it possible to create highly realistic, synthetic media that is virtually indistinguishable from authentic content to the human eye. While significant research has been dedicated to deepfake detection, manipulations are becoming increasingly imperceptible, and detectors often struggle to generalize to novel generation techniques not represented in their training data (Tolosana et al., 2020). In short-form videos, these manipulations are further compressed and degraded by platform-specific encoding, which can obscure the artifacts that detection models rely on.

Additionally, the alignment and fusion of multimodal data in short videos present profound difficulties. Unlike long-form videos where scenes change slowly, short-form videos are characterized by rapid cuts, dynamic transitions, and asynchronous audio-visual events. Effectively aligning a transcribed spoken phrase with the precise visual frame it references requires sophisticated temporal modeling. The field of multimodal machine learning continues to grapple with the complexities of cross-modal alignment, fusion, and representation learning, particularly when dealing with noisy, unaligned data streams typical of user-generated content (Liang et al., 2022). Overcoming these challenges requires not only architectural innovations but also a deeper understanding of the creative and deceptive techniques employed by malicious actors on these platforms.

# 6 Cross-Platform and Cross-Lingual Generalization
A critical yet often overlooked challenge in the detection of misinformation and harmful content is the ability of models to generalize across different platforms and linguistic contexts. Most current detection models are trained and evaluated on data from a single platform, leading to highly domain-specific classifiers that fail when exposed to the distinct data distributions of other platforms. For instance, a model trained to detect misinformation on Twitter, which relies heavily on text and static images, will likely perform poorly on TikTok, where content is driven by short-form video, music, and platform-specific trends. This domain shift encompasses differences in user demographics, content formatting, and the very nature of how misinformation spreads (Shu et al., 2017).

Cross-lingual generalization presents an equally formidable barrier. Short-form video platforms are inherently global, hosting content in hundreds of languages and dialects. However, the vast majority of datasets and detection models are heavily biased toward English, leaving a significant gap in moderation capabilities for low-resource languages. Malicious actors frequently exploit this gap by repurposing and translating harmful content across linguistic boundaries, knowing that moderation tools are less effective in non-English contexts. The nuances of hate speech and misinformation are deeply tied to cultural and linguistic context, making direct translation an inadequate solution for cross-lingual detection.

Recently, the advent of large language models and large multimodal models has offered a potential pathway toward more generalizable solutions. These foundation models, pre-trained on vast corpora of multilingual and multimodal data, exhibit impressive zero-shot and few-shot capabilities, allowing them to transfer knowledge across domains and languages without extensive fine-tuning (Zhao et al., 2023). Leveraging these models for cross-platform and cross-lingual detection represents a promising frontier. However, ensuring that these models do not perpetuate biases or hallucinate facts when applied to novel cultural contexts remains an active area of research, requiring careful evaluation and robust alignment strategies.

# 7 Conclusion
The rapid ascent of short-form video platforms has fundamentally reshaped the digital landscape, presenting both unprecedented opportunities for creative expression and severe challenges for content moderation. As platforms like TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts continue to dominate global media consumption, they have concurrently emerged as primary vectors for the dissemination of misinformation and harmful content. This survey has systematically reviewed the current state of automated detection methods, datasets, and multimodal approaches tailored to this dynamic medium.

Our analysis reveals that traditional unimodal detection strategies are fundamentally inadequate for the complexities of short-form video. The inherent multimodality of these videos—where harmful intent is frequently obfuscated across visual, auditory, and textual channels—necessitates sophisticated fusion architectures capable of capturing fine-grained, temporal cross-modal interactions. While recent advances in multimodal machine learning, including attention mechanisms and pre-trained foundation models, have pushed the boundaries of detection performance, significant gaps remain. Models still struggle with the asynchronous nature of video data and the computational demands of real-time processing.

Furthermore, this survey highlights a critical bottleneck in the field: the severe scarcity of large-scale, annotated datasets specifically designed for short-form video moderation. Existing benchmarks are largely confined to text-image pairs or long-form video, failing to capture the unique characteristics of short-form content, such as rapid transitions, viral audio tracks, and platform-specific metadata. Without robust datasets that reflect the true distribution of harmful short-form videos, the development and fair evaluation of novel detection algorithms will remain stifled.

We also underscore the fragility of current models in the face of domain shifts. The inability of classifiers to generalize across different platforms and linguistic contexts is a critical vulnerability that malicious actors actively exploit. As the generative AI landscape evolves, the sophistication of deepfakes and synthetic media will only increase, further blurring the line between authentic and manipulated content. Ultimately, safeguarding the integrity of short-form video platforms requires a concerted, interdisciplinary effort to develop scalable, multimodal, and culturally aware detection systems that can adapt as quickly as the threats they are designed to mitigate.

# 8 Future Directions
As the ecosystem of short-form video platforms continues to evolve, the research community must pivot toward innovative strategies to keep pace with emerging threats. The most urgent future direction is the construction and curation of comprehensive, multimodal datasets tailored explicitly to short-form video. These datasets must move beyond binary labels of "fake" or "real" to provide fine-grained annotations of manipulation techniques, temporal spans of harmful segments, and cross-lingual metadata. Collaborative efforts between academia, industry, and platform providers are essential to facilitate access to such data while preserving user privacy.

Another critical avenue for future research lies in the development of efficient, real-time multimodal architectures. Given the immense volume and velocity of short-form video uploads, current computationally heavy models are often impractical for deployment. Future work should explore lightweight fusion techniques, model distillation, and edge computing solutions that enable low-latency inference without sacrificing detection accuracy. Additionally, architectures must become more adept at processing streaming data, identifying harmful content on-the-fly as the video is being uploaded, rather than relying on post-hoc analysis.

The integration of large multimodal models (LMMs) and large language models (LLMs) into the moderation pipeline presents a transformative opportunity. Future systems could leverage LMMs as foundational reasoning engines, utilizing their vast world knowledge and few-shot learning capabilities to detect novel, subtle forms of misinformation that lack sufficient training examples. However, this integration must be accompanied by rigorous research into the adversarial robustness of these models. As LMMs become integral to moderation, they will inevitably become targets for adversarial attacks. Ensuring that these systems are resilient to prompt injections, subtle perturbations, and distributional shifts is paramount.

Finally, future research must adopt a socio-technical perspective, recognizing that misinformation and harmful content are not merely technical anomalies but deeply human phenomena. Detection models should be designed to account for cultural nuances, local contexts, and the psychological mechanisms driving virality. By combining advanced multimodal AI with insights from the social sciences, we can develop moderation systems that are not only technically proficient but also contextually empathetic, ultimately fostering healthier and more trustworthy digital environments.
