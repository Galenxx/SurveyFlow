# 1 Abstract
Short-form video platforms like TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts have become dominant mediums for information consumption and entertainment. However, their highly engaging, multimodal nature also makes them potent vectors for the spread of misinformation and harmful content. This survey provides a comprehensive overview of the current landscape in detecting malicious content within short-form videos. We examine the unique challenges posed by the brevity, rapid pacing, and dense multimodal integration characteristic of these platforms. The survey systematically categorizes existing methodologies, ranging from traditional unimodal analysis to advanced multimodal approaches that fuse textual, auditory, and visual cues. We also review the available datasets utilized for training and benchmarking detection models, highlighting the gap between existing resources and the specific needs of short-form video analysis. Furthermore, we discuss the limitations of current real-time detection frameworks when deployed in dynamic, high-throughput platform environments. By synthesizing findings from computer vision, natural language processing, and audio analysis, we outline the critical intersections that drive state-of-the-art detection. Finally, we identify open problems and propose future research directions, emphasizing the need for robust, context-aware, and scalable multimodal architectures to safeguard the integrity of short-form video ecosystems.

# 2 Introduction
The proliferation of short-form video platforms has fundamentally transformed how global audiences consume information, communicate, and entertain themselves. Platforms such as TikTok, YouTube Shorts, and Instagram Reels prioritize brevity, typically limiting content to a few seconds or minutes, and heavily rely on algorithmic recommendation systems to maximize user engagement. While this format offers unprecedented creative expression and accessibility, it also presents significant societal risks. The concise, fast-paced nature of short-form videos often discourages critical evaluation, making users highly susceptible to misinformation, disinformation, and harmful content, including hate speech, cyberbullying, and dangerous viral challenges.

Unlike traditional text-based social media or long-form videos, short-form videos present a unique detection challenge due to their dense multimodal composition. Creators seamlessly blend visual effects, background music, voiceovers, and on-screen text to convey messages rapidly. A video might feature innocuous visual content while the audio track contains harmful rhetoric, or vice versa. Consequently, unimodal detection approaches—analyzing only the transcript or only the visual frames—are fundamentally insufficient. Effective moderation requires sophisticated multimodal approaches capable of understanding the complex interplay and potential inconsistencies between different modalities.

The motivation for this survey stems from the urgent need to consolidate fragmented research efforts across computer vision, natural language processing, and audio processing to address this growing threat. While significant progress has been made in text-based fake news detection and image-based deepfake detection, the holistic analysis of short-form video content remains an under-explored frontier. The rapid evolution of generative AI further complicates this landscape, lowering the barrier for malicious actors to produce highly convincing synthetic media at scale. Recent advances in large vision-language models have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in understanding complex multimodal relationships, yet these same architectures can be exploited or must be leveraged for defense (Radford et al., 2021; Du et al., 2022).

This survey aims to provide a structured and comprehensive review of the current state-of-the-art in detecting misinformation and harmful content specifically tailored to short-form video platforms. We scope our review to encompass the methodological foundations, available datasets, and practical challenges of deploying these systems. We deliberately focus on the intersection of multimodal learning and content moderation, bridging the gap between theoretical advances and practical platform deployment.

The structure of this survey is as follows: Section 3 explores multimodal approaches for video misinformation detection, detailing fusion techniques and cross-modal architectures. Section 4 reviews the datasets and benchmarks currently available, discussing their limitations in the context of short-form video. Section 5 examines the specific methodologies applied to detect harmful and hateful content within multimedia. Section 6 addresses the practical challenges of real-time detection and deployment on live platforms. Finally, Sections 7 and 8 offer concluding remarks and outline promising future research directions.

# 3 Multimodal Approaches for Video Misinformation Detection
Detecting misinformation in short-form videos necessitates a departure from traditional text-centric or image-centric verification methods. In these videos, misinformation is rarely confined to a single modality; instead, it emerges from the complex interplay between audio, visuals, and text. For instance, a video might use misleading out-of-context visuals while the voiceover presents a fabricated narrative, requiring models to perform cross-modal reasoning. The foundational principles of integrating these disparate data streams are rooted in multimodal machine learning, which provides taxonomies for fusion strategies—early, late, and hybrid fusion (Baltrušaitis et al., 2017). Recently, state-of-the-art approaches have shifted towards transformer-based architectures that utilize cross-modal attention mechanisms to deeply align visual and textual representations. Models originally designed for image-text alignment, such as VisualBERT, have been adapted to process video frames alongside transcribed audio, enabling the detection of subtle inconsistencies that unimodal models would miss (Li et al., 2019). By jointly encoding the visual frames and the audio transcript, these multimodal frameworks can identify semantic contradictions, such as a speaker's claim not matching the visual evidence, which is a common tactic in short-form video misinformation.

# 4 Datasets and Benchmarks for Short-Form Video Analysis
The advancement of detection models is heavily constrained by the availability of high-quality, large-scale datasets that reflect the nuances of short-form video platforms. Currently, there is a significant scarcity of datasets specifically curated for short-form video misinformation, forcing researchers to rely on benchmarks designed for other modalities or long-form content. For instance, many multimodal fake news detection models are trained and evaluated on datasets like Fakeddit, which, despite being a comprehensive multimodal benchmark, primarily consists of image-text pairs sourced from Reddit rather than short-form videos with audio-visual dynamics (Nakamura et al., 2019). Similarly, in the realm of manipulated media, datasets such as FaceForensics++ provide high-quality deepfake videos, but they focus primarily on facial swapping in controlled scenarios, which does not capture the rapid editing, visual effects, and diverse content types typical of TikTok or Instagram Reels (Rossler et al., 2019). This gap highlights a critical bottleneck: without ecologically valid benchmarks that include authentic short-form video metadata, user engagement metrics, and native audio-visual synchronization, it is difficult to assess the true efficacy of detection algorithms in real-world platform environments.

# 5 Detection of Harmful and Hateful Content in Short Videos
The detection of harmful and hateful content on short-form video platforms introduces additional layers of complexity compared to traditional text-based social media. Harmful content in videos often relies on visual symbols, specific audio tracks, or sarcastic tone, which can completely alter the meaning of an otherwise benign transcript. The challenge is further compounded by the use of coded language, emojis, and memes that evolve rapidly within platform subcultures. While significant strides have been made in text-based hate speech detection, researchers have long noted the difficulty of distinguishing harmful intent from merely offensive language, a problem that is exacerbated when context is stripped away (Davidson et al., 2017). To move beyond text, the research community has begun developing multimodal hate speech datasets that incorporate images and text, aiming to capture the cross-modal semantics of online abuse (Mathew et al., 2020). However, applying these insights to short-form videos requires dynamic temporal modeling. A short video might only reveal its hateful nature in the juxtaposition of a specific visual meme with a particular background track at a precise timestamp. Consequently, effective detection models must not only fuse modalities but also understand the temporal synchronization and cultural context that give rise to harmful narratives in short-form content.

# 6 Challenges in Real-Time Detection and Platform Deployment
Deploying misinformation and harmful content detection models on live short-form video platforms presents formidable engineering and algorithmic challenges. The sheer volume of videos uploaded every minute necessitates highly efficient, low-latency models capable of processing dense multimodal streams in real-time. Furthermore, the adversarial nature of content moderation means that malicious actors continuously adapt their strategies to evade detection, employing subtle audio distortions, visual perturbations, or algorithmic gaming. Early approaches to fake news detection struggled with generalization across different events and domains, a problem that adversarial training seeks to mitigate by forcing models to learn event-invariant features (Wang et al., 2018). However, the dynamic environment of short-form video platforms requires more than just robust feature learning; it demands scalable data mining pipelines that can handle the rapid turnover of trends and hashtags (Shu et al., 2017). Additionally, platform deployment must navigate the trade-off between detection accuracy and computational cost, as processing high-frame-rate video with large transformer models is prohibitively expensive at scale. Addressing these challenges requires the development of lightweight multimodal architectures and continual learning frameworks that can adapt to emerging threats without requiring complete model retraining.

# 7 Conclusion
The rapid rise of short-form video platforms has fundamentally reshaped the digital information landscape, creating an urgent and complex frontier in the battle against misinformation and harmful content. This survey has systematically reviewed the current landscape of detection methods, available datasets, and multimodal approaches specifically tailored to this dynamic medium. We highlighted that the inherent brevity and dense multimodal nature of short-form videos render traditional unimodal detection techniques—whether text, audio, or vision alone—fundamentally inadequate. Effective content moderation requires sophisticated fusion strategies, particularly those leveraging cross-modal attention mechanisms, that can parse the intricate interplay between visual, auditory, and textual cues. Despite recent advances in multimodal transformers and representation learning, their application to short-form video remains severely constrained by a critical bottleneck: the lack of representative, large-scale datasets. Existing benchmarks, whether composed of text-image pairs or long-form deepfake videos, fail to capture the rapid editing styles, native audio integration, and unique cultural nuances inherent to platforms like TikTok and Instagram Reels. Furthermore, the detection of harmful and hateful content is complicated by the pervasive use of coded language, visual memes, and temporal juxtapositions that require dynamic, context-aware modeling over time. We also discussed the practical, engineering-level challenges of deploying these complex models in real-time, high-throughput environments, emphasizing the pressing need for computational efficiency and robustness against adversarial evasion. As generative AI continues to lower the barrier for creating highly convincing synthetic and manipulated media, the urgency for scalable, robust, and multimodal detection systems will only intensify. Ultimately, the fight against short-form video misinformation is an arms race. As platforms introduce new creative tools and effects, malicious actors will inevitably adopt them to evade detection. Therefore, the research community must not only react to current threats but also anticipate future vectors of abuse. By fostering closer collaboration between academic researchers, industry practitioners, and policymakers, we can develop proactive, resilient moderation frameworks. Only through such coordinated, multidisciplinary efforts can we hope to mitigate the societal harm caused by the viral spread of deceptive and toxic content on the world's most popular video platforms.

# 8 Future Directions
Looking ahead, several promising research directions are critical for advancing the detection of misinformation and harmful content on short-form video platforms. First, there is an urgent need for the creation of ecologically valid, large-scale datasets specifically curated for short-form video. These datasets must include synchronized audio-visual data, platform-specific metadata, and fine-grained annotations for temporal events to enable the training and robust evaluation of dynamic multimodal models. Second, the development of lightweight, efficient multimodal architectures is essential for real-time deployment. Techniques such as knowledge distillation, sparse attention, and early-exit networks could allow the computational demands of large transformer models to be met within the resource constraints of live platform moderation pipelines. Third, future research must prioritize cultural and linguistic inclusivity. Short-form video platforms are global, and harmful content or misinformation often relies on localized slang, regional memes, and culturally specific visual cues. Current models, predominantly trained on English-centric data, frequently fail to generalize across diverse global contexts. Fourth, the integration of large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs) offers a transformative opportunity. Leveraging the vast world knowledge and reasoning capabilities of foundation models could enable zero-shot or few-shot detection of novel misinformation campaigns and emerging harmful trends, significantly reducing the lag between threat emergence and model adaptation. Finally, as detection methods evolve, so too will the tactics of malicious actors. Establishing robust adversarial defense mechanisms and developing frameworks for explainable AI (XAI) in multimodal contexts will be crucial. Explainability not only aids in refining models but also ensures transparency and fairness in content moderation, fostering greater trust between platforms and their users.
