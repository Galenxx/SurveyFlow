# 1 Abstract
Diffusion models have recently emerged as a powerful framework for generative modeling, achieving unprecedented fidelity in image synthesis. Driven by this success, researchers have rapidly adapted diffusion-based techniques to the domain of 3D shape and scene generation. However, extending these 2D image models to 3D introduces unique challenges, including the scarcity of high-quality 3D training data, the computational burden of high-dimensional 3D representations, and the complexity of evaluating generated 3D assets. This survey provides a comprehensive overview of the current state of diffusion models for 3D generation, systematically categorizing the literature into methods, representations, and evaluation protocols. We first examine the methodological paradigms, distinguishing between 3D-native diffusion approaches that train directly on 3D data and 2D-prior-based methods that leverage pre-trained 2D image diffusion models via score distillation. Next, we analyze the diverse 3D representations employed, ranging from explicit structures like point clouds and meshes to implicit representations such as signed distance functions and neural radiance fields, highlighting the trade-offs between fidelity, expressiveness, and computational efficiency. Furthermore, we explore the transition from isolated object generation to complex, holistic 3D scene synthesis. Finally, we review the emerging evaluation metrics and benchmarks designed to assess the quality, diversity, and text-alignment of generated 3D content. By synthesizing these dimensions, this survey offers a structured roadmap for navigating the rapidly evolving landscape of 3D diffusion models and identifies critical avenues for future research.

# 2 Introduction
The advent of denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPMs) and score-based generative models has fundamentally transformed the landscape of generative artificial intelligence. By learning to reverse a gradual noising process, diffusion models have demonstrated an unparalleled capacity to model complex, high-dimensional data distributions, establishing new state-of-the-art results in image synthesis, audio generation, and video creation. As the capabilities of 2D generative models have matured, a natural and highly consequential frontier has emerged: the generation of 3D shapes and scenes. The ability to automatically generate high-quality 3D assets holds immense promise for industries ranging from gaming and visual effects to architecture and augmented reality, where the manual creation of 3D content remains a significant bottleneck. Automating this pipeline through generative modeling could democratize 3D content creation, allowing novice users to synthesize complex 3D structures from simple text prompts or reference images.

Despite the tremendous potential, transitioning from 2D to 3D generation is fraught with fundamental challenges. The most pressing obstacle is the comparative scarcity of 3D training data. While 2D image datasets comprise billions of readily available samples scraped from the internet, large-scale 3D datasets are orders of magnitude smaller and often limited in diversity and geometric complexity. Furthermore, 3D data exists in a variety of representations—such as point clouds, voxel grids, meshes, and implicit neural fields—each imposing distinct computational constraints and inductive biases. The memory requirements for processing high-resolution 3D volumes severely limit the scalability of native 3D diffusion models, as operating directly on volumetric data incurs cubic memory costs (Müller et al., 2023). Additionally, evaluating the quality and utility of generated 3D assets remains an open problem, lacking the straightforward and universally accepted metrics available in 2D, such as the Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) or Inception Score (IS).

To navigate these challenges, the research community has developed a diverse array of strategies. One prominent line of work leverages the robust visual priors encoded in pre-trained 2D diffusion models to guide 3D synthesis, circumventing the need for massive 3D datasets. Concurrently, other approaches seek to build native 3D diffusion models by operating on efficient 3D representations, aiming for faster inference and better 3D structural consistency (Cheng et al., 2023). The field has also recently expanded from the generation of single, isolated objects to the synthesis of complex, multi-object 3D scenes, which requires solving additional problems related to spatial layout, occlusion, and scene coherence.

This survey aims to provide a structured and comprehensive review of the recent advancements in diffusion models for 3D shape and scene generation. We focus on three core pillars: methods, representations, and evaluation. By dissecting the literature along these dimensions, we seek to clarify the trade-offs inherent in current approaches and provide a cohesive framework for understanding this rapidly evolving domain. The survey is organized as follows: Section 3 discusses the primary methodological paradigms, contrasting 2D-prior-based optimization with native 3D diffusion. Section 4 delves into the various 3D representations utilized in the literature and their impact on generation quality. Section 5 examines the frontier of scene-level generation. Section 6 reviews the current state of evaluation metrics and benchmarks. Finally, Sections 7 and 8 offer concluding remarks and outline promising future research directions.

# 3 Methodological Paradigms for 3D Generation
Current approaches to 3D generation using diffusion models can be broadly categorized into two main paradigms: 2D-prior-based methods and native 3D diffusion methods. 2D-prior-based methods exploit the vast knowledge encapsulated in pre-trained 2D image diffusion models to synthesize 3D assets without requiring large-scale 3D training datasets. The seminal work in this category introduced Score Distillation Sampling (SDS), a technique that optimizes a 3D representation, such as a Neural Radiance Field (NeRF), by distilling gradients from a frozen 2D diffusion model (Poole et al., 2022). SDS operates by rendering the 3D model from random viewpoints, adding noise to the 2D renderings, and using the diffusion model's denoising step to compute a gradient that updates the 3D parameters. While highly effective at generating diverse and text-aligned 3D objects, 2D-prior methods often suffer from oversaturation, the "multi-face" Janus problem, and slow optimization times, as each 3D asset requires a separate, computationally intensive optimization procedure. To mitigate some of these issues, subsequent approaches have explored operating within the latent space of pre-trained 2D models rather than the image space, significantly reducing memory consumption and improving generation speed (Metzer et al., 2022).

In contrast, native 3D diffusion methods train diffusion models directly on 3D data. These approaches typically operate on large datasets of existing 3D shapes, such as ShapeNet or Objaverse. By learning the 3D distribution directly, native 3D models can generate assets in a single feed-forward pass, drastically reducing inference time compared to SDS-based optimization. However, the performance of native 3D diffusion is fundamentally constrained by the scale and quality of available 3D training data, which lags significantly behind 2D counterparts. Additionally, applying diffusion processes directly to 3D representations requires carefully designed architectures to handle the memory and computational demands of 3D convolutions or attention mechanisms. Despite these challenges, native 3D diffusion models offer superior 3D consistency and are increasingly competitive as 3D datasets expand.

# 4 3D Representations for Diffusion
The choice of 3D representation is a critical design decision in 3D diffusion models, directly impacting the model's resolution, scalability, and fidelity. Early native 3D diffusion models primarily utilized point clouds due to their simplicity and compatibility with set-based neural architectures. By treating a 3D shape as an unordered set of coordinates, these models leverage standard diffusion frameworks to generate points progressively (Nichol et al., 2022). However, point clouds lack topological information and typically require additional post-processing steps, such as surface reconstruction, to produce watertight meshes, which can introduce artifacts.

To overcome the limitations of explicit representations, recent works have turned to implicit representations, particularly Signed Distance Functions (SDFs) and neural radiance fields. SDFs encode shape as a continuous field, allowing for high-resolution surface extraction at arbitrary levels of detail. Diffusion models operating on SDFs typically learn to generate the latent codes or the continuous spatial fields of these functions, enabling the synthesis of smooth, watertight surfaces that are difficult to achieve with point clouds (Chou et al., 2022). Alternatively, neural radiance fields and their variants represent 3D scenes as continuous volumetric functions mapping spatial coordinates to density and color. While implicit representations offer significant advantages in expressiveness and memory efficiency for high resolutions, they introduce challenges in training stability and require computationally expensive volume rendering during the diffusion process. The ongoing exploration of hybrid representations seeks to combine the topological guarantees of meshes with the high-fidelity texturing capabilities of implicit fields.
