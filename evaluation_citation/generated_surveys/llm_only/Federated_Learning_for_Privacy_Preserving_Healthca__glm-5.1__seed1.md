# 1 Abstract
Federated Learning (FL) has emerged as a transformative paradigm for privacy-preserving machine learning, particularly in the healthcare sector where data sensitivity and strict regulatory constraints severely limit the centralization of patient data. This survey provides a comprehensive review of FL in healthcare, focusing on the critical dimensions of system architectures, communication efficiency, and clinical deployment. We begin by examining the architectural frameworks tailored for medical data, contrasting cross-silo setups across hospital networks with cross-device architectures for wearable health monitoring. Next, we address the communication bottleneck, a fundamental challenge exacerbated by the high dimensionality of medical imaging and electronic health records, reviewing state-of-the-art compression and efficient aggregation strategies. We also explore the privacy and security landscape, analyzing vulnerabilities inherent in distributed systems and the integration of mechanisms like differential privacy to rigorously protect patient confidentiality. Furthermore, we investigate the transition from theoretical frameworks to real-world clinical deployment, highlighting critical challenges such as statistical heterogeneity across medical institutions and the practical constraints of regulatory compliance. By synthesizing recent advancements and identifying persistent bottlenecks, this survey aims to provide researchers and practitioners with a holistic understanding of the current state of FL in healthcare, paving the way for more robust, efficient, and clinically viable privacy-preserving systems in modern medicine.

# 2 Introduction
The rapid proliferation of digital health records, medical imaging, and wearable devices has generated unprecedented volumes of clinical data, unlocking immense potential for artificial intelligence (AI) to revolutionize healthcare. Machine learning models promise enhanced diagnostic accuracy, personalized treatment planning, and predictive analytics for patient outcomes. However, the development of robust, generalizable AI models necessitates large and diverse datasets, which are typically fragmented across numerous hospitals, clinics, and research institutions. This fragmentation creates severe data silos, hindering the collaborative potential of medical data. Furthermore, healthcare data is among the most sensitive categories of personal information, subject to stringent privacy regulations such as the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) in the United States and the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) in Europe. These regulations strictly govern the sharing and centralization of patient data, making traditional centralized machine learning approaches—where all data is pooled in a single server—largely unviable or legally prohibitive.

Federated Learning (FL) has emerged as a groundbreaking paradigm to resolve this tension between the need for collaborative model training and the imperative of data privacy. Originally introduced by Google, FL enables multiple institutions to collaboratively train a shared global model without exchanging their underlying raw data. Instead, each institution trains the model locally on its own data and only shares model updates, such as gradients or weight parameters, with a central server. The server then aggregates these updates to improve the global model, iteratively distributing the new version back to the local nodes. This decentralized approach ensures that sensitive patient data never leaves the local institution, inherently preserving privacy and complying with regulatory frameworks.

Despite its promise, applying FL to healthcare introduces unique challenges that differ significantly from its applications in other domains like mobile keyboard prediction or ad targeting. Medical data is characterized by extreme statistical heterogeneity; different hospitals may serve vastly different demographics, leading to non-independently and identically distributed (non-IID) data that can destabilize model convergence (Li et al., 2021). Additionally, the sheer size of modern medical data, particularly high-resolution 3D imaging like MRI and CT scans, creates substantial communication bottlenecks. Security is another critical concern, as FL is not immune to adversarial attacks such as model inversion or membership inference, which can compromise patient privacy even from model updates.

This survey provides a comprehensive exploration of Federated Learning specifically tailored for privacy-preserving healthcare. We focus on four pivotal aspects: system architectures designed for medical settings, communication efficiency techniques suited for voluminous medical data, privacy and security mechanisms ensuring clinical confidentiality, and the practical challenges of deploying FL in real-world clinical environments. The remainder of this survey is organized as follows. Section 3 delves into the architectural frameworks underpinning FL in healthcare, distinguishing between cross-silo and cross-device topologies. Section 4 tackles the critical issue of communication efficiency, exploring how techniques like gradient compression and efficient aggregation protocols are essential for handling high-dimensional medical data. Section 5 examines the privacy and security landscape, discussing the integration of differential privacy and secure aggregation to mitigate sophisticated adversarial attacks. Section 6 shifts the focus to clinical deployment, analyzing the practical hurdles of statistical heterogeneity, system reliability, and regulatory compliance. Finally, Sections 7 and 8 synthesize our findings, offering a conclusive summary and outlining promising future research directions that will shape the next generation of federated healthcare systems.

# 3 Architectures for Federated Healthcare
The architectural design of a Federated Learning system fundamentally dictates its applicability and effectiveness in healthcare settings. Unlike generic FL applications, healthcare demands architectures that can accommodate diverse organizational structures, varying data modalities, and strict regulatory boundaries. The most prominent architectural distinction in medical FL is between cross-silo and cross-device setups. Cross-silo FL involves a limited number of relatively reliable and powerful nodes, typically representing different hospitals, medical centers, or research institutions. This architecture is highly suitable for collaborative medical imaging or electronic health record analysis, where institutions possess large, stationary datasets but are restricted from sharing them due to privacy laws. In contrast, cross-device FL involves a massive number of mobile or edge devices, such as smartwatches or remote patient monitoring sensors. This architecture is increasingly relevant for continuous health tracking and early disease detection but faces challenges related to device availability, limited computational power, and network instability.

Beyond the silo-device dichotomy, the topology of the network—whether centralized, decentralized, or hierarchical—plays a crucial role. In a centralized architecture, a central server orchestrates the training and aggregation, which can create a single point of failure and communication bottleneck. Decentralized topologies, where institutions communicate directly with each other in a peer-to-peer manner, can mitigate this bottleneck and enhance fault tolerance, though they require more complex coordination. Recent advancements have also explored hierarchical architectures, where edge devices communicate with local gateways, which then communicate with a global cloud server, effectively bridging the cross-device and cross-silo paradigms. For instance, specialized architectures have been proposed to handle the unique constraints of medical imaging, ensuring that model updates from 3D segmentation tasks are efficiently synchronized without compromising patient privacy (Li et al., 2019). Furthermore, architectural choices must account for the heterogeneity of local data distributions, prompting the development of frameworks that adaptively weight local updates based on the structural properties of the participating nodes (Yurochkin et al., 2019).

# 4 Communication Efficiency in Medical Federated Learning
Communication efficiency is a paramount concern in Federated Learning, particularly in healthcare where data dimensionality is exceptionally high. Medical imaging data, such as 3D MRI and CT scans, and extensive longitudinal electronic health records, result in massive model sizes with millions or even billions of parameters. Transmitting these large model updates over constrained hospital networks or unstable wireless connections for numerous communication rounds can render FL practically infeasible. Consequently, reducing the communication overhead is critical for the scalability of clinical FL systems.

Several strategies have been developed to alleviate this bottleneck. The foundational Federated Averaging (FedAvg) algorithm significantly reduces communication rounds by allowing local clients to perform multiple epochs of stochastic gradient descent before transmitting their updates, rather than communicating after every single iteration (McMahan et al., 2017). While FedAvg is effective, the high dimensionality of medical models necessitates further compression. Techniques such as gradient quantization, which reduces the precision of the transmitted weights, and sparsification, which only transmits the most significant updates, have been widely adopted. However, applying these techniques to medical data requires caution, as aggressive compression can degrade model accuracy, which is intolerable in safety-critical clinical applications. Moreover, the statistical heterogeneity inherent in medical data—where different hospitals may have vastly different patient demographics—can cause local models to diverge, requiring more communication rounds to converge. To address this, novel optimization algorithms have been introduced that penalize local drift and ensure that local updates remain close to the global model, thereby accelerating convergence and reducing the total communication cost (Wang et al., 2020).

# 5 Privacy and Security Challenges in Clinical Data
While Federated Learning is often touted as a privacy-preserving technology, it is not inherently immune to privacy breaches. The fundamental premise of FL—that raw data remains local—protects against direct data exposure, but the shared model updates can inadvertently leak sensitive patient information. Adversaries can employ sophisticated attacks, such as membership inference, to determine whether a specific patient's data was used in training, or gradient inversion attacks, which can reconstruct training images from shared gradients. In the healthcare domain, where data is highly sensitive and strictly regulated, even partial information leakage can have severe ethical and legal consequences.

To fortify FL against these vulnerabilities, robust privacy-enhancing technologies must be integrated. Differential Privacy (DP) has emerged as the gold standard for providing rigorous mathematical guarantees of privacy. By injecting calibrated noise into the model updates before transmission, DP ensures that the output of the FL system is statistically indistinguishable whether or not any single patient's data is included in the training set.
