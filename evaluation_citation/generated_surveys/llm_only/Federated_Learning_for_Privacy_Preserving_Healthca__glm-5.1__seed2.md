# 1 Abstract
Federated Learning (FL) has emerged as a paradigm-shifting technology for privacy-preserving healthcare, enabling collaborative model training across decentralized medical data without transferring sensitive patient records. As healthcare data becomes increasingly siloed due to stringent privacy regulations and institutional policies, traditional centralized machine learning approaches face significant legal and technical barriers. This survey provides a comprehensive overview of FL in the healthcare domain, focusing on three critical pillars: system architectures, communication efficiency, and clinical deployment. We first examine the architectural frameworks tailored for medical institutions, highlighting the transition from centralized topologies to peer-to-peer and hierarchical models suited for multi-institutional collaborations. Next, we address the critical bottleneck of communication overhead, analyzing advanced optimization algorithms and model compression techniques that are essential for bandwidth-constrained clinical networks. We then explore the landscape of privacy-preserving mechanisms, discussing the integration of differential privacy and secure aggregation, while critically addressing vulnerabilities such as gradient inversion attacks. Finally, we review real-world clinical deployments, identifying the operational challenges of translating FL from theory to practice in domains like medical imaging, electronic health records, and genomics. By synthesizing current advancements and outlining persistent challenges, this survey serves as a roadmap for researchers and practitioners aiming to develop robust, efficient, and clinically viable federated learning systems for the future of digital medicine.

# 2 Introduction
The rapid proliferation of digital health records, medical imaging, and genomic sequencing has generated unprecedented volumes of clinical data. Machine learning (ML) holds immense promise for leveraging this data to improve diagnostic accuracy, personalize treatment plans, and predict patient outcomes. However, healthcare data is notoriously siloed, distributed across hospitals, clinics, and research institutions that are often restricted from sharing data due to stringent privacy regulations such as the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) in the United States and the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) in Europe. Traditional centralized ML approaches require aggregating raw data into a single repository, a practice that is frequently infeasible in healthcare due to legal, ethical, and security concerns. Consequently, the inability to pool data severely limits the scale, diversity, and generalizability of medical ML models, often resulting in algorithms that perform well on specific cohorts but fail to generalize across broader populations.

Federated Learning (FL) has emerged as a transformative paradigm to overcome these barriers. Originally introduced by Google for mobile keyboard prediction, FL enables multiple institutions to collaboratively train a shared global model by exchanging only model updates—such as weights or gradients—rather than raw patient data. This decentralized approach inherently preserves data privacy, allowing hospitals to maintain strict control over their sensitive datasets while still benefiting from the statistical power of a larger, diverse cohort. The application of FL in healthcare is particularly compelling because it directly addresses the dual challenge of data scarcity and privacy preservation, facilitating multi-institutional collaborations that were previously unattainable.

Despite its promise, deploying FL in clinical environments introduces a unique set of challenges that differ significantly from those in consumer-facing applications. Healthcare data is highly heterogeneous, often exhibiting non-IID (independent and identically distributed) characteristics across different sites due to variations in patient demographics, imaging protocols, and diagnostic practices. This statistical heterogeneity can severely degrade model convergence and performance. Furthermore, the sheer size of modern medical models, particularly deep neural networks for imaging, combined with the limited network bandwidth in hospital IT infrastructures, creates substantial communication bottlenecks. Privacy is also not guaranteed merely by data locality; sophisticated attacks can reconstruct sensitive training data from shared gradients, necessitating robust cryptographic and perturbation-based defenses.

This survey provides a comprehensive and structured examination of FL in the healthcare domain, focusing on the critical intersections of architectures, communication efficiency, and clinical deployment. We aim to bridge the gap between theoretical FL research and practical medical implementation. The scope of this survey encompasses the architectural paradigms specifically adapted for medical settings, the algorithmic innovations designed to mitigate communication overhead, the privacy vulnerabilities unique to clinical data, and the real-world systems that have successfully transitioned FL from theory to bedside. By addressing these interconnected dimensions, we provide a holistic view of the current state of FL in medicine.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section 3 explores the various FL architectures utilized in healthcare, from centralized to decentralized topologies. Section 4 delves into communication efficiency techniques critical for clinical networks. Section 5 examines privacy-preserving mechanisms and threat models, while Section 6 reviews clinical deployment strategies and real-world applications. Finally, Sections 7 and 8 offer concluding remarks and outline promising future research directions in this rapidly evolving field.

# 3 Architectures for Federated Learning in Healthcare
The architectural design of a federated learning system fundamentally dictates its scalability, robustness, and suitability for clinical environments. In healthcare, the most prevalent architecture is the centralized FL topology, where a single central server orchestrates the training process by aggregating model updates from participating hospitals. This architecture is the foundation of the seminal Federated Averaging (FedAvg) algorithm, which remains the baseline for multi-institutional medical collaborations (McMahan et al., 2017). However, centralized architectures introduce a single point of failure and may impose uneven communication burdens on the central server, which can be problematic when coordinating across large hospital networks.

To mitigate the limitations of centralized setups, decentralized and peer-to-peer FL architectures have been proposed. In a decentralized topology, institutions communicate directly with one another, typically following a ring or mesh topology, eliminating the need for a central coordinator. This approach can enhance fault tolerance and reduce communication bottlenecks at a single node, though it requires sophisticated consensus mechanisms. Another critical architectural variant for healthcare is hierarchical FL, which mirrors the organizational structure of healthcare systems. In this model, edge devices or local clinics aggregate updates within a regional hub (e.g., a regional hospital) before sending them to a global server. This hierarchical approach reduces communication latency and aligns well with the regulatory boundaries often found in national health systems. Furthermore, the development of open-source platforms tailored for medical FL, such as Fed-BioMed, has streamlined the deployment of these architectures, providing researchers with the necessary tools to implement secure, reproducible, and transparent medical FL applications (Terrail et al., 2022).

# 4 Communication Efficiency in Federated Healthcare Systems
Communication efficiency is a paramount concern in federated healthcare systems, as medical institutions often operate with constrained network bandwidth and strict firewall policies. The iterative exchange of large model updates between hospitals and the central server can lead to significant bottlenecks, stalling the training process. This challenge is exacerbated in modern medical imaging tasks that rely on voluminous deep learning architectures, such as 3D convolutional neural networks for tumor segmentation.

To address these bottlenecks, researchers have developed a variety of communication-reduction strategies. One prominent approach is the use of advanced federated optimization algorithms designed to minimize the number of communication rounds required for convergence. For instance, algorithms like FedProx modify the local objective function by adding a proximal term, which restricts local updates from diverging too far from the global model, thereby accelerating convergence under the statistical heterogeneity typical of medical data (Li et al., 2020). Additionally, model compression techniques such as quantization, sparsification, and pruning are widely employed. Quantization reduces the precision of the gradient values (e.g., from 32-bit floating-point to 8-bit integers), while sparsification transmits only the most significant updates based on a predefined threshold. By combining algorithmic innovations that reduce the frequency of communication with compression techniques that reduce the size of the transmitted payloads, federated systems can achieve substantial reductions in communication overhead, making FL viable for bandwidth-constrained clinical networks.

# 5 Privacy-Preserving Mechanisms and Threat Models
While federated learning inherently keeps raw data on local devices, it is not immune to privacy breaches. The exchange of model updates can inadvertently leak sensitive patient information through sophisticated inference attacks. Threat models in healthcare FL primarily include gradient inversion attacks, where an adversary reconstructs training data from shared gradients, and membership inference attacks, which determine whether a specific patient's data was used in training.

The vulnerability of FL to gradient inversion is particularly concerning in healthcare, where medical images contain highly sensitive diagnostic details. Research has demonstrated that deep leakage from gradients can reconstruct pixel-perfect copies of training images, posing a severe risk to patient confidentiality (Zhu et al., 2019). To counter these threats, several privacy-preserving mechanisms have been integrated into FL pipelines. Differential Privacy (DP) is a widely adopted technique that obscures the contribution of individual data points by injecting calibrated noise into the local gradients or model updates before transmission. While DP provides rigorous mathematical privacy guarantees, the addition of noise often leads to a degradation in model utility, a trade-off that must be carefully managed in clinical applications where diagnostic accuracy is critical. Alternatively, Secure Multi-Party Computation (SMPC) and Homomorphic Encryption (HE) enable secure aggregation, ensuring that the central server can only observe the aggregated global update without accessing individual hospital contributions. However, these cryptographic methods introduce significant computational and communication overhead, limiting their scalability in large-scale medical networks.

# 6 Clinical Deployment and Real-World Applications
The translation of federated learning from theoretical frameworks to clinical deployment involves navigating substantial operational, regulatory, and logistical hurdles. Real-world clinical applications must account for the fragmented nature of hospital IT infrastructures, the need for continuous model monitoring, and strict compliance with healthcare regulations. Despite these challenges, FL has been successfully deployed in several medical domains, demonstrating its potential to unlock the value of siloed clinical data.

One of the most prominent applications of FL in healthcare is in medical imaging, particularly for brain tumor segmentation. The first major multi-institutional FL study demonstrated that a globally trained model could achieve performance comparable to a model trained on centrally pooled data, without sharing patient scans (Sheller et al., 2018). Beyond oncology, FL is increasingly utilized in electronic health records (EHR) for predictive modeling, such as predicting patient readmission rates or disease progression, where data is highly tabular and non-IID across institutions. The future of digital health with federated learning also extends to genomics, where FL enables the analysis of rare genetic variants across biobanks without transferring raw genomic data (Rieke et al., 2020). For successful clinical deployment, platforms must provide robust auditing, fault tolerance, and interoperability with existing clinical workflows, ensuring that FL models are not only accurate but also trustworthy and clinically actionable.

# 7 Conclusion
Federated learning represents a fundamental shift in how machine learning models are developed for healthcare, offering a viable pathway to harness the power of decentralized clinical data while preserving patient privacy. This survey has explored the critical dimensions of FL in the medical domain, specifically focusing on system architectures, communication efficiency, privacy mechanisms, and clinical deployment. We have highlighted that the transition from centralized to hierarchical and decentralized architectures provides the flexibility needed to accommodate the complex organizational structures of healthcare systems. Furthermore, we examined the severe communication bottlenecks inherent in training large medical models across bandwidth-constrained hospital networks, underscoring the necessity of advanced optimization algorithms and model compression techniques to ensure practical viability.

Privacy, the core motivation for FL, remains a dynamic battleground. While data localization prevents the direct exposure of patient records, the susceptibility of shared gradients to inversion attacks necessitates the integration of robust defenses such as differential privacy and secure aggregation. However, these defenses are not without cost; the tension between privacy guarantees and model utility is a persistent challenge that must be carefully balanced, particularly in high-stakes clinical decision-making. Moreover, the deployment of FL in real-world clinical settings—from medical imaging to electronic health records—demonstrates both the feasibility and the transformative potential of the technology. Yet, operational hurdles, including IT infrastructure limitations, regulatory compliance, and the need for continuous model governance, remain significant obstacles to widespread adoption.

Ultimately, the success of federated learning in healthcare hinges on a multidisciplinary approach. Computer scientists, clinicians, and regulatory bodies must collaborate to design systems that are not only algorithmically efficient and secure but also clinically relevant and legally compliant. As the field matures, the focus must shift from proving the concept of FL in isolated studies to building scalable, interoperable, and resilient federated networks that can seamlessly integrate into the daily workflows of healthcare institutions. The ongoing evolution of FL promises to break down data silos, enabling the development of robust, generalizable medical AI that benefits diverse patient populations without compromising the confidentiality of individual health information.

# 8 Future Directions
As federated learning continues to mature within the healthcare sector, several promising research directions emerge that could further accelerate its adoption and impact. First, the development of personalized federated learning algorithms is critical. Given the high degree of statistical heterogeneity in medical data, a single global model may not optimally serve all participating institutions. Future research should focus on methods that allow local fine-tuning or the training of multi-task architectures, enabling hospitals to derive models tailored to their specific patient demographics while still benefiting from global knowledge sharing.

Second, the integration of FL with emerging privacy-enhancing technologies offers a fertile ground for innovation. Combining FL with trusted execution environments (TEEs) or leveraging advances in homomorphic encryption to create hybrid privacy frameworks could provide stronger guarantees against sophisticated inference attacks without imposing prohibitive computational overhead. Additionally, the exploration of synthetic data generation within FL networks—where generative models are trained federatedly to produce realistic, privacy-safe synthetic datasets—could provide a powerful tool for data augmentation and model validation.

Third, the standardization of federated learning frameworks for clinical deployment requires urgent attention. The development of open-source, clinically compliant platforms with standardized APIs will be essential for lowering the barrier to entry for healthcare institutions. These platforms must incorporate robust auditing mechanisms, data provenance tracking, and interoperability standards such as FHIR (Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources) to seamlessly interface with existing EHR systems.

Finally, the application of FL to longitudinal and multi-modal clinical data presents a significant opportunity. Most current FL applications in healthcare focus on cross-sectional data, such as single medical images. Extending FL to handle longitudinal patient trajectories, combining imaging, genomic, and EHR data over time, will unlock deeper insights into disease progression and treatment efficacy. Addressing these future directions will require sustained collaboration between the AI and medical communities, ensuring that the next generation of FL systems is not only technically advanced but also deeply integrated into the fabric of clinical care, ultimately improving patient outcomes on a global scale.
