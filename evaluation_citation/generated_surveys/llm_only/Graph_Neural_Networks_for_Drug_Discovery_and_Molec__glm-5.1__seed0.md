# 1 Abstract
Graph neural networks (GNNs) have emerged as a powerful paradigm for learning from molecular structures, driving significant advancements in drug discovery and molecular property prediction. Traditional computational methods for molecular representation relied heavily on hand-crafted features and molecular fingerprints, which are often limited in capturing complex topological and geometric information. GNNs overcome these limitations by directly operating on molecular graphs, where atoms serve as nodes and chemical bonds serve as edges, enabling the automatic extraction of highly expressive representations. This survey provides a comprehensive overview of the application of GNNs in drug discovery and molecular property prediction. We begin by discussing the fundamental paradigms of molecular graph construction, including both 2D topological and 3D geometric representations. We then systematically review prominent GNN architectures tailored for molecular modeling, such as message passing neural networks, directional message passing, and equivariant graph networks. Subsequently, we explore the specific tasks of molecular property prediction and de novo drug design, highlighting how GNN-based generative and discriminative models have accelerated the discovery pipeline. Finally, we identify current challenges and outline promising future directions, such as scaling to larger biomolecules, improving out-of-distribution generalization, and enhancing model interpretability. By synthesizing recent developments, this survey aims to provide researchers with a structured understanding of the landscape of GNNs in computational chemistry and drug discovery.

# 2 Introduction
The process of drug discovery is notoriously time-consuming, expensive, and fraught with high attrition rates. Traditionally, identifying a viable drug candidate can take decades and cost billions of dollars, largely due to the immense chemical space—estimated to be on the order of 10 to the power of 60—and the complex biological interactions that must be navigated. Computer-aided drug design (CADD) has long been employed to accelerate this pipeline, but classical approaches often rely on extensive domain expertise, simplified molecular representations, and force-field based simulations that can be computationally prohibitive. The advent of deep learning has fundamentally transformed computational chemistry, shifting the paradigm from manual feature engineering to end-to-end representation learning. Among the most impactful developments in this space is the rise of Graph Neural Networks (GNNs).

Molecules are inherently represented as graphs, where atoms correspond to nodes and chemical bonds correspond to edges. Unlike traditional string-based representations like SMILES, which suffer from non-uniqueness and lack explicit structural priors, or fixed-length molecular fingerprints, which discard topological information and fail to capture molecular similarity accurately, graph representations preserve the relational inductive bias of molecules. GNNs leverage this structure through iterative message passing or neighborhood aggregation schemes, allowing nodes to update their hidden states based on their local structural neighborhoods. This mechanism naturally mimics the way atomic environments dictate molecular properties, reactivity, and binding affinities.

The motivation for this survey stems from the explosive growth of GNN methodologies applied to molecular modeling over the past few years. While several reviews have touched upon general deep learning for drug discovery or broad GNN applications in other domains, there is a pressing need for a focused, up-to-date synthesis of how GNNs are specifically architected and deployed for molecular property prediction and drug generation. The rapid iteration of models—from basic convolutional graph networks to sophisticated 3D geometric and equivariant models—makes it challenging for researchers to keep pace with the state of the art. Furthermore, the integration of these predictive models with generative frameworks for de novo design requires a unified perspective that bridges discriminative and generative graph modeling.

This survey aims to bridge that gap by providing a comprehensive and structured review of the field. Our scope encompasses the core algorithmic advancements in GNN architectures tailored for molecules, as well as their application in both discriminative tasks (like property prediction) and generative tasks (like de novo molecule design). We purposefully exclude purely sequence-based models or traditional machine learning methods to maintain a sharp focus on graph-based learning, which we believe is the most natural and promising paradigm for molecular modeling.

The structure of this survey is as follows: Section 3 delves into the foundational aspects of molecular representation and graph construction, distinguishing between 2D and 3D modalities. Section 4 provides a detailed taxonomy of GNN architectures designed for molecular data, highlighting the evolution from standard message passing to directional and geometric models. Section 5 examines the application of these GNNs to molecular property prediction, covering both quantum mechanical and physicochemical endpoints. Section 6 shifts the focus to generative models, exploring how GNNs are utilized for de novo drug design. Finally, Sections 7 and 8 offer concluding remarks and outline critical future directions for the field.

# 3 Molecular Representation and Graph Construction
Molecules can be represented computationally in various ways, and the choice of representation profoundly impacts the performance of downstream machine learning models. Graph Neural Networks operate directly on graph-structured data, necessitating the conversion of molecular structures into appropriate graph formats. The most common approach is the 2D topological graph, where nodes represent atoms and edges represent chemical bonds. Node features typically include atomic number, chirality, formal charge, and hybridization, while edge features capture bond type, stereochemistry, and conjugation. This representation is highly effective for capturing the connectivity and topological invariants of molecules, which are crucial for predicting properties like toxicity or solubility.

However, 2D graphs inherently lack spatial information, which is vital for determining quantum mechanical properties and 3D conformer-dependent biological activity. To address this, 3D geometric graphs incorporate spatial coordinates, representing molecules as point clouds or radius graphs where edges are formed based on interatomic distances or spatial proximity. In these representations, geometric features such as distances, angles, and dihedrals become critical inputs. The construction of these graphs often involves defining cutoff distances to include interactions beyond covalent bonds, capturing the non-bonded interactions that dictate molecular folding and binding. The transition from simple 2D topological graphs to rich 3D geometric graphs has been a major catalyst for the development of specialized GNN architectures capable of respecting physical symmetries, such as rotation and translation invariance (Schütt et al., 2017). Furthermore, the choice of graph granularity—ranging from atom-level graphs to coarse-grained fragment-level graphs—adds another layer of design flexibility, allowing researchers to balance computational efficiency with representational fidelity (Fey & Lenssen, 2019).

# 4 Graph Neural Network Architectures for Molecules
The architecture of GNNs for molecular modeling has evolved significantly, driven by the unique physical and chemical properties of molecules. The foundational paradigm for molecular GNNs is the Message Passing Neural Network (MPNN) framework, which generalizes various convolutional and recurrent architectures for graphs. In an MPNN, each node updates its hidden state by aggregating messages from its neighbors, a process that is repeated for several iterations to propagate information across the molecular graph. While standard MPNNs are effective for 2D topological graphs, they struggle to capture the directional and geometric nuances of 3D molecular conformations.

To overcome the limitations of standard MPNNs, researchers have developed directional message passing techniques. These architectures explicitly model the angles and spatial directions between atoms, allowing messages to be modulated by the geometric orientation of the local environment. This is particularly important for modeling quantum mechanical properties, which are highly sensitive to the spatial arrangement of atoms. For instance, DimeNet uses spherical Bessel functions and spherical harmonics to embed interatomic distances and angles, enabling the network to learn expressive directional representations (Klicpera et al., 2020). Subsequent architectures have further refined this approach by incorporating more efficient angular encodings and expanding the receptive field to include higher-order geometric features like dihedrals. Another line of architectural innovation focuses on ensuring that the GNNs respect the fundamental symmetries of physical systems, leading to the development of equivariant GNNs that guarantee rotation and translation equivariance by construction, thereby improving sample efficiency and generalization (Gasteiger et al., 2021).

# 5 Molecular Property Prediction
Molecular property prediction is a cornerstone of computational drug discovery, as it allows researchers to screen vast chemical libraries and prioritize candidates with desirable pharmacological profiles. GNNs have demonstrated remarkable success in this domain, outperforming traditional machine learning models that rely on hand-crafted molecular fingerprints. Property prediction tasks can be broadly categorized into quantum mechanical predictions, such as calculating ground-state energies and forces, and physicochemical/biological predictions, such as estimating solubility, logP, and binding affinities.

For quantum mechanical property prediction, the ability of GNNs to learn directly from 3D geometries has been transformative. Datasets like QM9 have become standard benchmarks for evaluating the accuracy of geometric GNNs in predicting properties like HOMO-LUMO gaps and internal energies. By incorporating physical inductive biases, such as energy conservation and equivariance, these models achieve state-of-the-art accuracy while requiring significantly less training data than unstructured neural networks. In the realm of physicochemical and biological property prediction, 2D GNNs remain highly competitive. The Directed Message Passing Neural Network (D-MPNN) is a prominent example that has set strong baselines across numerous benchmarks. By passing messages along directed edges rather than undirected nodes, D-MPNNs avoid the creation of artificial loops and learn more distinct representations of atomic environments, leading to superior performance in tasks like toxicity prediction and drug efficacy classification (Yang et al., 2019). The continuous improvement in property prediction accuracy directly accelerates the drug discovery pipeline by enabling more reliable virtual screening and reducing the reliance on costly and time-consuming wet-lab experiments (Gilmer et al., 2017).

# 6 Generative Models for Drug Discovery
Beyond property prediction, GNNs play a crucial role in de novo drug design through generative modeling. The goal of molecular generation is to create novel molecular structures that satisfy multiple chemical and pharmacological constraints, such as high binding affinity, synthetic accessibility, and low toxicity. Generative models operating directly on molecular graphs offer a natural and flexible framework for this task, as they do not suffer from the validity issues that often plague SMILES-based generative models.

Several paradigms have emerged for graph-based molecular generation. Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) learn a continuous latent space of molecular graphs, enabling smooth interpolation and optimization. Early variational autoencoder frameworks like GraphVAE introduced probabilistic generation of small graphs using graph matching techniques (Simonovsky & Komodakis, 2018). Constrained Graph VAEs enforce chemical validity during the decoding process, ensuring that generated outputs are valid molecules (Liu et al., 2018). Autoregressive models generate molecular graphs sequentially, adding one atom or bond at a time. GraphAF combines autoregressive generation with normalizing flows, allowing for exact density estimation and efficient sampling, while maintaining high validity and property optimization capabilities (Shi et al., 2020). Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) have also been adapted for molecular graphs, with models like MolGAN utilizing a permutation-invariant discriminator to handle the non-uniqueness of graph representations (De Cao & Kipf, 2018). These generative frameworks are increasingly integrated with reinforcement learning or Bayesian optimization to steer the generation process toward desired property profiles, effectively navigating the vast and discrete chemical space to identify promising drug candidates.

# 7 Conclusion
The integration of Graph Neural Networks into drug discovery and molecular property prediction represents a paradigm shift in computational chemistry. By moving beyond traditional hand-crafted features and string-based representations, GNNs exploit the natural graph structure of molecules to learn highly expressive, context-aware representations. This survey has traced the evolution of these methods, from fundamental 2D topological message passing to sophisticated 3D geometric and equivariant architectures. The ability of GNNs to capture both the local chemical environment and the global topological structure of molecules has led to unprecedented accuracy in predicting a wide array of molecular properties, from quantum mechanical energies to biological toxicity. This capability is critical for virtual screening, allowing researchers to filter out unpromising compounds early in the discovery pipeline and focus resources on the most viable candidates.

Furthermore, the advent of GNN-based generative models has opened new avenues for de novo drug design. Unlike traditional high-throughput screening, which is limited to existing chemical libraries, generative models can propose entirely novel molecular structures tailored to specific target profiles. Whether through variational autoencoders, autoregressive flows, or adversarial networks, these models provide a powerful mechanism for navigating the vast and discrete landscape of chemical space. The incorporation of reinforcement learning and optimization algorithms into these generative frameworks ensures that the designed molecules are not only valid but also possess desirable pharmacological properties.

Despite these remarkable advances, the field is not without its challenges. Issues such as the limited availability of high-quality experimental data, the difficulty of modeling large biomolecular complexes, and the inherent interpretability limitations of deep neural networks remain active areas of research. Nevertheless, the progress outlined in this survey demonstrates that GNNs have firmly established themselves as indispensable tools in the modern computational chemistry toolkit. As algorithms become more refined and datasets grow larger and more diverse, the impact of GNNs on accelerating the discovery of life-saving drugs will only continue to expand.

# 8 Future Directions
While GNNs have achieved remarkable success in molecular modeling, several promising research directions remain to be explored to fully realize their potential in drug discovery. One critical area is the scaling of GNNs to larger, more complex systems. Current models excel at small molecule property prediction, but drug discovery increasingly involves modeling protein-ligand complexes, protein-protein interactions, and entire biological pathways. Developing hierarchical or coarse-grained GNN architectures that can efficiently process large biomolecular graphs without losing atomic-level detail is a pressing challenge.

Another vital direction is the integration of multi-modal data. Drug discovery relies on diverse data types, including genomic sequences, chemical structures, clinical text, and imaging data. Multi-modal GNNs that can seamlessly fuse graph-structured molecular data with these other modalities will enable more holistic and accurate predictions of drug efficacy and safety. Additionally, the field must address the persistent issue of out-of-distribution generalization. Real-world drug discovery often involves exploring chemical spaces far from the training data distribution, a scenario where standard deep learning models typically fail. Improving the robustness and uncertainty quantification of GNNs is essential for building reliable systems that can be trusted in high-stakes pharmaceutical applications.

Interpretability and explainability also stand out as crucial frontiers. For GNNs to be adopted by medicinal chemists, they must provide actionable insights into the structure-activity relationships driving their predictions. Developing post-hoc explainability methods tailored for molecular graphs, as well as inherently interpretable GNN architectures, will bridge the gap between machine learning predictions and chemical intuition. Finally, the intersection of GNNs with automated synthesis planning and high-throughput experimentation holds immense promise. Closing the loop between computational design, synthesis prediction, and experimental validation will create autonomous drug discovery platforms, dramatically accelerating the pace of pharmaceutical innovation. As these future directions are realized, GNNs will transition from powerful predictive models to comprehensive engines of scientific discovery.
