# 1 Abstract
Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have emerged as a transformative paradigm in drug discovery and molecular property prediction, offering a powerful framework to model the intricate structural and relational information inherent in molecules. By representing molecules as graphs—where atoms serve as nodes and chemical bonds as edges—GNNs circumvent the limitations of traditional hand-crafted molecular descriptors, enabling end-to-end learning of task-specific representations. This survey provides a comprehensive overview of the evolution and application of GNNs in this domain. We begin by establishing the foundational message-passing frameworks that catalyzed the field, subsequently exploring advanced spatial and geometric architectures designed to capture 3D molecular conformations. Furthermore, we examine the integration of GNNs into downstream drug discovery pipelines, focusing on molecular property prediction, virtual screening, and de novo molecule generation. We also discuss the critical role of equivariant models in handling 3D rotational and translational symmetries, which is essential for accurate quantum mechanical property prediction and molecular docking. Finally, we highlight current limitations regarding data scarcity, out-of-distribution generalization, and scalability, outlining promising future directions such as the incorporation of prior knowledge, foundation models for chemistry, and enhanced interpretability mechanisms to accelerate the drug discovery lifecycle.

# 2 Introduction
Drug discovery is an inherently complex, resource-intensive, and time-consuming endeavor, traditionally requiring over a decade and billions of dollars to bring a single therapeutic agent to market. A critical bottleneck in this pipeline is the identification of candidate molecules with desirable pharmacological properties, such as high binding affinity, low toxicity, and favorable absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADMET) profiles. Historically, computational methods have relied on quantitative structure-activity relationship (QSAR) modeling and molecular dynamics simulations, which often depend on hand-crafted features or approximations that fail to capture the nuanced complexity of molecular interactions.

The advent of deep learning has revolutionized computational biology and chemistry, with Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) emerging as a particularly potent tool. Molecules are naturally represented as graphs, where atoms form the node set and chemical bonds constitute the edge set. Unlike traditional fingerprinting methods that compress molecular structures into fixed-length bit vectors, potentially losing structural nuance, GNNs operate directly on the graph topology. This allows them to autonomously learn continuous, dense representations that encode both local chemical environments and global molecular topology.

The motivation for this survey stems from the rapid proliferation of GNN architectures tailored for chemical applications, creating a need to synthesize and contextualize these advancements. Over the past few years, the field has progressed from simple spectral graph convolutions to sophisticated message-passing neural networks (MPNNs) and, more recently, to geometric deep learning models that incorporate 3D spatial coordinates. This progression has expanded the predictive capabilities from basic physicochemical properties to complex quantum mechanical calculations and protein-ligand docking.

This survey aims to provide a structured and comprehensive review of GNNs applied to drug discovery and molecular property prediction. We focus specifically on methodological innovations in graph-based learning and their direct impact on pharmaceutical tasks. We exclude purely sequence-based or traditional machine learning approaches to maintain a focused scope on graph-centric methodologies.

The structure of this survey is as follows: Section 3 examines foundational GNN architectures, specifically Message Passing Neural Networks, and their application to 2D molecular graphs. Section 4 delves into the paradigm shift towards geometric deep learning, discussing 3D equivariant GNNs that capture spatial symmetries. Section 5 explores the application of GNNs in generative modeling and molecular docking for de novo drug design. Section 6 addresses the critical challenges of benchmarking and generalization in molecular property prediction. Finally, Sections 7 and 8 offer concluding remarks and outline promising future research directions.

# 3 Foundational Message Passing Architectures
The conceptualization of molecules as undirected graphs laid the groundwork for the application of GNNs in chemistry. The seminal framework that unified various GNN variants for molecular property prediction is the Message Passing Neural Network (MPNN). In an MPNN, the representation of a target atom is iteratively updated by aggregating feature vectors from its immediate neighbors along the chemical bonds. This forward pass consists of a message function and an update function, typically implemented using recurrent neural networks or gated units, followed by a readout phase that aggregates node representations into a global graph representation.

A significant advancement within this paradigm was the introduction of Directed MPNNs (D-MPNNs), which address the issue of redundant message passing along undirected edges. By propagating messages from the incoming edges of a node to its outgoing edges, D-MPNNs prevent the creation of artificial message loops and significantly improve the learning of edge-level features, which are crucial for representing bond types and properties. The efficacy of this approach was rigorously demonstrated in large-scale molecular property prediction benchmarks (Yang et al., 2019). Furthermore, the unification of these message-passing schemes under a generalizable framework established the standard methodology for molecular graph learning, proving that learned representations could consistently outperform traditional expert-engineered features like Extended-Connectivity Fingerprints (ECFPs) across diverse chemical datasets (Gilmer et al., 2017).

# 4 Geometric Deep Learning and 3D Equivariant GNNs
While 2D graph representations capture the topological connectivity of molecules, they inherently lack the spatial and geometric information critical for determining molecular conformation, reactivity, and binding interactions. To overcome this, the field has increasingly shifted toward geometric deep learning, where molecules are represented as 3D point clouds or geometric graphs. In this setting, it is paramount that the model's predictions are invariant to translational and rotational transformations of the input coordinates.

This requirement has driven the development of equivariant GNNs, which guarantee that rotations or translations of the input 3D coordinates result in correspondingly transformed output representations. Early approaches utilized invariant representations, such as inter-atomic distances or angles, to achieve rotational invariance. For instance, directional message passing was introduced to embed geometric information by explicitly modeling the angles between bonds, leading to substantial improvements in predicting quantum mechanical properties (Klicpera et al., 2020). More recently, fully equivariant models operating on spherical harmonics and E(3)-equivariant message passing have emerged. These models maintain the full geometric tensor field, allowing them to capture not just scalar distances but directional vectors in 3D space without losing information through premature invariant aggregation. Such spherical message passing schemes have set new state-of-the-art results on 3D molecular benchmarks by seamlessly integrating distance, angle, and torsion information (Liu et al., 2021).

# 5 Generative Models and Molecular Docking
Beyond property prediction, GNNs play an increasingly vital role in generative tasks and structural modeling within drug discovery. De novo molecule generation aims to design novel chemical entities with desired properties, while molecular docking seeks to predict the 3D binding pose of a ligand within a target protein's binding site. Both tasks require navigating complex, high-dimensional chemical and spatial landscapes.

In molecular docking, traditional physics-based methods are computationally expensive and often rely on heuristic search algorithms. Recent advances have reframed docking as a generative modeling problem over the space of ligand poses, leveraging GNNs to model the protein-ligand interaction complex. By representing the protein binding pocket as a graph and iteratively updating the ligand's atom positions using equivariant neural networks, these models can rapidly predict accurate binding poses, significantly outperforming traditional search algorithms in terms of both speed and accuracy (Corso et al., 2022).

Similarly, the generation of 3D molecules has been revolutionized by diffusion-based models combined with equivariant GNNs. These models learn to reverse a noise-adding diffusion process to generate molecules directly in 3D space. By ensuring that the denoising process respects the rotational and translational symmetries of the molecular space, equivariant diffusion models can generate realistic and synthetically accessible molecular conformations (Hoogeboom et al., 2022). This paradigm has also been extended to fragment-based drug design, where conditional diffusion models link molecular fragments with appropriate chemical scaffolds while maintaining 3D equivariance (Igashov et al., 2022).

# 6 Benchmarking and Generalization Challenges
As the complexity of GNN architectures for molecular modeling has increased, so too has the need for rigorous benchmarking and a deeper understanding of model generalization. Early datasets, such as QM9 and MoleculeNet, provided standardized platforms for comparing model performance, yet they often suffered from limited dataset size, data leakage through random splits, or narrow chemical diversity. These issues have sometimes led to inflated performance metrics that fail to translate to real-world drug discovery scenarios.

A critical challenge in the field is out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization. In practical drug discovery, newly synthesized molecules often occupy different regions of chemical space than the training data, requiring models to extrapolate rather than interpolate. Scaffold splitting, which partitions molecules based on their core structural scaffolds, provides a more realistic evaluation of OOD performance than random splitting. Recent large-scale empirical studies have systematically evaluated the performance of various GNN architectures under these rigorous conditions, revealing that more complex models do not always yield better generalization, and that simple baseline models can be highly competitive (Shwartz-Ziv et al., 2022). Furthermore, uncertainty quantification remains a significant hurdle; models must not only predict properties accurately but also indicate when their predictions are unreliable, which is crucial for high-stakes pharmaceutical decision-making (Gasteiger et al., 2020). The practical impact of these predictive models is already evident in recent discoveries, such as the identification of novel structural classes of antibiotics using deep learning approaches that leverage molecular graph representations (Stokes et al., 2020).

# 7 Conclusion
The integration of Graph Neural Networks into drug discovery and molecular property prediction represents a paradigm shift in how computational chemistry approaches the design and evaluation of novel therapeutics. This survey has traced the rapid evolution of GNN methodologies, from the foundational message-passing frameworks that operate on 2D molecular topologies to the sophisticated geometric deep learning models that capture the intricate 3D spatial symmetries of molecular conformations. By leveraging the natural graph structure of molecules, GNNs have effectively bypassed the limitations of hand-crafted molecular descriptors, enabling end-to-end learning that continuously improves with data.

The transition from 2D MPNNs to 3D equivariant GNNs has been particularly transformative. While 2D models excel at capturing topological invariants and are highly effective for predicting macroscopic physicochemical properties, the inclusion of geometric information through equivariant architectures has unlocked the ability to accurately model quantum mechanical properties and protein-ligand interactions. This capability is foundational for advanced tasks such as molecular docking and 3D-aware de novo molecule generation, where the spatial orientation of atoms dictates biological activity.

Furthermore, the application of GNNs within diffusion frameworks has revitalized generative chemistry, allowing for the creation of novel molecules directly in 3D space while respecting chemical and physical constraints. These generative models, alongside highly accurate docking networks, are beginning to close the gap between virtual screening and real-world synthesis, promising to drastically reduce the time and cost associated with hit identification and lead optimization.

Despite these remarkable advancements, the field is not without its challenges. As highlighted in our discussion on benchmarking, the generalization capabilities of current GNNs, particularly in out-of-distribution chemical spaces, remain a critical bottleneck. The reliance on random data splits has often obscured the true extrapolative power of these models, and the field must continue to adopt more rigorous evaluation protocols that mirror the realities of pharmaceutical research. Additionally, the interpretability of GNN predictions and the quantification of predictive uncertainty are still open problems that must be addressed to build trust in AI-driven drug discovery pipelines. Overall, GNNs have firmly established themselves as indispensable tools in the modern computational chemist's arsenal, offering a scalable and flexible framework for modeling the complexities of the chemical world.

# 8 Future Directions
As the field of Graph Neural Networks for drug discovery matures, several promising research directions are poised to address current limitations and further accelerate the drug development lifecycle. One of the most pressing needs is the development of molecular foundation models. Inspired by the success of large language models in NLP, pre-training GNNs on massive, unannotated molecular datasets before fine-tuning on downstream property prediction tasks could alleviate the data scarcity problem. Designing self-supervised objectives that capture fundamental chemical principles—rather than just topological heuristics—will be crucial for learning truly transferable representations.

Another vital direction is the integration of prior chemical and physical knowledge into GNN architectures. While equivariant models already respect spatial symmetries, incorporating constraints such as energy conservation, physical symmetries, and chemical valence rules directly into the network's forward pass or loss functions could significantly improve data efficiency and physical validity. This physics-informed approach would ensure that models do not merely fit statistical correlations but learn the underlying causal mechanisms governing molecular behavior.

Furthermore, the scope of graph representations must expand beyond static, single-molecule graphs. Drug discovery is a dynamic process involving complex interactions between proteins, ligands, and the biological environment. Future GNNs will likely focus on dynamic graphs that model molecular flexibility, allosteric effects, and intricate protein-ligand co-evolution. Combining GNNs with advanced sampling techniques or molecular dynamics simulations could provide a more holistic view of binding events.

Finally, enhancing the interpretability and explainability of GNN predictions is essential for clinical translation. Researchers need tools that not only predict a molecule's toxicity or binding affinity but also highlight the specific substructures or interactions driving that prediction. Developing robust post-hoc explanation methods and inherently interpretable GNN architectures will foster greater trust among medicinal chemists, facilitating a collaborative human-AI workflow. Additionally, improving uncertainty quantification will allow computational pipelines to confidently flag molecules for experimental validation, ensuring that only the most promising candidates consume valuable laboratory resources.
