# 1 Abstract
Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have emerged as a powerful paradigm for learning from molecular structures represented as graphs, driving significant advancements in drug discovery and molecular property prediction. The ability of GNNs to operate directly on molecular graphs—where atoms serve as nodes and chemical bonds as edges—enables the capture of complex structural and topological information that traditional hand-crafted molecular fingerprints often miss. This survey provides a comprehensive overview of the application of GNNs in these critical domains. We begin by establishing the foundational GNN architectures, including message passing and graph attention mechanisms, and their adaptation to molecular representations. We then explore the specific task of molecular property prediction, detailing how both 2D topological and 3D geometric information are leveraged to predict quantum mechanical, physicochemical, and biophysical properties. Subsequently, we examine the role of GNNs in generative tasks for de novo drug design, highlighting approaches that navigate the vast chemical space to generate valid and optimized molecules. Furthermore, we discuss recent advances in self-supervised pre-training strategies tailored for molecular graphs, which mitigate the scarcity of labeled biochemical data. Finally, we outline prevailing challenges and propose promising future research directions.

# 2 Introduction
Drug discovery is an inherently complex, resource-intensive, and time-consuming endeavor, traditionally requiring over a decade and billions of dollars to bring a single therapeutic candidate to market. A critical bottleneck in this pipeline is the identification of molecules that possess desirable pharmacological properties while maintaining safety and synthetic feasibility. Historically, computational methods like quantitative structure-activity relationship (QSAR) modeling and molecular dynamics simulations have been employed to screen chemical spaces. However, these traditional approaches often rely on hand-crafted features, such as extended-connectivity fingerprints, which fail to capture the intricate, non-linear relationships inherent in molecular structures.

The advent of deep learning has revolutionized numerous scientific domains, and drug discovery is no exception. Among deep learning architectures, Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have emerged as a particularly natural and powerful fit for molecular modeling. Molecules can be intuitively represented as graphs, where atoms serve as nodes and chemical bonds act as edges. This representation preserves the structural topology and allows GNNs to learn expressive, task-specific representations directly from raw molecular graphs, bypassing the limitations of manual feature engineering.

The core mechanism of GNNs, often formulated as message passing, involves iteratively aggregating neighborhood information to update node representations. This local aggregation naturally mimics the electronic interactions and steric effects that dictate molecular behavior. Over the past few years, the field has rapidly evolved from basic graph convolutional networks operating on 2D topological graphs to sophisticated architectures that incorporate 3D spatial coordinates, geometric symmetries, and attention mechanisms.

This survey provides a structured and comprehensive review of GNNs applied to drug discovery and molecular property prediction. We focus on four primary pillars. First, we detail the foundational GNN architectures that have been adapted for molecular modeling, emphasizing how they process molecular graphs. Second, we examine the task of molecular property prediction, categorizing the types of properties predicted and the datasets that drive this research. Third, we explore the integration of GNNs into generative models for de novo molecular design, a critical step in expanding accessible chemical space. Fourth, we discuss the paradigm shift towards self-supervised pre-training on large-scale unlabeled molecular datasets, a necessity given the paucity of high-quality labeled biochemical data. By organizing the current landscape, this survey aims to highlight the transformative potential of GNNs in accelerating drug discovery while acknowledging the significant challenges that remain.

# 3 Foundational GNN Architectures for Molecular Modeling
The application of GNNs to molecular property prediction relies heavily on robust architectures capable of capturing structural nuances. The Message Passing Neural Network (MPNN) framework formalized the forward pass of many GNNs into two phases: message passing and readout. In the message passing phase, hidden representations of each atom are updated by aggregating messages from adjacent atoms, effectively capturing local chemical environments (Gilmer et al., 2017). This framework unified earlier approaches like graph convolutional networks and provided a modular blueprint for developing new molecular GNNs.

Beyond standard message passing, attention mechanisms have been adapted to weigh the importance of neighboring atoms dynamically. Graph Attention Networks (GATs) compute attention coefficients that allow the model to focus on the most chemically relevant bonds during aggregation, improving the expressiveness of the learned atomic representations (Veličković et al., 2017). Furthermore, directed message passing has been introduced to distinguish the flow of information along bonds, recognizing that the electronic influence between two atoms is not strictly symmetric. These foundational advancements established the algorithmic substrate upon which all subsequent molecular GNN applications are built.

# 4 Molecular Property Prediction
Molecular property prediction is the cornerstone of virtual screening and drug discovery, aiming to infer biochemical activities and physicochemical properties directly from molecular structures. GNNs have demonstrated exceptional performance on benchmark datasets like MoleculeNet, which encompasses tasks ranging from quantum mechanical property calculation to toxicity prediction (Wu et al., 2017). For 2D graphs, models like Directed MPNNs have set state-of-the-art results by effectively capturing bond-directional information, proving highly effective for predicting properties like solubility and blood-brain barrier penetration (Yang et al., 2019).

However, molecular behavior is fundamentally dictated by 3D geometry. To address this, recent architectures have incorporated spatial coordinates to model conformational flexibility and geometric symmetries. SchNet introduced continuous-filter convolutional layers that respect rotational and translational invariance, enabling accurate prediction of quantum mechanical properties like energies and forces from 3D conformations (Schütt et al., 2017). More recently, models like GemNet have advanced geometric deep learning by explicitly modeling directional information and higher-order geometric relations, pushing the boundaries of accuracy for 3D molecular property prediction (Gasteiger et al., 2021).

# 5 Generative Models for De Novo Drug Design
While property prediction evaluates existing molecules, de novo drug design seeks to generate novel molecular structures with desired properties. The vastness of chemical space—estimated to be on the order of 10^60 possible compounds—makes exhaustive search intractable, motivating the use of GNN-based generative models. One prominent approach is the Junction Tree Variational Autoencoder (JT-VAE), which decomposes molecules into valid substructures and generates the molecular graph in two stages: first generating a tree of substructures, and then assembling them into a valid molecule, thereby ensuring chemical validity (Jin et al., 2018).

Alternatively, reinforcement learning has been combined with GNNs to optimize molecular properties directly during generation. Graph Convolutional Policy Networks (GCPN) formulate graph generation as a sequential decision-making process, where an agent builds a molecule atom-by-atom and bond-by-bond, receiving rewards for desired properties and structural validity (You et al., 2018). These GNN-driven generative frameworks allow researchers to navigate the combinatorial complexity of chemical space, proposing optimized lead candidates that are more likely to succeed in subsequent clinical trials.

# 6 Self-Supervised Pre-training for Molecular Graphs
A major bottleneck in molecular machine learning is the scarcity of high-quality labeled data; experimental assays for biological activities are expensive and time-consuming. To overcome this, self-supervised pre-training has emerged as a vital strategy, leveraging massive datasets of unlabeled molecules to learn robust representations before fine-tuning on downstream property prediction tasks. Strategies for pre-training GNNs at both the node and graph levels have shown significant improvements in out-of-distribution generalization, effectively transferring structural knowledge from large-scale unannotated datasets to specific downstream tasks (Hu et al., 2019).

Scaling this paradigm, the GROVER framework integrates message passing networks with the Transformer architecture to perform large-scale self-supervised training on millions of unlabeled molecules. GROVER employs novel self-supervised tasks, such as predicting the presence of substructures and recovering masked atom/bond properties, allowing the model to capture rich semantic and topological information without human annotation (Rong et al., 2020). Such pre-training approaches are revolutionizing molecular property prediction by drastically reducing the reliance on labeled data and improving model robustness.

# 7 Conclusion
The integration of Graph Neural Networks into drug discovery and molecular property prediction represents a paradigm shift in computational chemistry. By leveraging the natural graph representation of molecules, GNNs circumvent the limitations of traditional hand-crafted molecular fingerprints, enabling the end-to-end learning of highly expressive, task-specific representations. This survey has traced the evolution of these methods, from foundational message passing architectures that aggregate local atomic environments to sophisticated geometric networks that capture complex 3D spatial symmetries. We have highlighted how these architectures are applied to critical tasks, including the accurate prediction of quantum, physicochemical, and biophysical properties, as well as the generation of novel, optimized molecular structures through advanced generative models like VAEs and reinforcement learning agents.

Furthermore, the advent of self-supervised pre-training has addressed one of the most pressing challenges in the field: the scarcity of labeled biochemical data. By learning rich structural semantics from vast repositories of unlabeled molecules, pre-trained GNNs have achieved unprecedented generalization capabilities, significantly accelerating the virtual screening pipeline. The convergence of improved architectures, geometric reasoning, and data-efficient learning strategies has positioned GNNs as indispensable tools for modern drug discovery, offering the potential to drastically reduce the time and cost associated with bringing new therapeutics to market. Despite these remarkable advancements, the field remains nascent, with substantial hurdles to overcome before these models can be fully relied upon in clinical settings. The transition from theoretical benchmarking to real-world drug discovery demands rigorous validation and continuous methodological innovation.

# 8 Future Directions
While GNNs have achieved impressive benchmark performance, several critical challenges must be addressed to realize their full potential in practical drug discovery. First, out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization remains a significant hurdle. Real-world drug discovery often involves exploring chemical spaces far removed from the training data, and current GNNs frequently struggle to extrapolate to novel scaffolds. Developing architectures and training regimes that explicitly enforce invariance and robustness to distribution shifts is paramount.

Second, interpretability is crucial for building trust with domain experts. GNNs are largely black-box models, making it difficult to rationalize their predictions in terms of established chemical principles. Future research must focus on explainable AI techniques tailored for molecular graphs, enabling the attribution of predicted properties to specific substructures or interactions, thereby providing actionable chemical insights rather than mere numerical predictions.

Third, the integration of multi-modal data presents a promising frontier. Molecules operate within complex biological systems, and their efficacy is governed by interactions with proteins, nucleic acids, and cellular environments. Moving beyond single-molecule graphs to model protein-ligand interaction graphs and entire biological networks will provide a more holistic understanding of drug action.

Finally, the synthesis of generated molecules remains a critical bottleneck. A molecule may exhibit optimal predicted properties but be impossible to synthesize in a laboratory. Integrating retrosynthesis planning and synthetic accessibility scoring directly into the GNN generation and optimization loop is essential. By coupling generative models with robust synthetic constraints, researchers can ensure that computationally designed molecules are not just theoretically ideal, but practically realizable, bridging the gap between in silico design and experimental validation.
