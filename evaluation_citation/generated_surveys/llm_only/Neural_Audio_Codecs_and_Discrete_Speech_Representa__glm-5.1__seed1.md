# 1 Abstract
Neural audio codecs have emerged as a foundational technology in modern speech and audio processing, bridging the gap between continuous acoustic signals and discrete symbolic representations. By leveraging advanced architectures such as convolutional autoencoders and adversarial training, models like EnCodec and DAC achieve high-fidelity compression while extracting rich discrete tokens via Residual Vector Quantization (RVQ). These discrete speech representations have catalyzed a paradigm shift in generative audio modeling, enabling the application of large language model (LLM) paradigms to audio. This survey provides a comprehensive overview of the landscape of neural audio codecs and discrete speech representations, systematically examining their architectures, tokenization mechanisms, and generative applications. We begin by detailing the architectural innovations that enable high-fidelity audio reconstruction at low bitrates. Next, we explore tokenization strategies, including RVQ and specialized semantic-acoustic disentanglement methods like SpeechTokenizer. We then investigate how these discrete tokens serve as the vocabulary for generative models, powering zero-shot text-to-speech, audio continuation, and speech editing systems. Finally, we discuss the current limitations and outline promising future directions. By synthesizing recent advancements, this survey aims to provide researchers with a structured understanding of how neural codecs are reshaping the intersection of audio signal processing, generative AI, and natural language processing.

# 2 Introduction
The field of speech and audio processing has undergone a profound transformation over the past decade, driven initially by the advent of deep learning and more recently by the emergence of large language models (LLMs). Traditionally, speech systems relied on continuous spectral features such as Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs) or mel-spectrograms. While well-suited for discriminative tasks like automatic speech recognition, these continuous representations are difficult to model autoregressively at scale. The introduction of self-supervised learning (SSL) models like wav2vec 2.0 and HuBERT demonstrated that rich continuous speech representations could be learned from unlabeled data. However, the unprecedented success of autoregressive LLMs in natural language processing (NLP) has created a strong demand for discrete, tokenized representations of audio. Discrete tokens allow the same architectural paradigms, optimization objectives, and efficient inference techniques (such as KV-caching) to be seamlessly applied to speech generation, effectively treating audio as a foreign language.

This demand has catalyzed the development of neural audio codecs—models capable of compressing continuous audio waveforms into discrete tokens while preserving high perceptual quality. Unlike traditional codecs (e.g., Opus, Codec2) designed solely for efficient transmission and storage, neural codecs such as EnCodec and the Descript Audio Codec (DAC) are explicitly designed to serve as tokenizers for downstream generative models. By employing convolutional autoencoders augmented with Residual Vector Quantization (RVQ) and adversarial training, these models achieve impressive compression rates with minimal degradation in audio fidelity, producing tokens that are both compact and reconstructable.

The transition from continuous features to discrete tokens represents a paradigm shift, enabling the formulation of audio generation as a language modeling problem. Pioneering works like AudioLM demonstrated that by training language models on codec tokens, it is possible to generate coherent, high-fidelity speech and music over long horizons. Subsequent models, most notably VALL-E, have leveraged these discrete representations to achieve zero-shot text-to-speech (TTS) synthesis, matching the voice characteristics of unseen speakers from only a brief acoustic prompt, a task previously requiring complex multi-stage pipelines.

The implications of this paradigm shift extend far beyond simple TTS. By converting audio into a discrete token sequence, neural codecs facilitate the integration of speech into unified, multimodal large language models capable of processing and generating text, audio, and visual data within a single framework. This convergence has ignited a rapid influx of research, making it increasingly difficult for practitioners to navigate the diverse array of architectures, quantization strategies, and generative frameworks. Understanding the interplay between codec design and downstream language model performance is now critical. A codec that excels in reconstruction may not necessarily yield the best tokens for generative modeling, as factors like codebook utilization, token sequence length, and the entanglement of acoustic features profoundly impact the efficiency and quality of language model training. Therefore, a systematic review of these interconnected components is essential for advancing the state of the art.

This survey provides a comprehensive and structured overview of the rapidly evolving landscape of neural audio codecs and discrete speech representations. We focus specifically on the architectural designs, the mechanisms of audio tokenization, and the generative applications that these tokens unlock. We exclude traditional parametric codecs and continuous SSL models to maintain a sharp focus on the discrete tokenization paradigm. The survey is organized as follows: Section 3 examines the core architectural components of neural audio codecs. Section 4 delves into tokenization strategies, particularly RVQ and semantic-acoustic disentanglement. Section 5 explores the integration of codec tokens into generative audio language models. Section 6 discusses advanced generative applications, emphasizing zero-shot TTS and voice cloning. Finally, Sections 7 and 8 offer concluding remarks and outline future research directions.

# 3 Architectural Foundations of Neural Audio Codecs
Neural audio codecs are fundamentally built upon an encoder-decoder architecture, augmented with a quantization bottleneck and adversarial training mechanisms. The encoder typically consists of strided convolutional layers that progressively downsample the continuous audio waveform into a lower-dimensional latent representation. The decoder mirrors this process, upsampling the quantized latent vectors back to the original audio resolution. A critical innovation in achieving high-fidelity reconstruction at low bitrates is the integration of multi-scale and multi-period discriminators, which operate in both the time and frequency domains to capture perceptual nuances. The training objective usually combines a reconstruction loss (such as L1 or L2 distance on the waveform and mel-spectrogram), a perceptual loss, and an adversarial loss to ensure the generated audio remains crisp and realistic. EnCodec introduced a streamlined architecture utilizing a multiscale STFT discriminator alongside the standard convolutional encoder-decoder, setting a strong baseline for neural audio compression (Défossez et al., 2022). Building upon this, the Descript Audio Codec (DAC) identified and addressed several artifacts introduced by standard training, proposing improved VQ techniques, quantizer dropout, and an increased number of discriminators to eliminate pitch and periodicity artifacts, thereby pushing the boundaries of high-fidelity reconstruction (Kumar et al., 2023).

# 4 Tokenization Strategies and Residual Vector Quantization
The core mechanism enabling the discretization of continuous latent representations in neural audio codecs is Vector Quantization (VQ). VQ maps continuous latent vectors to a finite codebook, effectively converting the continuous signal into a sequence of discrete tokens. However, single-layer VQ often struggles to reconstruct high-fidelity audio without excessively large codebooks. To overcome this, Residual Vector Quantization (RVQ) is universally adopted. RVQ iteratively quantizes the residual error from previous quantization steps across multiple layers, allowing the model to capture both coarse, broad acoustic features in the initial layers and fine-grained details in the deeper layers. While RVQ provides excellent reconstruction, the resulting tokens are purely acoustic and entangled, making them difficult for language models to learn structural and semantic dependencies. To address this, recent research has focused on disentangling semantic and acoustic tokens. SpeechTokenizer introduces a novel approach by replacing the first RVQ layer with a semantic encoder distilled from a self-supervised speech model (like HuBERT), forcing the first layer to capture linguistic content while subsequent RVQ layers capture the remaining acoustic details (Zhang et al., 2023). Similarly, HiFi-Codec proposes a group-residual VQ structure that reduces the inter-dependency between codebooks, improving the efficiency of the tokenization process and facilitating better language modeling performance (Yang et al., 2023).

# 5 Generative Audio Language Models
The availability of discrete audio tokens has unlocked the application of large language model architectures to audio generation. By treating codec tokens as the vocabulary, audio generation can be formulated as a next-token prediction problem, analogous to text generation in NLP. AudioLM pioneered this approach by demonstrating that a hierarchy of language models could generate high-fidelity audio. It used a two-stage process: first generating coarse semantic tokens (from SSL models) autoregressively, and then conditioning a second model on these semantic tokens to generate fine acoustic tokens from a neural codec (Borsos et al., 2022). This hierarchical modeling ensures both long-term structural coherence and acoustic fidelity. As codec architectures evolved, particularly with the standardization of RVQ, subsequent models began directly modeling the codec tokens. The typical approach involves an autoregressive model predicting the first RVQ layer, followed by non-autoregressive models predicting the subsequent layers in parallel, significantly speeding up inference. SPEAR-TTS extended this paradigm by framing text-to-speech as a translation task between text and discrete speech tokens, demonstrating that minimal supervision is sufficient to train high-fidelity TTS systems using language models over discrete codec tokens (Kharitonov et al., 2023).

# 6 Zero-Shot TTS and Voice Cloning
One of the most transformative applications of neural audio codecs is zero-shot text-to-speech synthesis and voice cloning. By leveraging massive multilingual datasets and the discrete token framework, models can synthesize speech in the voice of an unseen speaker using only a brief acoustic prompt. VALL-E demonstrated that a neural codec language model trained on 60K hours of English speech could achieve remarkable zero-shot capabilities. By conditioning the language model on both the text prompt and the acoustic tokens of a 3-second enrollment snippet, VALL-E preserves the speaker's emotion, acoustic environment, and vocal timbre without any fine-tuning (Wang et al., 2023). This represents a significant leap over traditional multi-speaker TTS systems that require explicit speaker embeddings or extensive adaptation data. The success of VALL-E has spurred further research into aligning text and codec tokens more effectively and reducing the gaps between discrete speech and text representations. Language-Codec addresses the misalignment between text and codec vocabularies by injecting language structure into the codec tokens, making them easier for language models to learn and improving the robustness of zero-shot generation (Ji et al., 2024). These advancements highlight the potential of discrete codec tokens to serve as a universal interface for speech generation, enabling highly scalable and versatile voice synthesis systems.

# 7 Conclusion
The advent of neural audio codecs and discrete speech representations marks a pivotal evolution in the intersection of audio signal processing and natural language processing. By effectively bridging the continuous nature of acoustic signals with the discrete token paradigms required by large language models, these technologies have redefined the possibilities of generative audio. This survey has systematically examined the core components of this transformation. We began by exploring the architectural foundations, highlighting how convolutional autoencoders, multi-scale discriminators, and adversarial training enable high-fidelity audio reconstruction at remarkably low bitrates. Models such as EnCodec and DAC have demonstrated that neural networks can surpass traditional algorithms in perceptual quality when armed with sophisticated quantization bottlenecks. We then delved into the tokenization strategies, emphasizing the role of Residual Vector Quantization (RVQ) in capturing both coarse and fine acoustic details. Crucially, the field is moving toward disentangled tokenization, as seen in SpeechTokenizer, which separates semantic content from acoustic details, thereby streamlining the learning process for downstream language models. The generative applications of these discrete tokens represent the most impactful outcome of this research. By treating audio tokens as a language, models like AudioLM have achieved unprecedented coherence in audio continuation. Furthermore, the application of these codecs to zero-shot text-to-speech, exemplified by VALL-E, has demonstrated that high-fidelity voice cloning can be achieved with just seconds of enrollment audio, eliminating the need for speaker-specific fine-tuning. In summary, neural audio codecs have evolved from mere compression tools into foundational tokenizers that empower a new generation of audio language models. They provide the essential vocabulary that allows the powerful autoregressive architectures of NLP to be seamlessly transferred to the audio domain, fundamentally altering the landscape of speech synthesis, voice conversion, and audio generation. The transition from complex, multi-stage generative pipelines to unified language modeling frameworks simplifies system design and scales effectively with data and compute.

# 8 Future Directions
Despite the remarkable progress in neural audio codecs and discrete speech representations, several significant challenges and open questions remain, pointing toward promising avenues for future research. First, the computational complexity and latency of current codec architectures present a bottleneck for real-time applications, such as streaming voice conversion and live communication. Future work must focus on designing lightweight, sub-millisecond latency codecs without compromising the richness of the discrete tokens. Second, while current RVQ-based codecs produce high-fidelity audio, the entangled nature of the acoustic tokens across multiple codebooks makes them inefficient for autoregressive modeling. Developing more compact, factorized, or hierarchically disentangled token spaces—where prosody, speaker identity, and content are strictly isolated into separate codebooks—would drastically reduce the burden on language models and improve generation efficiency. Third, the multilingual and cross-lingual capabilities of codec language models require deeper investigation. While current models perform well on high-resource languages, their performance degrades on low-resource languages due to imbalanced training data. Future codecs must be inherently multilingual, ensuring that the codebook space represents global phonetic diversity equitably. Fourth, the security and ethical implications of zero-shot voice cloning pose a critical challenge. As models become capable of perfectly mimicking any voice from a short prompt, developing robust watermarking techniques and authentication mechanisms embedded directly into the codec tokens is imperative to prevent malicious deepfakes. Finally, the integration of discrete audio tokens with multimodal large language models is still in its infancy. Future research should explore unified tokenization frameworks that can seamlessly process and generate text, audio, and visual signals within a single model, enabling truly omnidirectional multimodal reasoning and generation. By addressing these challenges, the next generation of neural audio codecs will not only enhance the fidelity and efficiency of speech systems but also unlock new frontiers in human-computer interaction and artificial intelligence.
