# 1 Abstract
The emergence of neural audio codecs has fundamentally transformed audio processing by enabling the conversion of continuous waveforms into discrete token representations. This survey provides a comprehensive overview of the architectures, tokenization mechanisms, and generative applications of neural audio codecs and discrete speech representations. We begin by examining the foundational encoder-decoder architectures coupled with Residual Vector Quantization (RVQ) that enable high-fidelity audio reconstruction at low bitrates. We then explore the tokenization process, detailing how discrete acoustic and semantic tokens are extracted and utilized to bridge the gap between audio signals and large language models. Furthermore, we survey the transformative impact of these discrete representations on generative tasks, focusing on speech synthesis, zero-shot voice cloning, music generation, and audio generation. By unifying the paradigms of audio compression and language modeling, neural audio codecs have catalyzed a new era of generative models capable of producing complex acoustic scenes with unprecedented controllability and fidelity. Finally, we discuss the current limitations and outline promising future directions, including improved quantization techniques, real-time streaming capabilities, and the development of unified multimodal models. The transition from continuous signal processing to discrete token-based modeling has unlocked unprecedented scalability, allowing the direct application of transformer architectures to audio. As the field progresses, addressing challenges related to computational efficiency and token redundancy will be crucial for the next generation of audio foundation models.

# 2 Introduction
The field of audio processing has historically relied on continuous signal representations, such as mel-spectrograms or handcrafted acoustic features, which are inherently tied to the analog nature of sound. However, the recent advent of large language models (LLMs) has precipitated a paradigm shift toward discrete token-based representations. Just as text is naturally represented as a sequence of discrete tokens, the discretization of audio into compact, discrete units allows for the direct application of powerful sequence-to-sequence models originally developed for natural language processing. At the heart of this transformation lies the neural audio codec, a class of models designed to compress continuous audio waveforms into discrete codes and reconstruct them with high fidelity.

The motivation for this survey stems from the explosive growth of research at the intersection of neural audio compression and generative modeling. Traditional audio codecs, such as Opus or MP3, were optimized solely for transmission and storage, lacking the representational qualities necessary for generative tasks. In contrast, modern neural codecs leverage deep learning to produce discrete tokens that are not only compressive but also highly expressive, capturing both the fine acoustic details and the broader semantic structure of audio. This dual capability has proven instrumental in advancing generative audio models, enabling breakthroughs in text-to-speech, music generation, and zero-shot voice cloning.

By tokenizing audio into discrete units, researchers can frame audio generation as a language modeling problem. This approach has led to the development of audio language models that can generate complex acoustic scenes, mimic voices from brief prompts, and compose music with intricate temporal structures. The decoupling of semantic and acoustic tokens further allows for hierarchical generation, where high-level semantic content is planned first, followed by the synthesis of fine acoustic details. Despite these advances, the field remains fragmented, with rapid iterations on codec architectures, quantization schemes, and generative frameworks occurring simultaneously.

The ability to represent audio as discrete tokens has fundamentally altered the landscape of generative AI. Previously, generative models relied heavily on continuous latent spaces and diffusion processes, which, while effective, often struggled with long-term coherence and scalability. Discrete tokens circumvent these issues by allowing the use of autoregressive transformers, which excel at capturing long-range dependencies. This has led to a convergence between audio and language modeling, where the same architectural principles can be applied across modalities. Furthermore, the compression aspect of neural codecs is not merely a byproduct but a critical feature; by reducing the temporal resolution and dimensionality of audio, codecs make it computationally feasible to train large-scale autoregressive models on hours of high-fidelity audio data. This intersection of compression, discretization, and generation represents a fertile ground for innovation, necessitating a comprehensive survey to synthesize these parallel developments.

The scope of this survey encompasses the core architectures of neural audio codecs, the mechanisms of audio tokenization, and the generative applications that these representations enable. We focus primarily on the transition from continuous waveforms to discrete tokens, the role of Residual Vector Quantization (RVQ), and how these tokens are consumed by downstream generative models. We do not cover traditional signal processing codecs or non-neural feature extraction methods in depth, as our focus is strictly on neural approaches and their integration with modern language modeling techniques.

The structure of this survey is as follows. Section 3 delves into the architectures of neural audio codecs, detailing the encoder-decoder frameworks and adversarial training mechanisms. Section 4 examines the tokenization process, focusing on Residual Vector Quantization and the distinction between acoustic and semantic tokens. Section 5 explores generative applications in speech, including zero-shot text-to-speech and voice cloning. Section 6 extends the discussion to music and general audio generation, highlighting the versatility of discrete audio tokens. Finally, Sections 7 and 8 provide a conclusion and outline future directions for research in this dynamic field.

# 3 Architectures of Neural Audio Codecs
Neural audio codecs are designed to transform raw audio waveforms into compact representations and reconstruct them with minimal perceptual loss. The foundational architecture of these models typically consists of an encoder, a quantizer, and a decoder. The encoder progressively downsamples the raw audio into a continuous latent representation using strided convolutional layers. This latent representation is then discretized by the quantizer, which maps the continuous vectors to a finite set of codebook entries. Finally, the decoder upsamples the discrete latent codes back to the original audio waveform, often using transposed convolutions.

A critical innovation in the architecture of neural audio codecs is the incorporation of adversarial training. By combining reconstruction losses with a discriminator network, codecs are forced to generate audio that not only minimizes mathematical error but also sounds perceptually realistic to human listeners. The discriminator typically operates at multiple scales, evaluating both the local waveform structure and the global spectral envelope. This multi-scale adversarial approach, pioneered by SoundStream (Zeghidour et al., 2021), ensures that the reconstructed audio maintains high fidelity even at very low bitrates. Subsequent architectures, such as EnCodec, have refined this paradigm by introducing lightweight convolutional models and periodic activation functions, further improving the efficiency and quality of the reconstruction (Defossez et al., 2022). The encoder and decoder are often augmented with residual blocks and skip connections to capture the intricate temporal dynamics of audio signals. These architectural choices enable neural codecs to achieve compression rates that rival or surpass traditional algorithms while maintaining the perceptual quality necessary for downstream generative tasks.

# 4 Tokenization and Discrete Representations
The transition from continuous latent spaces to discrete tokens is orchestrated by the quantization module, with Residual Vector Quantization (RVQ) emerging as the de facto standard. Unlike standard Vector Quantization (VQ), which uses a single codebook, RVQ employs a cascade of codebooks. The first codebook quantizes the continuous latent vector, and subsequent codebooks quantize the residual error from the previous step. This hierarchical quantization allows the model to capture both the coarse, low-frequency structures of the audio and the fine-grained, high-frequency details. By adjusting the number of RVQ layers, one can seamlessly trade off between bitrate and reconstruction quality, making the representation highly adaptable.

The discrete tokens produced by RVQ serve as the vocabulary for audio language models. However, a key challenge is disentangling the semantic content from the acoustic details. Early approaches treated all tokens equally, but this often led to language models struggling with long-term coherence due to the overwhelming acoustic variability. To address this, recent methods have proposed decoupling semantic and acoustic tokens. For instance, AudioLM introduces a hierarchical modeling scheme where semantic tokens (derived from a self-supervised speech model like w2v-BERT) capture the linguistic content, while acoustic tokens (derived from the codec) capture the speaker identity and recording conditions (Borsos et al., 2022). This separation allows the generative model to first plan the high-level semantic structure before filling in the acoustic details, significantly improving the coherence and quality of generated audio. Furthermore, specialized tokenizers like SpeechTokenizer have been designed to explicitly force the first RVQ layer to capture semantic information, aligning it with self-supervised representations while leaving the remaining layers to handle acoustic residuals (Zhang et al., 2023). This targeted tokenization streamlines the generative process and reduces the burden on the language model.

# 5 Generative Applications in Speech
The availability of discrete audio tokens has revolutionized generative speech applications, most notably text-to-speech (TTS) and voice cloning. By treating speech generation as a sequence-to-sequence translation problem from text tokens to audio tokens, researchers have leveraged the power of large language models to achieve unprecedented naturalness and robustness. Traditional TTS systems relied on complex, multi-stage pipelines, but token-based models can directly generate high-fidelity audio from text in an end-to-end manner.

One of the most groundbreaking applications is zero-shot voice cloning, where a model synthesizes speech in a voice it has never explicitly trained on, using only a brief acoustic prompt. VALL-E demonstrated the efficacy of this approach by framing TTS as a conditional language modeling task over EnCodec tokens (Wang et al., 2023). By prompting a large autoregressive transformer with both text and a short snippet of the target speaker's voice, VALL-E can preserve the speaker's vocal timbre, emotion, and acoustic environment with remarkable accuracy. This capability relies heavily on the codec's ability to disentangle and faithfully reconstruct speaker-specific characteristics from the discrete tokens. Furthermore, the hierarchical nature of RVQ tokens allows for a two-stage generation process: an autoregressive model predicts the coarse tokens (capturing content and prosody), followed by a non-autoregressive model that predicts the fine acoustic tokens, drastically accelerating inference. Extensions like VALL-E X have further expanded this paradigm to cross-lingual voice preservation, enabling a user to speak in a foreign language while retaining their original voice. These advancements highlight how discrete codec tokens serve as a versatile interface for controllable and scalable speech generation.

# 6 Generative Applications in Music and Audio
Beyond speech, discrete audio tokens have catalyzed significant advancements in music and general audio generation. Music generation presents unique challenges, requiring models to maintain long-term temporal coherence, adhere to complex harmonic structures, and support fine-grained user control. By converting music into discrete tokens, the same autoregressive transformer architectures used in text generation can be applied to musical composition and synthesis.

MusicGen exemplifies the power of this approach by utilizing a single-stage transformer language model over EnCodec tokens to generate high-quality music (Copet et al., 2023). Unlike hierarchical models that generate coarse-to-fine, MusicGen predicts all RVQ codebooks in parallel using a delay pattern, which significantly improves generation speed and reduces error accumulation. It also integrates text conditioning through cross-attention, allowing for precise control over the genre, instrumentation, and mood of the generated audio. Similarly, AudioGen focuses on environmental sound generation, tackling the complexity of everyday acoustic scenes (Kreuk et al., 2022). By training on large datasets of audio-text pairs, AudioGen can generate realistic sound effects, such as barking dogs or passing cars, conditioned on textual descriptions. The success of these models underscores the versatility of neural codec tokens; they are not merely a compression tool but a universal representation that bridges the gap between abstract human concepts (expressed as text) and the rich, continuous reality of sound. The ability to generate diverse audio modalities using a unified token-based framework points toward a future of general-purpose audio foundation models.

# 7 Conclusion
The advent of neural audio codecs and discrete speech representations marks a pivotal evolution in the field of audio processing and generation. By bridging the continuous nature of audio waveforms with the discrete paradigms of natural language processing, these technologies have unlocked unprecedented capabilities in generative AI. This survey has traced the development of neural codec architectures, highlighting how encoder-decoder frameworks augmented with multi-scale adversarial training and Residual Vector Quantization (RVQ) have achieved remarkable compression rates while preserving perceptual fidelity. The shift from traditional signal processing codecs to neural codecs has not only redefined audio compression but also established a new modality for large language models.

The tokenization process, particularly the hierarchical structuring of semantic and acoustic tokens, has proven essential for the success of modern audio language models. By decoupling the high-level linguistic or musical content from the fine-grained acoustic details, these models can generate complex audio with long-term coherence and high fidelity. This decoupling has facilitated a hierarchical generation process, where autoregressive models can first plan the semantic structure before filling in the acoustic nuances, balancing computational efficiency with output quality.

Furthermore, the generative applications enabled by discrete audio tokens are transformative. In the realm of speech, models like VALL-E have demonstrated the ability to perform zero-shot voice cloning, synthesizing highly realistic speech that preserves speaker identity and emotion from minimal prompts. In music and environmental audio, models like MusicGen and AudioGen have shown that complex acoustic scenes and musical compositions can be generated and controlled through simple textual prompts. These achievements underscore the versatility and scalability of discrete audio representations, positioning them as the foundational building blocks for the next generation of audio foundation models. 

The implications of this paradigm shift extend beyond mere technical improvements. By providing a common discrete language for audio, neural codecs facilitate the integration of auditory modalities into broader multimodal AI systems. This interoperability means that the same architectural principles and scaling laws that have driven progress in text-based LLMs can now be directly applied to audio, democratizing access to high-quality audio generation and synthesis. As the field matures, the continued refinement of codec architectures and tokenization strategies will undoubtedly yield even more robust, efficient, and controllable generative models, solidifying the role of discrete audio representations as a cornerstone of modern artificial intelligence.

# 8 Future Directions
While neural audio codecs and discrete representations have catalyzed remarkable progress, several promising avenues remain for future research. First, the current reliance on Residual Vector Quantization (RVQ) introduces a variable bitrate and a hierarchical token structure that can complicate sequence modeling. Future work could explore alternative quantization methods, such as lookup-free quantization or finite scalar quantization, which have shown promise in simplifying the token space and improving codebook utilization. Reducing the number of tokens required to represent a given audio segment without sacrificing fidelity is also critical for scaling up autoregressive models, as the quadratic complexity of attention mechanisms makes long audio sequences computationally prohibitive.

Second, the development of unified, streaming-capable codecs is an urgent priority. Most current neural codecs operate on fixed-length audio segments, introducing latency and boundary artifacts that hinder real-time applications like live voice conversion or interactive dialogue systems. Designing causal, low-latency codecs that can process audio in a streaming fashion while maintaining high reconstruction quality will be essential for deploying these models in production environments.

Third, there is a growing need for truly multimodal codecs that can jointly represent audio, text, and visual information within a shared discrete space. While current models rely on separate tokenizers for different modalities, a unified tokenizer could enable seamless cross-modal generation and reasoning, allowing a model to generate the sound of a barking dog directly from an image, or to synthesize speech with facial expressions.

Furthermore, the disentanglement of audio attributes within the discrete latent space remains an open problem. While current approaches separate semantic and acoustic tokens, finer-grained control over attributes such as room acoustics, recording device characteristics, and subtle emotional intonations is still limited. Future codecs might incorporate disentanglement mechanisms directly into the quantization process, allowing users to manipulate specific acoustic properties independently. Additionally, improving the robustness of these codecs to diverse acoustic conditions and noisy environments will be vital for their deployment in real-world scenarios. As we look ahead, the intersection of neural audio codecs with emerging techniques such as diffusion models and flow matching also presents a fertile ground for hybrid architectures that combine the strengths of continuous and discrete generation paradigms. Finally, as these generative models become more capable, addressing the ethical implications and potential misuse of zero-shot voice cloning is paramount, necessitating robust watermarking and detection mechanisms.
