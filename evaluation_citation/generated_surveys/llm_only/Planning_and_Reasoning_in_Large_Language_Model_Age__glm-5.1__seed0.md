# 1 Abstract

The rapid advancement of Large Language Models (LLMs) has catalyzed a paradigm shift from static text generators to dynamic, autonomous agents capable of complex reasoning, planning, and tool utilization. This survey provides a comprehensive overview of the foundational methodologies and recent advancements in planning and reasoning within LLM agents. We systematically explore four interdependent pillars of LLM agent research. First, we examine reasoning and planning methodologies, detailing how prompt engineering and structured search algorithms enable models to decompose complex tasks. Second, we investigate tool use and integration, highlighting mechanisms through which LLMs extend their capabilities beyond parametric memory by interacting with external APIs and software. Third, we delve into interactive and reflective agent architectures, emphasizing the role of environmental feedback and self-correction in sustaining long-term autonomy. Finally, we review evaluation benchmarks designed to assess agent performance across diverse domains, including web navigation and software engineering. By synthesizing these areas, we identify key challenges, such as error compounding and hallucination in multi-step reasoning, and outline the current state-of-the-art solutions. This survey aims to serve as a foundational resource for researchers seeking to understand the architectural and algorithmic underpinnings of LLM-based agents, providing clarity on how reasoning, planning, and tool use converge to create robust, interactive AI systems. We conclude by emphasizing the necessity for unified frameworks that bridge the gap between isolated reasoning capabilities and holistic, real-world agent deployment.

# 2 Introduction

The emergence of Large Language Models (LLMs) represents one of the most significant milestones in artificial intelligence. Initially designed for natural language processing tasks such as translation and summarization, LLMs have rapidly evolved far beyond their original scope. Today, these models are increasingly being deployed as autonomous agents—systems that can perceive their environment, make decisions, and execute actions to achieve specific goals. This transition from passive text generators to active participants in complex environments hinges on two critical capabilities: planning and reasoning. Planning allows an agent to formulate a sequence of actions to reach a goal, while reasoning provides the logical framework necessary to evaluate states, infer consequences, and adapt to new information.

The motivation behind this survey stems from the explosive growth of research focused on augmenting LLMs with agentic capabilities. Traditional LLMs, despite their impressive linguistic fluency, suffer from well-documented limitations, including hallucinations, an inability to access real-time information, and a lack of grounding in physical or digital environments. To overcome these constraints, researchers have developed sophisticated architectures that integrate LLMs with external tools, memory systems, and iterative feedback loops. This integration has given rise to LLM agents capable of navigating the web, writing and debugging code, and even controlling robotic systems. However, the rapid pace of innovation has led to a fragmented landscape, where methodologies for reasoning, tool use, and evaluation are often developed in isolation.

This survey seeks to unify these disparate threads by providing a holistic examination of planning and reasoning in LLM agents. We focus specifically on the algorithmic and architectural innovations that enable autonomous behavior, rather than the underlying mechanics of model pre-training. The scope of this paper encompasses four primary domains: (1) the reasoning and planning methodologies that allow LLMs to decompose complex objectives; (2) the mechanisms for tool use that extend the operational reach of these models; (3) the interactive and reflective paradigms that facilitate robust, long-term autonomy; and (4) the evaluation benchmarks designed to measure agent performance in grounded environments.

Understanding these components is crucial for advancing the field. Without robust planning mechanisms, agents are prone to getting stuck in suboptimal trajectories. Without tool use, they remain disconnected from the world, unable to verify their outputs or take meaningful action. Without reflection, they cannot learn from their mistakes, leading to repetitive failures. Finally, without rigorous evaluation, it is impossible to track progress or identify systemic weaknesses. The environments these agents operate within—ranging from simulated sandboxes to live software repositories—are inherently complex, stochastic, and partially observable. Consequently, the static, single-pass inference mechanisms of traditional language modeling are fundamentally inadequate. Agents must instead engage in dynamic, multi-step interactions, continuously updating their beliefs and strategies based on environmental feedback. This necessity has driven the development of specialized planning paradigms, such as tree-based search and iterative refinement, which explicitly treat the LLM as a policy network within a broader agent loop. Furthermore, the integration of external tools transforms the LLM from a closed system into an open one, capable of performing mathematical computations, querying databases, and executing code. These advancements collectively represent a fundamental re-architecture of how we deploy language models, shifting the focus from mere generation to grounded, goal-directed action.

The structure of this survey is as follows. Section 3 delves into reasoning and planning methodologies, exploring techniques such as chain-of-thought prompting and structured search algorithms. Section 4 examines tool use, detailing how models are trained or prompted to interact with external APIs and software. Section 5 discusses interactive and reflective agents, focusing on architectures that leverage environmental feedback for self-correction. Section 6 reviews the current landscape of evaluation benchmarks, highlighting the challenges of assessing agentic capabilities. Finally, Sections 7 and 8 offer concluding remarks and outline promising future directions for the field.

# 3 Reasoning and Planning Methodologies

The capacity for complex reasoning and structured planning is the cornerstone of autonomous LLM agents. Early approaches relied heavily on standard prompting techniques, which often failed to elicit the multi-step logical deductions required for agentic tasks. The introduction of Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting marked a significant leap forward, enabling models to intermediate reasoning steps. However, CoT often struggles with complex tasks requiring extensive lookahead and backtracking. To address this, researchers have developed more sophisticated planning paradigms. One prominent approach is Plan-and-Solve Prompting, which explicitly decouples the planning phase from the execution phase, prompting the model to first generate a high-level plan and then execute each step sequentially (Wang et al., 2023). This decomposition mitigates the cognitive load on the model and reduces reasoning errors in complex tasks.

Beyond linear decomposition, structured search algorithms have been adapted for LLMs to explore a broader space of possible actions. Tree of Thoughts (ToT) extends CoT by framing problem-solving as a search over a tree, where each node represents a partial solution or a thought step. ToT allows the model to evaluate different reasoning paths, look ahead, and backtrack when a path proves unviable, mimicking human deliberative reasoning (Yao et al., 2023). Similarly, Graph of Thoughts models the reasoning process as a graph, enabling the merging of different reasoning paths and more flexible exploration of the solution space. These methodologies fundamentally shift the inference paradigm from a single, left-to-right pass to an iterative, deliberative process, significantly enhancing the planning capabilities of LLM agents in environments requiring strategic foresight.

# 4 Tool Use and Integration

While advanced reasoning allows LLMs to conceptualize solutions, their inherent lack of access to real-time information and inability to perform precise computations severely limits their autonomy. Tool use bridges this gap by enabling LLM agents to interact with external software, APIs, and digital environments. The integration of tools transforms LLMs from isolated models into central orchestrators of broader computational systems. A foundational approach to tool integration is teaching models to autonomously generate specific tokens that trigger API calls. Toolformer demonstrated that LLMs could be fine-tuned in a self-supervised manner to decide which tools to call, when to call them, and how to pass arguments, significantly enhancing their mathematical and factual accuracy without sacrificing core linguistic capabilities (Schick et al., 2023).

In more complex agentic architectures, tool use is not merely about executing a single API, but rather orchestrating multiple specialized models or tools to solve a multi-modal task. HuggingGPT exemplifies this paradigm by utilizing an LLM as a central controller that parses user requests, selects appropriate models from the Hugging Face ecosystem, and synthesizes their outputs into a coherent final response (Shen et al., 2023). This architecture allows a single LLM to leverage the strengths of numerous specialized models for tasks such as image generation, object detection, and speech recognition. The challenge in tool use lies not only in the mechanical execution of APIs but in the semantic alignment between the model's reasoning and the tool's expected inputs, as well as the robust handling of tool failures and error propagation.

# 5 Interactive and Reflective Agents

Real-world environments are inherently dynamic and unpredictable, requiring agents to continuously interact with their surroundings and adapt their strategies based on feedback. Static planning is often insufficient; agents must be capable of reflection and self-correction. The ReAct framework pioneered the integration of reasoning and acting within a single prompt, allowing the agent to interleave thought processes with environmental actions. This synergy enables the agent to update its reasoning based on observations gathered from the environment, creating a more grounded and adaptive decision-making loop (Yao et al., 2023).

Building upon interactive paradigms, reflective architectures introduce mechanisms for learning from past failures. Reflexion implements verbal reinforcement learning, where the agent receives a linguistic reward—typically an error summary generated by the LLM itself—after a failed trajectory. Instead of updating model weights, Reflexion stores this feedback in an episodic memory buffer, which is consulted in subsequent trials to avoid repeating the same mistakes (Shinn et al., 2023). This approach allows LLM agents to achieve remarkable improvements in long-horizon tasks without the computational expense of traditional gradient-based fine-tuning. Interactive and reflective mechanisms are critical for sustaining autonomy, ensuring that agents can navigate the compounding errors and partial observability characteristic of complex, real-world tasks.

# 6 Evaluation Benchmarks

As LLM agents become increasingly capable, the development of robust evaluation benchmarks is essential to measure progress and identify limitations. Traditional NLP benchmarks are inadequate for assessing agentic capabilities, which require dynamic interaction with an environment. Consequently, researchers have developed interactive benchmarks that simulate real-world tasks. WebShop is an early example, providing a simulated e-commerce environment where agents must navigate a website, search for products, and make purchases according to specific user instructions. This benchmark evaluates an agent's ability to handle multi-step navigation, parse HTML, and make grounded decisions under uncertainty (Yao et al., 2022).

More recently, comprehensive evaluation suites have emerged to assess a broader range of agentic skills. AgentBench provides a multi-dimensional framework encompassing eight distinct environments, including operating systems, web browsing, and software development scenarios. By testing LLMs across these diverse domains, AgentBench reveals that while proprietary models exhibit strong agentic capabilities, there remains a significant performance gap compared to open-source alternatives, particularly in complex reasoning and long-context dependency tasks (Liu et al., 2023). These benchmarks highlight the necessity of evaluating agents not just on task success, but on efficiency, robustness to perturbation, and the ability to recover from errors, pushing the field toward more reliable and deployable autonomous systems.

# 7 Conclusion

The transition of Large Language Models from static text generators to dynamic, autonomous agents marks a pivotal evolution in artificial intelligence. This survey has systematically examined the core pillars enabling this transformation: planning and reasoning methodologies, tool use and integration, interactive and reflective architectures, and evaluation benchmarks. We have detailed how naive prompting strategies have given way to sophisticated planning algorithms, such as tree and graph-based searches, which allow models to deliberate over multiple trajectories and backtrack from suboptimal paths. Furthermore, we highlighted how the integration of external tools fundamentally expands the operational scope of LLMs, allowing them to transcend the limitations of their parametric memory to perform precise computations and interact with digital ecosystems.

The shift toward interactive and reflective paradigms, such as ReAct and Reflexion, underscores a critical realization: in complex, stochastic environments, static planning is insufficient. Agents must be capable of closing the loop between action and observation, continuously updating their beliefs and learning from their failures through verbal feedback and episodic memory. These reflective mechanisms represent a departure from traditional reinforcement learning, offering a parameter-efficient method for achieving sustained autonomy.

Concurrently, the development of robust evaluation benchmarks like WebShop and AgentBench has provided the community with the necessary tools to rigorously assess these capabilities. These benchmarks have revealed that while current state-of-the-art models demonstrate impressive agentic behaviors, they remain prone to error compounding, hallucination, and inefficient exploration. The evaluation landscape continues to evolve, moving toward more realistic, multi-turn, and multi-modal environments that better reflect the complexities of real-world deployment.

In summary, the architecture of modern LLM agents is a complex orchestration of reasoning, action, and reflection. The convergence of structured planning algorithms with dynamic tool use and self-corrective feedback loops has established a robust foundation for autonomous AI. However, the field remains in its nascent stages. The gap between laboratory benchmarks and real-world deployment is still significant, and the reliability of these agents in high-stakes scenarios remains an open question. As research progresses, the integration of these disparate components into cohesive, theoretically grounded systems will be paramount in realizing the full potential of LLM-based autonomous agents.

# 8 Future Directions

As the field of LLM agents continues to mature, several critical avenues for future research emerge. First, there is an urgent need to address the scalability and efficiency of current planning and reasoning methodologies. While tree-based and graph-based search algorithms significantly improve decision-making, their computational cost is prohibitive, often requiring dozens of LLM calls for a single task. Future work must focus on developing more efficient search heuristics, adaptive depth limits, and hybrid approaches that dynamically allocate compute based on task complexity.

Second, the robustness and safety of tool use remain a significant concern. Current agents are highly susceptible to prompt injection and adversarial manipulation, particularly when interacting with untrusted external environments. Research into secure tool execution, formal verification of agent actions, and robust failure detection mechanisms is essential before these systems can be deployed in high-stakes domains such as finance or healthcare.

Third, while reflective paradigms like Reflexion have shown promise, they rely heavily on the model's ability to accurately diagnose its own errors. When an agent fails due to a fundamental misunderstanding of the environment, its self-generated verbal feedback may be flawed, leading to repetitive failure loops. Future research should explore grounding the reflection process in external, verifiable signals, and developing more sophisticated memory architectures that can generalize across tasks rather than merely memorizing specific trajectories.

Fourth, the current evaluation benchmarks, while a step in the right direction, often lack the complexity and partial observability of true real-world environments. There is a need for open-ended, multi-agent benchmarks where LLMs must collaborate, negotiate, and compete with both human and AI counterparts. Furthermore, evaluation metrics must evolve beyond simple task success rates to encompass efficiency, safety, and adherence to implicit social norms.

Finally, the theoretical underpinnings of LLM agent behavior remain poorly understood. Why do certain prompting strategies elicit stronger planning capabilities? How do reasoning capabilities scale with model size and context length? Addressing these questions will require rigorous empirical studies and the development of formal frameworks to characterize the boundaries of LLM agency. By tackling these challenges, the research community can pave the way for the next generation of reliable, efficient, and truly autonomous LLM agents.
