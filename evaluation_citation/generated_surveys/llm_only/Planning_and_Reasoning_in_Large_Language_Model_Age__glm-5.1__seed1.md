# 1 Abstract
The integration of planning and reasoning capabilities into Large Language Model (LLM) agents marks a paradigm shift from static text generation to dynamic, interactive problem-solving. This survey provides a comprehensive overview of the methodologies, tool use mechanisms, and evaluation benchmarks that define the landscape of LLM-based agents. We begin by examining the foundational reasoning techniques, such as chain-of-thought prompting and its extensions, which enable LLMs to decompose complex tasks. We then explore planning strategies, including tree-of-thought and reflexive approaches, which allow agents to anticipate outcomes and adapt their strategies. A critical dimension of autonomous agents is their ability to interact with external environments; thus, we analyze tool use and action generation frameworks, highlighting how models learn to invoke APIs and leverage external resources. Furthermore, we review the emerging suite of evaluation benchmarks designed to assess agent performance in diverse interactive settings, from web navigation to multi-task orchestration. By synthesizing these areas, we identify the current limitations of LLM agents, including error compounding and hallucination in multi-step reasoning. This survey aims to serve as a foundational resource for researchers seeking to understand the state-of-the-art in LLM agent planning, reasoning, and tool use, paving the way for more robust and reliable autonomous systems.

# 2 Introduction
The advent of Large Language Models (LLMs) has fundamentally transformed the landscape of artificial intelligence, moving the field from narrow, task-specific systems toward general-purpose reasoning engines. Initially celebrated for their prowess in text generation, translation, and summarization, LLMs are increasingly being deployed as autonomous agents capable of perceiving environments, reasoning about goals, and executing actions. This evolution from passive language models to active LLM agents represents one of the most exciting frontiers in AI research. However, the realization of truly autonomous agents hinges on two critical cognitive capabilities: planning and reasoning. Without the ability to reason, an agent cannot decompose complex objectives into manageable sub-tasks; without planning, it cannot sequence these sub-tasks into a coherent, goal-oriented strategy.

The motivation for this survey stems from the rapid and fragmented development of techniques aimed at enhancing these exact capabilities. As the community has recognized the limitations of zero-shot prompting for complex, multi-step problems, a proliferation of methods—ranging from sophisticated prompt engineering to fine-tuning on trajectory data—has emerged. Concurrently, the closed-world nature of LLMs necessitates mechanisms for tool use, allowing agents to offload computations, access real-time information, and interact with digital or physical environments via APIs. The intersection of reasoning, planning, and tool use forms the bedrock of modern LLM agent architectures.

Despite the rapid progress, the field lacks a unified framework for understanding how these components interact. Researchers often propose novel reasoning strategies or tool-use mechanisms in isolation, making it difficult to assess their synergies and trade-offs. Furthermore, the evaluation of LLM agents remains a challenging endeavor. Traditional NLP metrics are insufficient for assessing an agent's ability to navigate an interactive environment or utilize a tool correctly. Consequently, a new generation of evaluation benchmarks has emerged, specifically designed to test agentic capabilities in grounded, dynamic settings.

This survey seeks to bridge these gaps by providing a structured and comprehensive review of planning and reasoning in LLM agents. We focus specifically on the methods that enable complex problem-solving, the integration of external tools to augment the agent's capabilities, and the benchmarks used to measure success. We deliberately constrain our scope to the algorithmic and architectural innovations that empower LLMs as agents, rather than the broader applications of generative AI.

The structure of this survey is as follows: Section 3 delves into the reasoning and planning methodologies that underpin agent behavior, exploring both prompting techniques and self-reflective architectures. Section 4 examines the critical role of tool use, detailing how agents are trained or prompted to leverage external APIs and models. Section 5 surveys the evaluation benchmarks that have been developed to assess these agents in interactive environments. Section 6 discusses the overarching challenges and limitations currently hindering the deployment of LLM agents. Finally, Sections 7 and 8 offer concluding remarks and outline promising directions for future research.

# 3 Reasoning and Planning Methodologies
The capacity for LLM agents to solve complex problems relies heavily on advanced reasoning and planning methodologies. Early approaches utilized standard prompting, but these often fell short on multi-step tasks. The introduction of chain-of-thought prompting marked a significant leap forward, enabling models to generate intermediate reasoning steps before arriving at a final answer (Wei et al., 2022). Building upon this, researchers developed more structured approaches to planning. Plan-and-Solve Prompting improves zero-shot reasoning by explicitly instructing the model to first devise a plan and then execute it step-by-step (Wang et al., 2023). Beyond linear reasoning, planning often requires exploring multiple potential paths and backtracking when a strategy fails. Techniques like tree search extend chain-of-thought by framing problem-solving as a search over possible reasoning steps, allowing the agent to evaluate different paths and select the most promising one. Furthermore, static planning is often insufficient in dynamic environments. Agents must be able to reflect on their past actions and adjust their plans accordingly. Self-reflective mechanisms introduce frameworks where agents receive verbal feedback from their environment and store these reflections in memory, using them to guide future planning and avoid repeating mistakes. These iterative and self-reflective mechanisms are crucial for robust planning in open-ended scenarios.

# 4 Tool Use and Action Generation
While reasoning and planning form the cognitive core of LLM agents, tool use and action generation provide the interface through which agents interact with the external world. LLMs inherently possess a knowledge cutoff and lack the ability to perform precise mathematical computations, query databases, or interact with web services. To overcome these limitations, agents must be equipped with the ability to utilize external tools. ReAct is a foundational framework in this domain, demonstrating that interleaving reasoning traces with action generation allows agents to dynamically decide when to invoke a tool, what parameters to pass, and how to incorporate the tool's output into subsequent reasoning (Yao et al., 2023). The integration of tools can be achieved through in-context learning, where tool descriptions and usage examples are provided in the prompt, or through fine-tuning. Toolformer introduced a self-supervised approach where the LLM itself learns to insert API calls into its generated text, significantly expanding its functional capabilities without requiring extensive human annotation (Schick et al., 2023). For more complex tasks, agents can orchestrate multiple tools. Orchestration systems use an LLM as a controller that parses user requests, selects appropriate models from external repositories, and synthesizes the results. This paradigm highlights the evolution of LLMs from isolated models to central coordinators in complex agentic systems, capable of delegating tasks to specialized sub-agents or external APIs.

# 5 Evaluation Benchmarks and Metrics
As LLM agents become more capable of reasoning, planning, and using tools, evaluating their performance requires moving beyond traditional static datasets. Interactive and grounded benchmarks have emerged to assess how well agents can operate in dynamic environments. WebShop is an early example of such a benchmark, providing a real-world e-commerce environment where agents must navigate web pages, understand product specifications, and complete purchase instructions, thereby testing both language understanding and interactive decision-making (Yao et al., 2022). To evaluate a broader range of agentic capabilities, comprehensive benchmarks like AgentBench have been introduced. AgentBench assesses LLMs as agents across a diverse set of environments, including operating systems, web browsing, database querying, and digital card games, providing a holistic view of an agent's reasoning and acting proficiency (Liu et al., 2023). These benchmarks typically employ metrics such as task completion rate, the efficiency of the action trajectory, and the ability to recover from errors. By placing the agent in a multi-turn interactive loop where the environment state changes based on the agent's actions, these evaluation platforms reveal the true practical utility of planning and reasoning algorithms, exposing failure modes like hallucinated tool calls or infinite action loops that are invisible in static evaluations.

# 6 Challenges and Limitations
Despite the remarkable progress in planning, reasoning, and tool use, LLM agents still face significant challenges that limit their reliability in real-world deployments. One of the most pervasive issues is error compounding. Because multi-step planning and execution are sequential, a single hallucination or reasoning error in an early step can cascade, leading the agent down an irrecoverable path. While self-reflection mechanisms attempt to mitigate this, agents often struggle to identify their own errors, especially when the initial reasoning is flawed but internally consistent (Shinn et al., 2023). Another major limitation is the high computational cost and latency associated with complex planning strategies. Techniques like graph-based search or iterative reflection require multiple forward passes through the LLM, which can be prohibitively slow and expensive for real-time applications (Besta et al., 2023). Furthermore, the robustness of tool use remains a concern. Agents frequently generate syntactically incorrect API calls or fail to handle unexpected return values from external tools, such as error messages or empty results. The reliance on in-context examples for tool use also makes agents brittle; slight changes in prompt formatting or tool descriptions can drastically alter performance. Finally, current evaluation benchmarks, while more interactive than their predecessors, often lack the complexity and unpredictability of true real-world environments, making it difficult to predict how agents will perform outside of curated sandbox settings.

# 7 Conclusion
This survey has explored the rapidly evolving landscape of planning and reasoning in Large Language Model agents, focusing on the core methodologies, tool integration mechanisms, and evaluation benchmarks that define the field. The transition of LLMs from passive text generators to active, autonomous agents represents a fundamental shift in artificial intelligence. We have detailed how foundational reasoning techniques, such as chain-of-thought prompting, have evolved into sophisticated planning frameworks like Tree of Thoughts and Reflexion, enabling agents to navigate complex problem spaces, explore multiple reasoning paths, and learn from their mistakes through self-reflection.

Furthermore, we examined the critical role of tool use in bridging the gap between the internal knowledge of LLMs and the external world. Frameworks like ReAct and Toolformer have demonstrated that synergizing reasoning with action generation allows agents to dynamically interact with APIs, databases, and digital environments, vastly expanding their operational scope. The emergence of orchestration systems like HuggingGPT further illustrates the potential of LLMs as central controllers that coordinate specialized tools to solve multifaceted tasks.

However, the development of robust evaluation methodologies has had to keep pace with these architectural innovations. We reviewed how the field has moved from static question-answering datasets to interactive, grounded benchmarks like WebShop and AgentBench, which rigorously test an agent's ability to perceive, reason, and act within dynamic environments. Despite these advancements, significant challenges remain. Issues such as error compounding, high latency in complex planning, and the brittleness of tool invocation continue to hinder the deployment of LLM agents in high-stakes, real-world scenarios. Addressing these limitations will require continued innovation in both the underlying models and the agentic frameworks that harness them. Ultimately, the synthesis of robust planning, reliable tool use, and rigorous evaluation will be the cornerstone of the next generation of autonomous AI systems.

# 8 Future Directions
As the field of LLM agents matures, several promising research directions are poised to address current limitations and unlock new capabilities. First, the development of more efficient and scalable planning algorithms is critical. Current methods like tree search are computationally expensive; future work should focus on adaptive planning techniques that dynamically allocate compute resources based on task difficulty, potentially drawing inspiration from human cognitive processes that switch between fast, intuitive thinking and slow, deliberate reasoning.

Second, improving the robustness and generalization of tool use remains a key priority. Future agents must move beyond the brittleness of few-shot prompting for tool integration. This could involve the development of more sophisticated self-supervised learning techniques for tool discovery and mastery, as well as the creation of agents that can autonomously learn to use new tools by reading documentation or exploring APIs through trial and error, much like a human user. Additionally, building agents that can gracefully handle tool failures, network latencies, and adversarial manipulations will be essential for real-world deployment.

Third, the community must prioritize the creation of more diverse, open-ended, and long-horizon evaluation benchmarks. Current environments are often constrained in scope and duration. Future benchmarks should simulate complex, multi-agent ecosystems where agents must collaborate, negotiate, and compete over extended periods, testing not only their individual reasoning and planning skills but also their social intelligence and adaptability.

Finally, as LLM agents become more autonomous, the alignment and safety of these systems become increasingly urgent. Future research must develop robust mechanisms for ensuring that an agent's planning and tool use remain aligned with human values and intentions, particularly in scenarios where agents have access to sensitive data or critical infrastructure. Establishing rigorous safety protocols, fail-safes, and interpretable reasoning traces will be paramount in fostering trust and ensuring the responsible deployment of autonomous LLM agents in society.
