# 1 Abstract
The rapid advancement of Large Language Models (LLMs) has catalyzed a paradigm shift from static text generators to dynamic, autonomous agents capable of planning, reasoning, and interacting with external environments. This survey provides a comprehensive overview of the foundational methods, tool use mechanisms, and evaluation benchmarks that define the landscape of LLM-based agents. We begin by examining the core reasoning and planning techniques, such as chain-of-thought prompting and search-based planning algorithms, that enable these models to decompose complex tasks into actionable steps. Subsequently, we explore the critical role of tool use, detailing how LLMs augment their capabilities by interfacing with external APIs, code interpreters, and digital environments to overcome inherent limitations in knowledge and computation. Furthermore, we systematically review the evolving suite of evaluation benchmarks designed to assess agent performance in diverse, interactive settings, ranging from web navigation to multi-domain task execution. By synthesizing these three pillars, we highlight the synergistic relationship between robust planning, effective tool integration, and rigorous evaluation. Finally, we identify current challenges—such as error propagation and hallucination in planning—and outline promising future research directions. This survey aims to serve as a foundational resource for researchers seeking to understand the architectural and methodological underpinnings of LLM agents, fostering the development of more reliable, versatile, and autonomous artificial intelligence systems.

# 2 Introduction
The emergence of Large Language Models (LLMs) represents one of the most significant milestones in artificial intelligence, transforming natural language processing and extending its reach into broader cognitive computing domains. Initially designed for text generation and comprehension, LLMs have evolved beyond their passive roles. Today, they are increasingly deployed as autonomous agents—systems capable of perceiving their environment, making decisions, and taking actions to achieve specific goals. This transition from passive models to active agents hinges on two fundamental cognitive capabilities: planning and reasoning. Planning allows an agent to formulate a sequence of actions to achieve a long-term objective, while reasoning enables it to evaluate the consequences of those actions, adapt to new information, and solve complex logical problems.

The motivation for this survey stems from the explosive growth of research focused on empowering LLMs with agentic capabilities. Traditional LLMs, despite their vast parametric knowledge, are confined by their training data and lack the ability to interact with the real world or perform precise computations. To overcome these limitations, the integration of external tools has become indispensable. Tool use allows LLM agents to offload tasks such as arithmetic calculations, information retrieval, and code execution to specialized external modules, thereby bridging the gap between abstract linguistic reasoning and concrete task execution. However, effectively utilizing tools requires sophisticated planning and reasoning mechanisms to decide which tools to use, when to use them, and how to interpret their outputs.

Despite the rapid proliferation of methods and frameworks, the field lacks a unified understanding of how planning, reasoning, and tool use interconnect within LLM agents. Existing surveys often treat these components in isolation, overlooking the synergistic interplay that defines state-of-the-art agentic systems. Furthermore, the evaluation of these agents remains a fragmented landscape, with diverse benchmarks assessing narrow aspects of agent performance, making cross-comparison and holistic assessment challenging.

This survey aims to bridge these gaps by providing a structured, comprehensive review of planning and reasoning in LLM agents, with a specific focus on methods, tool use, and evaluation benchmarks. We systematically categorize the reasoning paradigms that underpin agent planning, explore the mechanisms by which agents integrate and interact with external tools, and review the benchmarks designed to measure their efficacy. Advanced planning algorithms, such as the Tree of Thoughts framework, allow models to explore multiple reasoning paths and backtrack when necessary (Yao et al., 2023). Similarly, iterative methods like Reflexion endow agents with verbal reinforcement learning, enabling them to reflect on failed attempts and adjust their planning strategies (Shinn et al., 2023). The scope of this paper covers these foundational prompting techniques, advanced search algorithms, tool-learning frameworks, and the interactive environments used for evaluation.

The structure of this survey is as follows: Section 3 delves into the planning and reasoning methods that form the cognitive core of LLM agents. Section 4 examines the mechanisms of tool use and integration, highlighting how external resources expand agent capabilities. Section 5 surveys the evaluation benchmarks that quantify agent performance across various domains. Section 6 discusses the prevailing challenges and limitations in the field. Finally, Sections 7 and 8 offer concluding remarks and outline promising future directions for research in LLM-based agents.

# 3 Planning and Reasoning Methods
The cognitive architecture of an LLM agent is fundamentally anchored in its planning and reasoning capabilities. At the most basic level, these capabilities are elicited through prompting techniques that structure the model's generation process. Chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting emerged as a breakthrough, enabling LLMs to solve complex problems by generating intermediate reasoning steps before arriving at a final answer (Wei et al., 2022). Building upon this, zero-shot and few-shot CoT variants have demonstrated that LLMs can spontaneously decompose tasks when appropriately prompted.

However, standard CoT operates as a linear, left-to-right generation process, which struggles with tasks requiring exploration, backtracking, or lookahead. To address this, structured planning methods have been introduced. Plan-and-Solve prompting improves zero-shot CoT by explicitly asking the model to first generate a comprehensive plan and then execute it step-by-step, significantly reducing calculation and reasoning errors in multi-step tasks (Wang et al., 2023). This explicit decomposition forces the model to allocate cognitive resources to high-level strategy before low-level execution.

Beyond single-pass generation, advanced search algorithms allow agents to navigate vast solution spaces. Tree-based and graph-based planning methods enable the model to explore multiple reasoning paths, evaluate the state of each path, and backtrack when necessary, mimicking human problem-solving search algorithms. These methods transform the planning process from a static prediction into a dynamic, self-correcting cycle, significantly enhancing the agent's robustness in complex, multi-step environments where initial linear trajectories might fail.

# 4 Tool Use and Integration
While planning and reasoning provide the cognitive framework, tool use provides the physical interface through which LLM agents interact with the world. LLMs inherently suffer from limitations such as outdated knowledge, mathematical inaccuracies, and an inability to access real-time information. Tool integration directly addresses these constraints, transforming language models from isolated reasoning engines into versatile agents capable of grounded action.

The ReAct framework was pivotal in demonstrating how reasoning and acting can be synergistically combined. By interleaving the generation of reasoning traces with task-specific actions, ReAct allows agents to dynamically gather information from external environments and update their reasoning accordingly (Yao et al., 2022). This paradigm has become a foundational architecture for modern LLM agents, establishing a tight feedback loop between thinking and doing.

Furthermore, the scope of tool use has expanded from simple API calls to complex orchestration of heterogeneous tools. Toolformer introduced a self-supervised approach where LLMs teach themselves to use tools by injecting API calls into training data, learning when and how to invoke external functionalities like calculators or search engines (Schick et al., 2023). This self-supervised paradigm ensures that tool invocation is seamlessly integrated into the model's natural language generation process, rather than relying solely on hard-coded heuristics. By autonomously learning to leverage external resources, agents can tackle highly diverse, multi-modal tasks that far exceed the native capabilities of a single LLM.

# 5 Evaluation Benchmarks
As LLM agents become increasingly sophisticated, robust evaluation benchmarks are essential to measure progress, identify bottlenecks, and compare different architectures. Evaluating an agent differs fundamentally from evaluating a standard language model; it requires dynamic, interactive environments where the agent's actions alter the state of the world.

Early benchmarks focused on constrained, text-based environments, but the field has rapidly matured to encompass complex, multi-domain assessments. AgentBench provides a comprehensive, multi-dimensional evaluation framework that assesses LLMs as agents across eight distinct environments, including operating systems, web browsing, and digital card games (Liu et al., 2023). This multi-environment approach is crucial for evaluating the generalizability of an agent's reasoning and planning capabilities across diverse real-world applications.

Similarly, as tool use becomes more prevalent, benchmarks specifically targeting API interaction and tool orchestration have emerged. ToolLLM focuses specifically on tool use, evaluating agents on their ability to navigate massive API repositories, understand tool documentation, and execute complex, multi-tool instructions (Qin et al., 2023). These benchmarks collectively shift the evaluation paradigm from static question-answering to dynamic, long-horizon task completion, providing a more accurate reflection of an agent's real-world utility and its capacity to recover from errors during execution.

# 6 Challenges and Limitations
Despite the remarkable progress in planning, reasoning, and tool use, LLM agents still face significant challenges that hinder their deployment in high-stakes, real-world applications. One of the most pervasive issues is error propagation. Because agents operate in a sequential, step-by-step manner, a single hallucination or reasoning error in an early planning stage can compound, leading the agent down an irrecoverable path. While reflexive methods attempt to mitigate this, they are often computationally expensive and struggle to identify the exact step where the failure occurred.

Another critical limitation is the reliability of tool use. The lack of standardized interfaces and the semantic ambiguity in natural language descriptions of tools make robust tool integration a persistent bottleneck, particularly in multi-tool orchestration systems like HuggingGPT (Shen et al., 2023). Agents frequently misinterpret API documentation, generate syntactically incorrect function calls, or fail to parse the returned data correctly.

Furthermore, the computational cost and latency associated with advanced planning methods pose practical limitations. Search-based algorithms like Language Agent Tree Search require multiple LLM inference calls per task, making them prohibitively slow for real-time applications (Zhou et al., 2023). Finally, safety and alignment remain unresolved; an agent with the ability to execute code or modify files poses inherent risks, and current alignment techniques are insufficient to guarantee that LLM agents will not perform harmful actions when encountering unexpected environmental states.

# 7 Conclusion
The evolution of Large Language Models from static text generators to dynamic, autonomous agents marks a transformative era in artificial intelligence. This survey has systematically reviewed the core pillars underpinning this transition: planning and reasoning methods, tool use and integration, and evaluation benchmarks. We have detailed how foundational techniques like chain-of-thought prompting have evolved into sophisticated search algorithms and reflexive reasoning frameworks, enabling agents to decompose complex tasks and learn from their own errors. Furthermore, we have highlighted the indispensable role of tool use, demonstrating how frameworks like ReAct and Toolformer bridge the gap between abstract linguistic reasoning and concrete, grounded action. The integration of external tools not only overcomes the inherent limitations of LLMs—such as mathematical inaccuracies and outdated knowledge—but also exponentially expands their operational domain.

Equally important is the parallel evolution of evaluation methodologies. We have observed a necessary shift from static, isolated question-answering datasets to dynamic, interactive benchmarks like AgentBench and ToolLLM. These modern environments rigorously test an agent's ability to navigate complex, multi-step tasks, adapt to environmental feedback, and orchestrate multiple tools effectively. By mapping the interdependencies between reasoning, tool use, and evaluation, it becomes evident that progress in this field is highly synergistic; advanced planning methods are prerequisite for complex tool orchestration, and sophisticated benchmarks are required to measure these new capabilities.

However, as discussed in the challenges section, the current landscape is not without its flaws. Error propagation, tool unreliability, computational overhead, and safety concerns remain significant hurdles. Overcoming these limitations will require more than just scaling model parameters; it will demand novel architectural designs, more robust error-correction mechanisms, and stringent safety protocols. As the field continues to advance, the vision of truly autonomous, reliable, and general-purpose LLM agents is becoming increasingly tangible, promising profound implications for how we interact with and leverage artificial intelligence in complex digital and physical environments.

# 8 Future Directions
Looking ahead, the field of LLM agents is poised for rapid advancements across several critical frontiers. First, there is a pressing need for more robust and fault-tolerant planning mechanisms. Future research should focus on developing hierarchical planning architectures that can dynamically adjust their level of abstraction, allowing agents to seamlessly switch between high-level strategic planning and low-level tactical execution. Additionally, integrating formal verification methods into the planning process could help ensure that generated action sequences adhere to safety constraints before execution, mitigating the risks of irreversible actions.

Second, the paradigm of tool use must evolve towards autonomous tool creation and modification. Currently, agents are limited to interacting with pre-defined, human-crafted APIs. A promising direction is enabling agents to write their own scripts, compose new tools from existing ones, and adapt their toolset based on task requirements. This would significantly enhance an agent's adaptability in open-ended environments where pre-defined tools are insufficient or poorly documented.

Third, multi-agent collaboration represents a fertile ground for future exploration. Complex real-world problems often require diverse expertise and parallel task execution. Developing frameworks where multiple LLM agents, each with specialized tools and personas, can communicate, negotiate, and coordinate their actions will be crucial for scaling agent systems to enterprise-level applications.

Fourth, the evaluation landscape must mature to encompass long-horizon, open-ended tasks. Current benchmarks are often constrained by short trajectories and deterministic success metrics. Future benchmarks should evaluate agents on their ability to maintain coherence and safety over extended interactions, handle ambiguous user intents, and operate in environments where success is subjective or multi-faceted. Finally, addressing the safety and alignment of autonomous agents must remain a paramount priority. Research into red-teaming, constrained decoding, and sandboxed execution environments will be essential to mitigate the risks associated with granting LLMs the autonomy to execute real-world actions.
