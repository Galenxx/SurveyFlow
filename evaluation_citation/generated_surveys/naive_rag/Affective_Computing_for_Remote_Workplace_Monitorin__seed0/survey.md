# 1 Abstract

The integration of affective computing into workplace technologies has given rise to Emotional AI, a new generation of biometric devices capable of sensing, reading, monitoring, and evaluating workers' internal emotional states, marking a significant departure from earlier forms of biometric surveillance that focused primarily on physical presence and behavior. This survey systematically examines the emerging landscape of Emotional AI in occupational settings, synthesizing current research on its technological foundations, deployment practices, and the socio-demographic factors shaping worker attitudes toward emotional monitoring. Our analysis reveals that Emotional AI represents a paradigm shift in workplace surveillance, introducing unprecedented capabilities for computational assessment of affective states while raising profound ethical concerns regarding privacy, consent, and the boundaries of legitimate organizational oversight. The evidence further demonstrates significant cross-cultural variability in acceptance of these technologies, with attitudes strongly mediated by demographic factors, cultural context, and the perceived legitimacy of monitoring practices. These findings highlight critical gaps in existing regulatory frameworks and underscore the urgent need for interdisciplinary research addressing the ethical boundaries of emotional surveillance in the workplace.

# 2 Introduction

The rapid integration of artificial intelligence into everyday work environments has fundamentally reshaped how organizations approach productivity, collaboration, and employee management. Among the most transformative developments is the emergence of affective computing, a multidisciplinary field that enables machines to recognize, interpret, and respond to human emotional states. Originally conceived to enhance human-computer interaction, affective computing has progressively expanded its reach into domains such as healthcare, education, and marketing. In recent years, however, its application has extended into workplace monitoring, where biometric devices and algorithmic systems are increasingly deployed to sense and evaluate the emotional conditions of employees. This shift marks a departure from traditional forms of workplace surveillance, which historically focused on tracking physical presence, task completion, or digital activity. Instead, a new generation of technologies, often marketed under the term Emotional AI, now promises to provide real-time insights into workers’ affective states, raising profound questions about the boundaries of organizational oversight and the nature of employee autonomy.

The deployment of affective computing for remote workplace monitoring introduces a series of interconnected challenges that span technical, ethical, and social dimensions. On the technical front, accurately inferring emotional states from physiological and behavioral signals remains a formidable task, given the complexity and context-dependence of human affect. Sensor modalities such as facial expression analysis, voice tone assessment, heart rate variability, and electrodermal activity each carry inherent limitations and are susceptible to misinterpretation when divorced from situational context. Beyond sensing and inference, the ethical implications of emotional surveillance in the workplace constitute a pressing concern. The normalization of continuous affective monitoring risks eroding trust between employers and employees, intensifying power asymmetries, and enabling new forms of psychological manipulation. Furthermore, attitudes toward such technologies are not uniform across populations; socio-demographic factors, including gender, age, cultural background, and occupational status, significantly shape individuals’ perceptions of and resistance to emotional monitoring. These divergences highlight the need for a nuanced understanding of how different worker populations experience and respond to the intrusion of affective computing into their professional lives.

This survey paper makes two primary contributions to the growing discourse on affective computing in occupational contexts. First, it provides a comprehensive and structured overview of the sensing and inference techniques that underpin contemporary Emotional AI systems used for remote workplace monitoring. By systematically examining the range of sensor technologies, from contact-based wearables to contactless computer vision methods, and the machine learning models employed to classify affective states, the paper offers a technical foundation for understanding both the capabilities and the limitations of these systems. The analysis extends to the challenges of data quality, contextual sensitivity, and the generalizability of affective models across diverse populations and work settings, thereby equipping researchers and practitioners with a critical perspective on the state of the art.

Second, the survey advances the conversation by foregrounding the ethical and socio-demographic dimensions that are often marginalized in purely technical treatments of affective computing. It synthesizes existing empirical evidence on worker attitudes toward emotional monitoring, paying particular attention to how factors such as cultural norms, power dynamics, and individual privacy expectations mediate acceptance and resistance. In doing so, the paper bridges the gap between engineering-oriented research and the social sciences, advocating for a human-centered approach that respects worker dignity and agency. The discussion also identifies pressing regulatory and design challenges, emphasizing the need for transparent consent mechanisms, algorithmic accountability, and culturally adaptive deployment strategies. By integrating technical analysis with ethical scrutiny, the survey aims to inform the development of responsible frameworks that can guide the future evolution of affective workplace technologies.

The primary aim of this survey is to provide a holistic examination of affective computing as it intersects with remote workplace monitoring, moving beyond isolated technical descriptions to encompass the broader implications of its adoption. The paper is structured to first introduce the foundational concepts of affective computing and Emotional AI, followed by a detailed review of sensing modalities and inference algorithms. Subsequent sections critically assess the ethical landscape, including privacy, consent, and the risk of discriminatory outcomes, before delving into the socio-demographic factors that shape worker perceptions. The survey concludes by outlining open research questions and proposing directions for future work that prioritize ethical integrity alongside technological innovation. Through this structure, the paper seeks to serve as a resource for researchers, policymakers, and organizational leaders navigating the complex terrain of emotion-sensing technologies in the workplace.

# 7 Future Directions

Current workplace monitoring technologies have advanced beyond tracking physical presence to sensing and evaluating workers’ emotional states through Emotional AI, yet the research community has only begun to examine the implications of this shift. A critical gap remains in understanding how these affective computing systems are perceived across different socio-demographic groups and cultural contexts. Moreover, the ethical boundaries governing emotional surveillance in occupational settings remain poorly defined, leaving both employers and employees without clear guidance on acceptable practices. The long-term psychological and social consequences of continuous emotional monitoring on worker well-being, autonomy, and trust have not been systematically investigated. Additionally, existing studies have not adequately addressed how organizational power dynamics may be reshaped when employers gain access to the internal affective states of their workforce.

Future research should prioritize cross-cultural investigations into the socio-demographic determinants of attitudes toward workplace Emotional AI. Comparative studies spanning different regulatory environments, labor traditions, and cultural norms around emotional expression would illuminate the conditions under which such monitoring is accepted or resisted. Researchers should also develop and empirically test ethical frameworks specifically tailored to emotional surveillance, moving beyond general privacy principles to address the unique intrusiveness of technologies that claim to read internal states. Longitudinal studies are needed to assess the cumulative effects of emotional monitoring on worker mental health, job satisfaction, and organizational citizenship behaviors over extended periods. Furthermore, scholars should examine the accuracy and potential biases embedded in affective computing systems, particularly whether certain demographic groups face disproportionate risks of misclassification or unfair treatment based on automated emotional assessments.

The proposed research directions carry significant implications for policy development, organizational practice, and the future of worker rights. A deeper understanding of cross-cultural variability in attitudes toward Emotional AI can inform the design of context-sensitive regulations that respect diverse values while protecting workers from intrusive surveillance. Robust ethical frameworks grounded in empirical evidence would provide much-needed guidance for technology developers, employers, and policymakers navigating this rapidly evolving landscape. Longitudinal findings on worker well-being could establish whether the purported productivity benefits of emotional monitoring come at unacceptable human costs, potentially reshaping cost-benefit analyses that currently favor deployment. Finally, exposing biases in affective computing systems would advance the broader goal of ensuring that workplace technologies do not perpetuate or amplify existing inequalities, contributing to a more just and humane future of work.

# 8 Conclusion

This survey has examined the rapid proliferation of Emotional AI technologies in the workplace, charting their evolution from foundational affective computing research to a new generation of biometric devices capable of sensing, reading, monitoring, and evaluating workers’ internal affective states. The central finding of this review is the identification of a paradigmatic shift in organizational surveillance: unlike earlier biometric systems that focused on physical presence, location, or basic physiological data, contemporary Emotional AI targets the subjective and transient domain of human emotion. This transition carries profound implications, as the computational assessment of emotions moves monitoring from the objective to the interpretive, raising fundamental questions about accuracy, consent, and the very definition of an emotional state. The literature reveals a landscape characterized by a tension between the promise of optimized well-being and productivity and the peril of unprecedented invasive oversight, with worker attitudes shaped by a complex interplay of socio-demographic factors and cultural contexts.

The significance of this survey lies in its synthesis of a fragmented and rapidly evolving field, bridging technical capabilities with their critical social and ethical ramifications. By consolidating research on the mechanisms, applications, and reception of workplace Emotional AI, this paper provides a foundational framework for understanding a technology that fundamentally alters the employer-employee relationship. It moves beyond a purely technical cataloguing to foreground the human consequences, highlighting how these systems risk codifying biased interpretations of emotional expression, eroding psychological autonomy, and creating new vectors for discrimination and control. The survey’s analysis of the socio-demographic determinants of acceptance underscores that the impact of Emotional AI is not uniform but is refracted through cultural norms, power dynamics, and individual vulnerabilities, making it a critical area of study for organizational psychology, business ethics, and technology policy.

In closing, the trajectory of workplace Emotional AI demands immediate and sustained interdisciplinary attention. The current lack of robust governance frameworks leaves a vacuum in which commercial interests may outpace the protection of fundamental worker rights. We call upon researchers, policymakers, and industry leaders to move beyond the question of technical feasibility and confront the normative challenge of establishing ethical boundaries for emotional surveillance. Future work must prioritize the development of transparent, auditable systems and legally enforceable standards for consent that account for the inherent power asymmetry in employment. The ultimate goal should not be to perfect the sensing of emotion, but to foster a work environment where technology serves human dignity, and where the inner emotional life of a worker remains a sanctuary, not a data stream to be mined.
