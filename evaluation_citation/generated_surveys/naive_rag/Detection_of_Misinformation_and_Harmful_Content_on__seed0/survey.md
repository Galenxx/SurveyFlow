# 1 Abstract

The proliferation of misinformation in short-form video platforms has introduced urgent challenges for automated fact-checking, as identifying content that warrants verification now requires the integration of visual, auditory, and textual signals within highly compressed, multimodal formats. Despite notable advances in claim detection research, a substantial gap persists between theoretical models and practical tools that can operate effectively in real-world, high-throughput fact-checking environments. This survey examines recent developments in checkworthiness detection for short-form videos, with a particular focus on approaches that prioritize deployability and interpretability. We review methods that move beyond unimodal text analysis to address the unique complexities of platforms such as TikTok, where multilingual and multimodal content demands robust, explainable system outputs tailored to the needs of human fact-checkers. Our analysis reveals a foundational shift in the field toward systems that augment rather than replace human judgment, emphasizing output alignment with practitioner workflows and the capacity to function under time-sensitive conditions. By synthesizing current strategies and identifying persistent barriers to deployment, this survey provides a structured overview of the state of the art and outlines critical directions for future research. The findings underscore that the practical utility of automated checkworthiness detection increasingly depends on designing interpretable, deployment-ready tools that bridge the enduring divide between research innovation and operational reality.

# 2 Introduction

The rapid growth of short-form video platforms has fundamentally reshaped how information is created, consumed, and disseminated across global audiences. Platforms such as TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts now serve as primary information sources for millions of users, delivering content that blends visual imagery, spoken language, on-screen text, and music into compact, highly engaging formats. This multimodal nature, combined with algorithmic amplification and rapid content turnover, has created an environment where misinformation and harmful content can spread with unprecedented speed and reach. Unlike traditional text-based media, short-form videos present unique challenges for content moderation and fact-checking, as deceptive claims may be embedded across multiple modalities simultaneously, making detection inherently more complex than analyzing textual assertions alone. The sheer volume of content uploaded daily further compounds this challenge, rendering manual review processes insufficient and underscoring the urgent need for automated detection systems capable of operating at scale.

Despite growing research interest in automated misinformation detection, several critical problems remain inadequately addressed in the context of short-form video platforms. Current approaches often prioritize model accuracy in controlled experimental settings while neglecting the practical requirements of real-world deployment, including interpretability, processing speed, and integration with human fact-checking workflows. A substantial gap persists between research prototypes and tools that can function reliably in high-throughput environments where fact-checkers must make rapid, defensible decisions about content veracity. Furthermore, the multimodal nature of short-form videos introduces technical challenges that extend beyond those encountered in text-only or image-only analysis, requiring systems that can effectively fuse and reason across visual, auditory, and textual signals. The multilingual and cross-cultural dimensions of these platforms add another layer of complexity, as detection methods developed for one language or cultural context often fail to generalize to others. These interconnected problems highlight the need for a comprehensive examination of the current state of research and the identification of pathways toward more robust, deployable solutions.

This survey makes several contributions to the field of misinformation and harmful content detection on short-form video platforms. First, it provides a systematic and comprehensive review of existing methods, categorizing them according to their underlying technical approaches and the modalities they address. By examining techniques ranging from unimodal analysis of individual signal types to advanced multimodal fusion strategies, the survey offers researchers a structured understanding of the methodological landscape and the relative strengths and limitations of different approaches. This categorization reveals both the progress made in recent years and the persistent challenges that continue to limit the practical utility of current systems, particularly in terms of cross-modal reasoning and temporal dynamics understanding.

Second, this survey consolidates and critically evaluates the datasets and evaluation frameworks that underpin research in this domain. The availability and quality of benchmark datasets fundamentally shape the trajectory of research, yet the field currently lacks standardized resources that adequately capture the diversity and complexity of real-world short-form video content. By cataloging existing datasets, identifying their limitations in terms of scale, language coverage, and multimodal annotation, and discussing the metrics used to assess system performance, this survey provides a foundation for more rigorous and reproducible research. Additionally, the survey highlights the critical importance of interpretability and human-centered design in detection systems, examining how current tools align with the needs of human fact-checkers and content moderators who operate under significant time constraints. This focus on deployment readiness reflects a necessary evolution in the field from purely academic inquiry toward solutions that can meaningfully support human decision-making in operational settings.

The aim of this survey is to provide researchers and practitioners with a comprehensive resource that maps the current state of misinformation and harmful content detection on short-form video platforms while identifying promising directions for future work. The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. We begin by defining the scope of the problem and establishing key terminology, followed by a detailed taxonomy of detection methods organized by modality and fusion strategy. We then present a thorough review of available datasets and evaluation practices, critically examining their suitability for advancing the field. Subsequent sections address the challenges of interpretability, multilingual detection, and real-world deployment, drawing on insights from both academic research and practical fact-checking contexts. The survey concludes with a discussion of open problems and recommendations for future research directions that prioritize the development of robust, interpretable, and deployable tools capable of operating effectively in the dynamic information ecosystem of short-form video platforms.

# 7 Future Directions

Current systems for detecting checkworthy content in short-form videos remain largely confined to research prototypes, with a persistent gap between laboratory performance and real-world utility. The primary limitations include insufficient multimodal integration, where visual, auditory, and textual signals are often processed in isolation rather than as a unified whole, leading to missed contextual cues that human fact-checkers naturally perceive. Additionally, existing tools rarely prioritize interpretability, producing opaque outputs that fail to explain why a particular video warrants verification, which undermines trust and slows down human review processes. The throughput demands of modern fact-checking organizations further expose the inadequacy of current solutions, as many systems lack the efficiency and scalability required for continuous monitoring of rapidly evolving social media platforms. Multilingual and cross-cultural adaptability also remains underdeveloped, limiting the applicability of these tools beyond a narrow set of languages and content norms.

Future research should prioritize the development of truly integrated multimodal architectures that jointly reason over visual scenes, spoken language, on-screen text, and non-verbal audio cues to assess checkworthiness holistically. Such systems must move beyond simple feature concatenation toward deeper fusion mechanisms capable of capturing the interplay between modalities, such as detecting mismatches between spoken claims and visual evidence that often signal misleading content. Equally important is the design of inherently interpretable models that generate human-readable justifications for their assessments, enabling fact-checkers to quickly understand and validate system recommendations without extensive technical expertise. These explanations should highlight specific segments, frames, or utterances that triggered the checkworthiness determination, effectively guiding human attention to the most salient evidence. Another critical direction involves engineering systems for high-throughput deployment, which requires optimizing inference speed, implementing efficient content ingestion pipelines, and designing user interfaces that seamlessly integrate into existing fact-checking workflows rather than imposing additional cognitive burdens. Cross-lingual transfer learning and culturally adaptive models also warrant focused investigation, as checkworthiness cues can vary significantly across linguistic and social contexts, and systems trained predominantly on English-language content may fail to capture region-specific misinformation patterns.

Advancing these research directions would fundamentally transform the capacity of fact-checking organizations to cope with the scale and sophistication of modern misinformation. Interpretable, high-throughput tools would dramatically reduce the time human fact-checkers spend on initial triage, allowing them to allocate more resources to in-depth investigation and corrective content production. The ability to process multilingual short-form video at scale would extend the protective reach of fact-checking efforts to underserved language communities that currently lack adequate verification infrastructure. Moreover, by producing transparent reasoning alongside automated assessments, these systems would foster greater trust between technology providers, fact-checking professionals, and the broader public, addressing concerns about opaque algorithmic decision-making in content moderation. Ultimately, closing the gap between research prototypes and deployable tools would strengthen the overall information ecosystem, enabling more timely and effective responses to viral falsehoods before they achieve widespread reach.

# 8 Conclusion

In conclusion, this survey has examined the evolving landscape of checkworthiness detection, tracing its expansion from predominantly text-based claim identification to the complex domain of multimodal short-form videos. The central finding is that while significant progress has been made in developing automated methods capable of processing visual, auditory, and textual signals simultaneously, a persistent gap remains between the capabilities of research prototypes and the practical demands of real-world fact-checking workflows. The work of Vatndal and Setty serves as a pivotal case study, demonstrating that effective deployment hinges not solely on improving the accuracy of multimodal understanding, but equally on designing system outputs that are interpretable and aligned with the cognitive and operational needs of human fact-checkers. This shift in focus—from theoretical model performance to the utility of tools in high-throughput, time-sensitive environments—represents a foundational reorientation in the field, where the ultimate measure of success is the capacity to augment rather than replace human judgment. The survey further highlights that the unique characteristics of platforms like TikTok, including the fusion of ephemeral trends, multilingual content, and layered audiovisual narratives, demand specialized approaches that go beyond conventional claim detection paradigms.

The significance of this survey lies in its synthesis of a critical yet underexplored nexus between multimodal machine learning and practical fact-checking. By foregrounding the imperative for deployability and interpretability, it provides a structured understanding of the current state-of-the-art and the bottlenecks that prevent laboratory advances from translating into societal impact. This work serves as a roadmap for researchers and practitioners alike, clarifying that future innovations must be evaluated not only through traditional metrics of precision and recall but also through their integration into existing journalistic routines and their ability to foster trust among users. The analysis underscores that the development of systems like ShortCheck is not merely a technical achievement but a necessary response to the urgent information integrity challenges posed by short-form video platforms, which have become primary vectors for both information consumption and misinformation dissemination globally.

Moving forward, the field must embrace a human-centered design ethos that places fact-checkers at the core of the development lifecycle. Researchers are called upon to forge deeper collaborations with newsrooms and verification organizations to co-design tools that are both robust and responsive to the fluid dynamics of online misinformation. The path ahead requires a concerted effort to create systems that are transparent in their reasoning, adaptable to emerging formats and languages, and capable of operating at the speed and scale demanded by modern social media. Only through such a pragmatic and collaborative approach can the promise of automated checkworthiness detection be fully realized, transforming it from a promising academic pursuit into an indispensable pillar of the global fact-checking infrastructure.
