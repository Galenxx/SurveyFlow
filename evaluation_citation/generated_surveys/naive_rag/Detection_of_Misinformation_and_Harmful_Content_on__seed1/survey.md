# 1 Abstract

The rapid growth of short-form video platforms has amplified the spread of misinformation, necessitating automated tools for detecting checkworthy content at scale. This paper surveys the emerging field of checkworthiness detection in short-form video, with a particular focus on bridging the persistent gap between research prototypes and the practical demands of real-world fact-checking workflows. We examine recent advances in multimodal understanding and system design, highlighting the critical importance of developing interpretable, deployable tools that align with the needs of human fact-checkers operating in high-throughput, multilingual environments. Our analysis reveals that current systems must balance technical sophistication with operational viability, emphasizing human-in-the-loop considerations and output formats tailored to verification workflows. The survey identifies key challenges and open problems in creating systems that are both methodologically robust and practically useful for fact-checking organizations.

# 2 Introduction

The rapid proliferation of short-form video platforms such as TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts has fundamentally reshaped the digital information landscape, creating new avenues for content creation, social interaction, and information dissemination. These platforms prioritize brevity, visual engagement, and algorithmic curation, enabling content to achieve viral reach at unprecedented speed and scale. However, the very characteristics that make short-form video so compelling also render it a uniquely potent vector for the spread of misinformation and harmful content. The multimodal nature of this medium, which seamlessly blends dynamic visuals, spoken audio, on-screen text, and music, presents a complex information environment where false or misleading claims can be conveyed implicitly through combinations of modalities that are difficult to parse automatically. Unlike text-based platforms where natural language processing techniques have achieved considerable maturity, short-form video demands a holistic understanding that integrates auditory, visual, and textual signals. The scale of content generation, coupled with the platform-specific vernaculars, evolving trends, and cross-lingual reach, further complicates the challenge, making the development of robust detection mechanisms an urgent and multifaceted research priority.

Despite growing academic and industrial interest, the field faces several critical and interconnected problems that hinder effective detection of misinformation and harmful content on short-form video platforms. A primary obstacle is the substantial gap between research prototypes and the practical requirements of real-world deployment. Many existing detection models are developed and evaluated in controlled laboratory settings, yet they lack the interpretability, efficiency, and robustness needed for high-throughput fact-checking environments where human moderators must make rapid, consequential decisions. Furthermore, the multimodal fusion problem remains inadequately addressed; current approaches often struggle to model the nuanced interplay between modalities, failing to capture how misinformation can emerge from the incongruence between what is shown and what is said. The scarcity of large-scale, high-quality, and publicly available datasets that accurately reflect the diversity of real-world misleading content, including its linguistic, cultural, and platform-specific variations, severely limits the development and benchmarking of generalizable models. Finally, the dynamic and adversarial nature of the problem, where content creators continuously adapt their tactics to evade detection, demands adaptive systems that can evolve in tandem with emerging forms of manipulation, a capability that remains largely aspirational.

This survey paper makes several contributions that collectively advance the understanding and development of detection systems for misinformation and harmful content on short-form video platforms. First, it provides a comprehensive and structured taxonomy of the methods employed in this domain, systematically categorizing approaches based on their modality focus, including unimodal techniques for visual, audio, and textual analysis, as well as early, intermediate, and late fusion strategies for multimodal integration. By delineating the architectural paradigms and learning frameworks that underpin state-of-the-art models, the survey offers researchers a clear conceptual map of the technical landscape, highlighting the evolution from traditional feature engineering to advanced deep learning and transformer-based architectures. This taxonomy not only organizes existing work but also reveals underexplored methodological intersections and opportunities for cross-pollination between research communities working on different facets of the problem.

Second, the survey delivers an in-depth analysis of the datasets and evaluation protocols that form the backbone of empirical research in this area. It catalogs available resources, detailing their composition, scale, annotation methodologies, and limitations, while critically examining the metrics and benchmarks used to assess model performance. A particular emphasis is placed on the practical challenges of dataset construction, including the difficulties of capturing real-world distribution shifts, ensuring ecological validity, and addressing the ethical considerations inherent in curating harmful content. By foregrounding the gap between research-oriented benchmarks and the operational needs of human fact-checkers, the survey underscores the importance of designing systems with interpretability and human-in-the-loop integration as first-order requirements, rather than afterthoughts, thereby bridging the divide between technical innovation and practical utility.

The aim of this survey is to provide a structured and critical overview of the current state of research on detecting misinformation and harmful content on short-form video platforms, serving as a foundational resource for researchers, practitioners, and platform developers. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 formalizes the problem definition and delineates the scope of the survey, establishing key terminology and the boundaries of the review. Section 3 presents the taxonomy of detection methods, systematically examining unimodal and multimodal approaches. Section 4 surveys the available datasets and discusses the challenges associated with their creation and use. Section 5 analyzes evaluation methodologies and the critical gap between research and real-world deployment. Section 6 identifies open challenges and outlines promising directions for future research, and Section 7 concludes the paper with a summary of key insights.

# 7 Future Directions

The current landscape of checkworthy content detection in short-form video is marked by a persistent divide between research advancements and the operational realities of fact-checking organizations. Existing systems, while demonstrating technical promise, often lack the interpretability and throughput efficiency required for real-world deployment. The multimodal nature of short-form video—combining visual, auditory, and textual cues in rapidly evolving formats—presents unresolved challenges in achieving robust understanding across languages and cultural contexts. Furthermore, the design of automated tools has not yet fully integrated the workflows and decision-making processes of human fact-checkers, leaving a gap in producing outputs that are both actionable and trustworthy in high-pressure, high-volume environments.

Future research should prioritize the co-design of checkworthiness detection systems with fact-checking practitioners to ensure that technical capabilities translate into practical utility. This involves developing interpretable models that can explain their reasoning in ways that align with journalistic standards, enabling fact-checkers to quickly assess and validate system suggestions. Efforts should also focus on advancing multimodal fusion techniques that can dynamically weigh evidence from speech, text overlays, and visual content, particularly for multilingual and code-switched videos that dominate global platforms. Another critical direction is the creation of lightweight, efficient architectures capable of operating at scale without sacrificing accuracy, thereby supporting real-time or near-real-time triage of content. Additionally, research must explore adaptive systems that can evolve alongside emerging misinformation narratives and platform-specific trends, reducing the time lag between new threats and detection capability. Finally, rigorous field studies and longitudinal evaluations in partnership with fact-checking organizations will be essential to measure the true impact of these tools on verification speed, accuracy, and resource allocation.

Advancing these research directions holds the potential to fundamentally reshape the misinformation detection pipeline for short-form video. By delivering tools that are both technically sophisticated and operationally viable, the field can empower fact-checkers to manage the overwhelming volume of content more effectively, focusing human expertise on nuanced verification tasks while automating the initial triage. This integration can accelerate the speed at which false claims are identified and countered, ultimately reducing the window of viral spread. Moreover, interpretable and human-aligned systems can enhance trust in automated assistance, fostering greater adoption within newsrooms and civil society organizations. The broader societal impact lies in strengthening the resilience of information ecosystems against visual disinformation, protecting public discourse during critical events such as elections and public health crises.

# 8 Conclusion

The detection of checkworthy content in short-form video remains a nascent and rapidly evolving research area, and this survey has highlighted the central challenges and emerging directions shaping the field. A primary finding is the persistent and substantial gap between research prototypes and the practical demands of real-world fact-checking workflows. Current systems, while advancing in multimodal understanding, often fall short of providing the interpretable and deployable tools that human fact-checkers require in high-throughput environments. The review underscores that technical sophistication alone is insufficient; effective solutions must be designed with operational constraints at their core, ensuring that system outputs align directly with the needs of practitioners. Furthermore, the scarcity of large-scale, multilingual, and multimodal datasets tailored to short-form video platforms continues to hinder progress, limiting the development of models that can robustly generalize across diverse cultural and linguistic contexts. The field is thus characterized by a critical need for holistic approaches that integrate model development with human-in-the-loop considerations from the outset.

The significance of this survey lies in its systematic articulation of the disconnect between academic innovation and practical utility in the domain of automated checkworthiness detection for short-form video. By synthesizing the current landscape, this work provides a clear diagnostic of why many promising research directions have yet to translate into tools that fact-checking organizations can reliably adopt. It foregrounds the often-underestimated importance of interpretability, operational speed, and workflow integration as first-order design requirements rather than afterthoughts. In doing so, the survey serves as a roadmap for researchers and practitioners alike, identifying the specific technical and socio-technical bottlenecks that must be addressed to move beyond isolated demonstrations. The emphasis on bridging the research-practice divide contributes a necessary corrective to a field that risks optimizing for benchmark performance at the expense of real-world impact, thereby refocusing attention on the ultimate goal of supporting human verifiers in combating misinformation at scale.

Looking ahead, the path forward demands a concerted and collaborative effort to close the gap between what is computationally possible and what is operationally necessary. Researchers must prioritize the development of interpretable, multilingual, and platform-aware systems that are co-designed with fact-checking professionals from the earliest stages. This requires not only advances in multimodal fusion and cross-lingual transfer but also a deeper engagement with the organizational realities of verification work, including latency constraints, user interface design, and the need for explainable confidence scores. Funding bodies, technology platforms, and academic institutions should incentivize the creation of shared benchmarks and datasets that reflect the messy, context-dependent nature of real-world misinformation rather than sanitized laboratory conditions. Ultimately, the fight against misinformation in short-form video will not be won by algorithms alone but by building socio-technical systems that meaningfully augment human judgment. The research community must now translate the insights mapped in this survey into actionable, deployable tools that can keep pace with the scale and speed of today’s information ecosystem.
