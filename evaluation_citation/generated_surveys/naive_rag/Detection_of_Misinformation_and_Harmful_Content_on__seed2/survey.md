# 1 Abstract

The rapid proliferation of short-form video platforms has introduced novel challenges for automated fact-checking, as the multimodal and fast-paced nature of this medium renders traditional text-based checkworthiness detection methods insufficient. This survey examines the emerging landscape of checkworthy content detection in short-form videos, with a particular focus on the intersection of technical innovation and practical deployment. We systematically review recent advances in multimodal understanding that jointly process visual, auditory, and textual streams, while critically assessing the extent to which these systems produce outputs aligned with the operational workflows of human fact-checkers in high-throughput environments. Our analysis reveals a persistent and substantial gap between research prototypes and deployable, interpretable tools, identifying the dual requirement of technical sophistication and operational usability as a foundational tension that constrains progress in the field. We further highlight the specific demands of multilingual, short-form content and the limitations of current demonstration systems in addressing real-world fact-checking pipelines. This survey underscores the urgent need for system designs that prioritize both multimodal reasoning capabilities and human-centered interpretability to advance automated checkworthiness detection toward practical, real-world application.

# 2 Introduction

The rapid proliferation of short-form video platforms has fundamentally transformed the digital information ecosystem, creating new vectors for both legitimate content creation and the spread of misinformation. Platforms such as TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts now serve as primary information sources for billions of users worldwide, with their algorithmic curation and immersive formats enabling unprecedented levels of engagement. Unlike traditional text-based social media, short-form video content combines visual, auditory, and textual modalities in tightly integrated packages that are specifically designed to capture attention and evoke emotional responses. This multimodal nature, coupled with the brevity and high-velocity consumption patterns characteristic of these platforms, presents unique challenges for content moderation and fact-checking efforts. The virality mechanics embedded in recommendation algorithms can rapidly amplify misleading or harmful content before human moderators have the opportunity to intervene, while the format itself makes deceptive content particularly persuasive and difficult to scrutinize. As these platforms continue to grow in cultural and political significance, the need for robust, scalable detection mechanisms has become increasingly urgent, drawing attention from researchers across natural language processing, computer vision, and human-computer interaction communities.

The detection of misinformation and harmful content on short-form video platforms confronts several interconnected problems that distinguish this domain from more established text-focused research. First, the multimodal nature of the content demands systems capable of jointly reasoning over visual, auditory, and textual streams, where misleading information may manifest in any single modality or emerge from the interaction between them. A video may feature innocuous visuals paired with deceptive audio narration, or manipulative editing techniques that create false contextual associations. Second, the sheer volume and velocity of content uploads necessitate approaches that can operate at scale while maintaining acceptable accuracy, creating tension between computational efficiency and analytical depth. Third, the linguistic and cultural diversity of global platforms introduces additional complexity, as detection systems must contend with code-switching, regional dialects, and culture-specific references that may obscure harmful intent or checkworthy claims. Perhaps most critically, a substantial gap persists between research advances and the availability of deployable, interpretable tools suitable for real-world moderation workflows. Human fact-checkers operating in high-throughput environments require system outputs that not only flag potentially problematic content but also provide actionable, transparent justifications that can inform downstream decisions. This dual requirement for technical sophistication and operational usability represents a foundational challenge that current systems are only beginning to address.

This survey makes several contributions to the understanding and advancement of misinformation detection on short-form video platforms. First, it provides a comprehensive taxonomy of detection methods, systematically categorizing approaches based on their treatment of modality—ranging from unimodal techniques that analyze individual streams in isolation to fully integrated multimodal architectures that jointly model visual, auditory, and textual information. This taxonomy encompasses both classical machine learning pipelines and contemporary deep learning frameworks, including transformer-based architectures, multimodal fusion strategies, and large vision-language models that have recently demonstrated promising capabilities in cross-modal reasoning tasks. The survey further examines how these methods address the specific affordances and constraints of short-form video, such as limited temporal duration, variable production quality, and platform-specific stylistic conventions that influence both content creation and detection strategies.

Second, this survey consolidates and critically evaluates the datasets and benchmark resources available for research in this domain, highlighting their characteristics, limitations, and suitability for different detection tasks. The analysis reveals significant gaps in dataset coverage, particularly regarding linguistic diversity, cultural representation, and the inclusion of real-world platform data that reflects authentic distribution patterns of misinformation. The survey also examines evaluation protocols and metrics employed across studies, identifying inconsistencies that complicate cross-method comparisons and proposing standardized frameworks for future benchmarking efforts. By synthesizing insights from both the methods and datasets dimensions, this work identifies promising research directions, including the development of more interpretable system outputs aligned with fact-checker workflows, approaches for handling evolving misinformation narratives, and techniques for robust cross-platform generalization.

The aim of this survey is to provide researchers and practitioners with a structured overview of the current landscape in short-form video misinformation detection, while also serving as a roadmap for future work that bridges the persistent gap between academic research and operational deployment. The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 establishes foundational concepts and formally defines the problem space, including the characteristics of short-form video platforms and the taxonomy of misinformation types encountered therein. Section 3 presents a detailed examination of detection methods, organized by modality treatment and architectural paradigm. Section 4 surveys available datasets and benchmarks, analyzing their composition and limitations. Section 5 discusses evaluation challenges and proposes standardized assessment frameworks. Section 6 identifies open problems and outlines directions for future research, with particular emphasis on interpretability, multilingual support, and real-world deployability. Section 7 concludes with a summary of key findings and their implications for the field.

# 7 Future Directions

The current landscape of checkworthy content detection in short-form video is defined by a persistent and significant gap between research prototypes and deployable, real-world systems. Existing approaches, while demonstrating progress in multimodal understanding, largely fail to produce outputs that are interpretable and actionable for human fact-checkers. The primary limitation is not solely a matter of technical accuracy, but a fundamental misalignment between system design and operational workflow. High-throughput fact-checking environments demand tools that can not only identify potentially false claims across visual, auditory, and textual modalities but also justify their reasoning in a transparent manner. Without this interpretability, even a technically sophisticated model becomes a black box that slows down rather than accelerates the verification process, leaving the core obstacle of practical utility unaddressed.

Future research must pivot from optimizing isolated model performance metrics to co-designing systems with fact-checking practitioners. One promising direction involves the development of explainable multimodal architectures that can generate natural language justifications for why a specific video segment is deemed checkworthy, pointing to the exact combination of spoken words, on-screen text, and visual elements that triggered the assessment. This could be coupled with user-centric interface studies that establish standardized explanation formats, such as structured evidence summaries or confidence-calibrated highlights overlaid on the video timeline, which align with the cognitive processes of human verifiers. Another critical avenue is the creation of robust, multilingual, and cross-platform benchmarks that move beyond curated datasets to reflect the noisy, evolving, and culturally nuanced nature of content on platforms like TikTok. Research should also explore lightweight, on-device models that can provide real-time triage without compromising the privacy constraints of high-throughput moderation pipelines, ensuring that practical deployment is feasible at scale.

Pursuing these directions would fundamentally reshape the relationship between automated systems and human expertise in the fight against misinformation. By making system reasoning transparent and aligned with verification workflows, future tools could drastically reduce the cognitive load on fact-checkers, enabling them to prioritize and process claims with unprecedented speed and confidence. The impact extends beyond efficiency; interpretable systems foster trust and allow for human oversight to correct model errors, creating a synergistic loop where machine scalability and human judgment reinforce each other. Ultimately, closing the gap between technical capability and operational usability would transform checkworthiness detection from an academic exercise into an indispensable, deployable component of the information integrity infrastructure, directly empowering those on the front lines of content moderation.

# 8 Conclusion

This survey has examined the emerging landscape of checkworthy content detection in short-form video, revealing a domain marked by both rapid technological change and persistent methodological challenges. The central finding is that current systems, while increasingly capable of processing multimodal inputs, remain insufficiently aligned with the operational realities of fact-checking workflows. The fundamental tension lies between the technical sophistication required for accurate detection—demanding joint reasoning over visual, auditory, and textual streams—and the equally critical need for interpretable, actionable outputs that can be seamlessly integrated into high-throughput human review processes. This dual requirement has created a substantial gap between research prototypes and deployable tools, a gap that existing approaches have only begun to narrow. The analysis further indicates that progress in this area cannot be achieved through advances in model architecture alone; rather, it necessitates a holistic design philosophy that treats the human fact-checker as a primary stakeholder from the earliest stages of system development.

The significance of this survey resides in its articulation of a problem space that sits at the intersection of multiple rapidly evolving fields, including multimodal machine learning, natural language processing, and computational fact-checking. By foregrounding the operational constraints faced by human fact-checkers, the discussion reframes checkworthiness detection not merely as a technical benchmark but as a sociotechnical challenge requiring careful calibration between automation and human judgment. This perspective carries implications for how future systems are designed, evaluated, and deployed. It suggests that metrics of success must extend beyond standard accuracy measures to encompass dimensions such as output interpretability, workflow compatibility, and the cognitive load imposed on end users. Furthermore, the survey highlights the unique affordances and constraints of short-form video as a medium, where the compressed, multimodal nature of the content demands detection strategies that differ fundamentally from those developed for text-based or long-form media.

Moving forward, the research community must prioritize the co-development of detection capabilities and user-centered design principles. This entails fostering closer collaboration between system builders and fact-checking practitioners to ensure that technical innovations are grounded in genuine operational needs. Equally important is the pursuit of transparency in system outputs, enabling fact-checkers to understand not only what content has been flagged but also the reasoning underlying that determination. The path toward effective, deployable solutions will require sustained investment in multimodal datasets that reflect the linguistic and cultural diversity of platforms where short-form video proliferates, as well as evaluation frameworks that capture the nuanced demands of real-world fact-checking environments. The challenge is formidable, but the stakes—the integrity of information ecosystems shaped increasingly by short-form video—demand a concerted and pragmatic response.
