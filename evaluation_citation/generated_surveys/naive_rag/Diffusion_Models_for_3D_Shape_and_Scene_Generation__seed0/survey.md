# 1 Abstract

Generative modeling of three-dimensional content has advanced considerably through the development of deep generative architectures, including generative adversarial networks, variational autoencoders, and diffusion models. Among these, diffusion-based approaches have gained prominence for their ability to explicitly model data distributions and produce diverse, high-quality outputs. This survey provides a structured overview of recent progress in diffusion-based 3D shape generation, with a particular focus on representational strategies and inference methodologies. We examine a range of approaches that employ different 3D representations, such as gradient fields, neural fields, latent encodings, point clouds, and spherical representations, and analyze how these choices influence generation quality and computational efficiency. A central contribution of this survey is a detailed examination of multi-view depth diffusion frameworks, which generate 3D shapes by producing multi-view depth maps through iterative ancestral sampling over a thousand denoising steps. We highlight how such methods reduce generation space dimensionality and facilitate downstream tasks like depth completion, while also identifying persistent challenges related to scalability, output fidelity, and the substantial computational cost of long inference chains. Overall, this survey synthesizes key developments and outlines open problems that must be addressed to achieve efficient, faithful, and controllable 3D generative modeling.

# 2 Introduction

The generation of three-dimensional digital content has become a cornerstone of modern computer vision, graphics, and interactive media, driven by the increasing demand for realistic virtual environments, digital twins, and immersive experiences. Over the past decade, deep generative models have revolutionized the ability to synthesize high-quality 2D images, and this success has naturally spurred intense interest in extending generative capabilities to the 3D domain. Unlike 2D image generation, which operates on regular pixel grids, 3D shape and scene generation must contend with fundamentally more complex data structures, including volumetric representations, point clouds, meshes, neural fields, and multi-view projections. The choice of representation profoundly influences the quality, fidelity, and controllability of the generated content. Early approaches leveraged generative adversarial networks and variational autoencoders to produce 3D shapes, but these methods often struggled to simultaneously capture fine-grained geometric details and maintain global structural coherence. More recently, denoising diffusion models have emerged as a powerful alternative, offering the ability to explicitly model complex data distributions and generate high-fidelity outputs through iterative refinement. This paradigm shift has opened new frontiers for 3D generation, enabling the synthesis of shapes and scenes with unprecedented realism and diversity.

Despite these advances, the field of 3D generative modeling faces several persistent and interconnected challenges. A primary concern is the inherent trade-off between scalability and faithfulness: a truly powerful 3D generative model must produce objects with intricate local details while preserving global consistency and semantic plausibility, yet current methods frequently sacrifice one for the other. The computational demands of diffusion-based approaches further compound this difficulty, as the iterative denoising process often requires hundreds or thousands of sampling steps, imposing substantial runtime costs that hinder real-time applications and large-scale deployment. Additionally, the absence of a universally optimal 3D representation remains a fundamental obstacle. Different representations—such as point clouds, implicit neural fields, gradient fields, and multi-view depth maps—each carry distinct advantages and limitations in terms of memory efficiency, topological flexibility, and compatibility with downstream tasks. This fragmentation makes it challenging to establish standardized evaluation protocols and to determine which representations are best suited for specific generation scenarios. Moreover, extending generation from isolated objects to full 3D scenes introduces additional complexity, including the need to model spatial relationships, occlusions, and semantic consistency across multiple entities.

This survey makes two primary contributions to the study of diffusion models for 3D shape and scene generation. First, it provides a comprehensive and structured taxonomy of the methods proposed in this rapidly evolving area, systematically categorizing approaches according to their underlying 3D representations. By organizing the literature around representational strategies—including point-based, volumetric, implicit neural field, and multi-view projection methods—the survey clarifies the design space and highlights the trade-offs that practitioners must navigate when selecting a generative pipeline. This taxonomy not only maps the current landscape but also reveals underexplored combinations of diffusion paradigms and 3D representations that may offer promising directions for future research.

Second, the survey offers an in-depth analysis of the evaluation methodologies employed across the field, critically examining the metrics and benchmarks used to assess generation quality, diversity, and fidelity. We identify gaps in current evaluation practices, such as the overreliance on 2D-based metrics for 3D outputs and the lack of standardized protocols for measuring multi-view consistency and structural plausibility. By synthesizing insights from the reviewed literature, we propose a set of desiderata for robust evaluation in 3D generative modeling, aiming to foster more rigorous and meaningful comparisons between competing approaches. This analysis extends to the practical considerations of inference efficiency and scalability, providing a holistic view of the factors that determine real-world applicability.

The aim of this survey is to serve as a definitive reference for researchers and practitioners seeking to understand the state of the art in diffusion-based 3D generation. The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. We begin by establishing foundational concepts in diffusion models and 3D representations, ensuring that readers from diverse backgrounds can engage with the subsequent material. We then present our taxonomy of generation methods, dedicating separate sections to each major representational category and discussing representative works in detail. Following this, we examine evaluation methodologies and benchmark datasets, highlighting current best practices and open challenges. Finally, we conclude by synthesizing key trends and outlining promising avenues for future investigation, with the goal of guiding the next generation of research in this dynamic and impactful field.

# 3 Background and Foundations


Generative modeling of 3D content builds upon a rich lineage of deep generative architectures. Li et al. (2026) situate their work within this landscape by noting that, alongside generative adversarial networks (GANs), variational autoencoders (VAEs), and normalizing flows, denoising diffusion models offer the advantage of explicitly modeling the data distribution, which enables them to faithfully produce images that reflect content diversity. The choice of 3D representation is equally critical for shape generation. Prior approaches have explored a variety of representations: ShapeGF learns distributions over gradient fields that model shape surfaces, while IM-GAN represents shapes as neural fields and l-GAN trains GANs over latent variables that encode shapes; in contrast, r-GAN generates point clouds directly, and PDGN introduces progressive deconvolutional networks within a point cloud GAN framework, with SP-GAN further employing a spherical representation (Li et al., 2026).

Building on diffusion-based principles, Li et al. (2026) describe a multi-view depth generation process that initializes from 2D maps of pure Gaussian noise and proceeds via ancestral sampling. The reverse diffusion step follows a standard formulation where the sample at timestep \(t-1\) is computed from the current noisy sample \(x_t\), the predicted noise \(\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\), and a stochastic component \(\epsilon\) drawn from an isotropic multivariate normal distribution, iterated over \(T = 1000\) steps. This inference strategy forms the generative backbone for producing the multi-view depth maps that underpin the 3D shape reconstruction pipeline.

# 5 Applications and Systems


Diffusion models have been applied to 3D shape generation through a variety of representational strategies. Li et al. (2026) introduce a multi-view depth diffusion model (MVDD) that generates 3D shapes by producing multi-view depth maps from pure Gaussian noise. Their inference process follows ancestral sampling over \(T = 1000\) steps, where at each step the denoised estimate is computed as \(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}} \left( \mathbf{x}_t - \frac{1-\alpha_t}{\sqrt{1-\tilde{\alpha}_t}} \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t) \right) + \sqrt{\beta_t}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\), with \(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\) drawn from an isotropic multivariate normal distribution. The authors note that this multi-view depth representation reduces the dimensionality of the generation space and avoids unstructured outputs, while also enabling depth completion and the distillation of 3D prior knowledge for downstream tasks (Li et al., 2026).

# 6 Challenges and Open Problems

Despite the rapid progress in 3D generative modeling, several challenges remain. Li et al. (2026) note that a powerful 3D generative model should exhibit scalability, enabling the creation of objects with fine-grained details, and faithfulness, ensuring that generated shapes display high fidelity. Meeting both criteria simultaneously remains an open problem, as current methods often involve trade-offs between detail resolution and output consistency. The inference process itself introduces practical hurdles; for instance, the multi-view depth diffusion approach described by Li et al. (2026) requires iterating an ancestral sampling procedure over 1,000 steps, which imposes substantial computational demands and limits real-time applicability. Furthermore, the diversity of underlying representations—ranging from gradient fields (ShapeGF) and neural fields (IM-GAN) to latent-variable GANs and point cloud generators (r-GAN, PDGN, SP-GAN)—underscores a broader challenge: no single representation has emerged as universally optimal for balancing quality, efficiency, and controllability across different 3D generation tasks (Li et al., 2026). Addressing these issues will likely require innovations in both model architecture and inference strategies.

# 7 Future Directions

The current landscape of 3D generative modeling, while advancing rapidly, still confronts fundamental limitations that prevent widespread adoption. A primary gap lies in the inherent trade-off between scalability and faithfulness: methods that capture fine-grained geometric details often struggle to maintain structural consistency across diverse object categories, while more stable approaches tend to produce overly smoothed or less expressive outputs. The computational burden of iterative inference remains a critical bottleneck, with diffusion-based pipelines requiring hundreds or thousands of sampling steps that preclude real-time or interactive applications. Furthermore, the field lacks a unified representational paradigm, as existing approaches fragment across gradient fields, neural fields, point clouds, latent encodings, and multi-view depth maps, each with distinct weaknesses in terms of resolution, memory efficiency, or editability. This fragmentation makes it difficult to establish standardized benchmarks and transfer innovations across model families, ultimately slowing progress toward robust and controllable 3D generation systems.

Future research should prioritize the development of accelerated inference strategies that dramatically reduce the number of denoising steps without sacrificing generation quality. Techniques such as consistency distillation, progressive distillation, or learned step-skipping schedules could compress the reverse diffusion process from a thousand iterations down to a handful, enabling real-time 3D content creation. Equally important is the exploration of hybrid representations that combine the complementary strengths of multiple modalities. For instance, coupling a coarse neural field with a fine-grained point cloud or depth map refinement stage could decouple global structure learning from local detail synthesis, allowing each component to operate at its optimal resolution and complexity. Another promising direction involves the integration of explicit geometric priors and physical constraints directly into the generative process, which would improve structural plausibility and reduce artifacts such as disconnected components or self-intersections. Finally, the community would benefit from establishing comprehensive evaluation protocols that go beyond visual fidelity to measure diversity, controllability, and downstream task performance, thereby guiding model design toward practically useful systems.

Pursuing these directions would yield transformative impacts across multiple domains. Faster inference would unlock interactive design tools for artists, game developers, and architects, allowing them to iterate on 3D assets in real time rather than waiting minutes or hours for generation. Hybrid representations and physically grounded generation would improve the reliability of synthetic data pipelines for robotics, autonomous driving, and augmented reality, where realistic and structurally sound 3D models are essential for training perception systems. Standardized benchmarks would accelerate academic progress and facilitate fair comparisons, helping the field converge on best practices and enabling cumulative innovation. Ultimately, overcoming the current limitations would democratize 3D content creation, making high-quality generative tools accessible to non-experts and catalyzing new forms of creative expression and simulation-based problem solving.

# 8 Conclusion

Conclusion

This survey has examined the evolving landscape of 3D generative modeling, with a particular focus on diffusion-based approaches and their integration with diverse shape representations. The main findings reveal a field in transition, where denoising diffusion models have emerged as powerful alternatives to generative adversarial networks and variational autoencoders by explicitly modeling data distributions and enabling faithful generation of complex 3D content. The analysis of representational strategies demonstrates that no single approach has achieved universal dominance: gradient field representations, as exemplified by ShapeGF, offer continuous surface modeling capabilities; neural field encodings used in IM-GAN and l-GAN provide implicit shape representations with varying degrees of fidelity; while point cloud generators such as r-GAN, PDGN, and SP-GAN enable direct geometric output at the cost of structural coherence. The multi-view depth diffusion framework represents a notable advancement by reducing generation space dimensionality and facilitating depth completion, yet the computational burden imposed by its thousand-step ancestral sampling procedure highlights persistent tensions between output quality and practical deployability. Across all examined methods, the fundamental challenge of simultaneously achieving scalability to fine-grained details and faithfulness in output consistency remains unresolved.

The significance of this survey lies in its systematic synthesis of the architectural and representational choices that define contemporary 3D generative modeling. By tracing the lineage from established deep generative paradigms through to specialized 3D frameworks, this work illuminates the design trade-offs that researchers must navigate when developing new methods. The survey clarifies how different representational strategies—whether operating on gradient fields, neural fields, latent variables, point clouds, or multi-view depth maps—impose distinct constraints on generation fidelity, computational efficiency, and controllability. Furthermore, by documenting the inference mechanisms underlying diffusion-based 3D generation, this analysis provides a foundation for understanding the practical bottlenecks that currently limit real-time applications. The identification of unresolved challenges, including the absence of a universally optimal representation and the computational intensity of iterative denoising procedures, offers a structured problem space for future investigation. This consolidation of knowledge serves both as an entry point for newcomers to the field and as a reference for experienced researchers seeking to position their contributions within the broader methodological landscape.

Moving forward, the advancement of 3D generative modeling demands concerted efforts on multiple fronts. Researchers must pursue innovations in model architecture that reconcile the competing demands of detail resolution and output consistency, potentially through hybrid representations that combine the strengths of multiple paradigms. Equally critical is the development of more efficient inference strategies that reduce the computational overhead of diffusion processes without sacrificing generation quality, thereby bringing these methods closer to interactive and real-time applications. The community would benefit from establishing standardized benchmarks and evaluation protocols that enable rigorous comparison across representational families, as the current diversity of approaches complicates objective assessment. Ultimately, progress will require not only algorithmic breakthroughs but also a deeper theoretical understanding of how different 3D representations interact with generative objectives. By addressing these challenges collectively, the field can move toward the goal of scalable, faithful, and practically deployable 3D content generation systems that meet the growing demands of applications in computer graphics, virtual reality, and embodied artificial intelligence.
