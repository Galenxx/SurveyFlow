# 1 Abstract

Federated learning has emerged as a transformative decentralized paradigm that enables collaborative artificial intelligence model training across multiple institutions without the need to share sensitive raw data, making it particularly well-suited for privacy-critical domains such as healthcare. This survey provides a comprehensive examination of the adoption, evolution, and current state of federated learning in healthcare settings, with a specific focus on foundational algorithms and their application to modern model architectures. The review systematically analyzes the Federated Averaging algorithm as a core technique that facilitates multi-institutional collaboration while preserving data privacy and security, and it explores how the field has progressed from basic implementations to more specialized approaches tailored to the unique challenges posed by medical data. Key findings highlight that while Federated Averaging and related methods have established a robust foundation for privacy-preserving collaboration in healthcare, significant open challenges persist, including the need for rigorous comparative benchmarking against state-of-the-art techniques, the optimization of federated learning frameworks for emerging large language models, and the evaluation of efficiency and performance trade-offs in real-world clinical environments. This survey underscores the critical importance of continued methodological advancement and empirical validation to fully realize the potential of federated learning in sensitive healthcare applications.

# 2 Introduction

Federated learning has rapidly emerged as a transformative paradigm in artificial intelligence, offering a decentralized approach to model training that fundamentally rethinks how sensitive data can be utilized for machine learning. In traditional centralized learning frameworks, data from multiple sources must be aggregated into a single repository before model training can commence, a requirement that poses significant privacy risks and regulatory challenges, particularly in domains governed by strict data protection laws. Federated learning addresses this limitation by enabling collaborative model development across distributed institutions without requiring the exchange of raw data. Instead, participating entities train models locally on their own datasets and share only model updates, such as gradients or weight parameters, with a central server for aggregation into a global model. This paradigm has garnered substantial attention across various sectors, but its adoption has been especially pronounced in healthcare, where the tension between data utility and patient confidentiality is most acute. The ability to harness diverse clinical datasets from multiple hospitals, clinics, and research centers while maintaining compliance with privacy regulations has positioned federated learning as a cornerstone technology for the next generation of medical artificial intelligence.

Despite its considerable promise, the deployment of federated learning in healthcare settings is accompanied by a distinct set of challenges that extend beyond those encountered in conventional applications. Medical data are characterized by inherent heterogeneity, as patient populations, imaging protocols, diagnostic criteria, and data collection standards vary widely across institutions. This non-identically distributed nature of clinical data can severely degrade the performance of standard federated aggregation algorithms, leading to suboptimal global models that fail to generalize across diverse healthcare environments. Furthermore, the communication overhead associated with exchanging model updates becomes particularly problematic in resource-constrained clinical settings, where network infrastructure may be limited and the size of modern deep learning models continues to grow. Security vulnerabilities, including the risk of model inversion attacks and gradient leakage, threaten to compromise the very privacy guarantees that federated learning seeks to provide. Additionally, the transition from experimental research settings to real-world clinical deployment introduces practical obstacles related to system interoperability, regulatory approval, and integration with existing hospital information systems. These interconnected problems demand a comprehensive examination of the architectural designs, communication strategies, and deployment frameworks that can make federated learning both effective and practical in healthcare contexts.

This survey makes several significant contributions to the understanding and advancement of federated learning for privacy-preserving healthcare. First, it provides a systematic and in-depth taxonomy of federated learning architectures specifically tailored to medical applications, categorizing existing approaches based on their network topologies, aggregation mechanisms, and privacy-enhancing components. Unlike prior reviews that treat federated learning as a monolithic concept, this work delineates the spectrum of design choices available, from centralized and hierarchical architectures to fully decentralized peer-to-peer frameworks, and analyzes their respective suitability for different clinical scenarios. The survey further examines how these architectural decisions interact with privacy-preserving techniques such as differential privacy, secure multi-party computation, and homomorphic encryption, offering a nuanced perspective on the trade-offs between model utility, computational cost, and privacy protection that healthcare practitioners and researchers must navigate.

A second major contribution lies in the comprehensive analysis of communication-efficient federated learning strategies and their applicability to healthcare environments. This survey critically evaluates techniques including gradient compression, sparsification, quantization, and asynchronous update protocols, assessing their impact on both model convergence and clinical performance metrics. By synthesizing findings from recent empirical studies, the paper identifies which communication optimization methods are most viable for medical imaging, electronic health record analysis, and other healthcare tasks under realistic bandwidth and latency constraints. Moreover, the survey bridges the gap between theoretical advances and practical implementation by reviewing documented clinical deployment experiences, extracting lessons learned from real-world pilot studies, and proposing a structured framework for evaluating the readiness of federated learning systems for integration into clinical workflows. This deployment-oriented perspective distinguishes the survey from purely algorithmic reviews and provides actionable guidance for healthcare institutions seeking to adopt federated learning.

The primary aim of this survey is to serve as a comprehensive reference for researchers, engineers, and clinical informaticians working at the intersection of federated learning and healthcare. The paper is structured to guide the reader through the entire pipeline of federated learning system design and deployment. Following this introduction, the survey begins by establishing foundational concepts in federated learning and healthcare data privacy, then progresses to a detailed taxonomy of architectural paradigms. Subsequent sections address communication efficiency, privacy and security mechanisms, and the specific considerations for clinical validation and regulatory compliance. The survey concludes by identifying open research challenges and outlining future directions that hold the potential to accelerate the responsible adoption of federated learning in patient care.

# 7 Future Directions

The current landscape of federated learning in healthcare, while promising, is constrained by several notable limitations. Foundational algorithms such as Federated Averaging have enabled multi-institutional collaboration without direct data exchange, yet their evaluation has largely been confined to conventional model architectures and narrowly defined performance metrics. There is a scarcity of rigorous comparative studies that benchmark these aggregation strategies against state-of-the-art federated techniques specifically tailored for large language models. This gap leaves unanswered questions about scalability, communication efficiency, and convergence stability when deploying increasingly complex models across heterogeneous medical datasets. Furthermore, existing implementations often overlook the nuanced challenges of non-identically distributed clinical data, system-level heterogeneity across participating institutions, and the trade-offs between model utility and formal privacy guarantees. These unresolved issues indicate that the field has not yet fully matured to handle the demands of modern, large-scale healthcare AI systems.

To address these gaps, future research should prioritize the development of adaptive aggregation frameworks that go beyond simple parameter averaging. One promising direction involves designing heterogeneity-aware federated strategies that dynamically adjust to variations in local data distributions, computational resources, and network conditions across clinical sites. Such methods could incorporate meta-learning or personalized federated learning techniques to produce models that are both globally robust and locally fine-tuned for institution-specific patient populations. Another critical avenue is the systematic benchmarking of federated learning protocols in the context of large language models for healthcare tasks, including clinical note summarization, medical question answering, and diagnostic coding. These benchmarks must evaluate not only accuracy and efficiency but also fairness, interpretability, and resilience to distribution shifts over time. Additionally, integrating formal differential privacy guarantees into the federated training pipeline without catastrophically degrading model performance remains an open challenge that warrants dedicated investigation. Research could explore novel privacy-utility trade-off mechanisms, such as adaptive noise injection or secure multi-party computation hybrids, to enable safer deployment in real-world clinical environments.

The pursuit of these research directions holds substantial potential to transform the practical deployment of federated learning in healthcare. By establishing robust, heterogeneity-aware training protocols, institutions could collaborate on building large-scale medical AI models that generalize across diverse patient demographics while maintaining strict data sovereignty. Rigorous benchmarking against large language model-specific federated techniques would provide the empirical foundation needed to guide real-world implementation choices, accelerating the translation of research prototypes into clinical workflows. Moreover, advancing privacy-preserving mechanisms that do not compromise clinical accuracy would strengthen regulatory compliance and foster greater trust among participating institutions and patients. Ultimately, these efforts could unlock the full potential of decentralized AI, enabling the development of powerful diagnostic and prognostic tools trained on unprecedented volumes of diverse medical data without ever centralizing sensitive information, thereby setting a new standard for privacy-first innovation in healthcare.

# 8 Conclusion

Federated learning has rapidly evolved into a foundational paradigm for privacy-preserving collaborative model training, particularly within the healthcare domain. This survey has traced the progression of the field from its initial conceptualization to its current state, where decentralized frameworks enable multiple institutions to jointly train artificial intelligence models without exposing sensitive patient data. A central finding is the pivotal role of the Federated Averaging algorithm, which has been widely adopted as a core technique for aggregating model updates from disparate sources into a robust global model. The literature consistently highlights that this method successfully balances the dual imperatives of leveraging diverse, multi-institutional datasets and maintaining stringent data privacy and security. However, the analysis also reveals that the field is not without its challenges. While foundational methods provide a crucial basis for collaboration, there is a clear and ongoing need for rigorous comparative analysis against state-of-the-art techniques, especially as the focus shifts toward optimizing federated learning for large language models. The evidence points to persistent open problems concerning efficiency and performance, indicating that the simple application of established algorithms may be insufficient for the next generation of complex medical AI systems.

The significance of this survey lies in its synthesis of the current landscape, bridging the gap between foundational federated learning methodologies and their specialized application in healthcare. By consolidating the discourse around key algorithms and their limitations, this work provides a structured overview that clarifies the field’s trajectory. It underscores a critical juncture where the proven success of privacy-preserving collaboration must be reconciled with the demands of increasingly sophisticated model architectures. The survey highlights that the future utility of federated learning in medicine does not rest solely on its ability to protect data, but equally on its capacity to achieve performance parity with centralized, state-of-the-art models. This dual focus is essential for researchers and practitioners aiming to deploy federated systems in high-stakes clinical environments, where both privacy and predictive accuracy are non-negotiable.

Moving forward, the research community must prioritize the development of rigorous benchmarking frameworks and novel optimization strategies tailored to the unique constraints of healthcare data. The transition from validating basic federated averaging to mastering the complexities of large-scale models represents the next frontier. A concerted effort is required to systematically compare emerging federated techniques, ensuring that advancements in efficiency do not come at the cost of the privacy guarantees that make the paradigm so valuable. The call to action is clear: collaborative, cross-institutional initiatives must move beyond proof-of-concept studies to tackle the open problems of scalability and performance head-on. Only through such dedicated efforts can the full potential of federated learning be unlocked, ultimately enabling the creation of powerful, trustworthy AI systems that can be safely and effectively integrated into clinical workflows for the benefit of patients worldwide.
