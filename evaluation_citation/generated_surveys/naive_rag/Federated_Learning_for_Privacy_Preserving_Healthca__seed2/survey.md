# 1 Abstract

Federated learning has emerged as a transformative decentralized paradigm that enables collaborative artificial intelligence model training across multiple institutions without the direct exchange of sensitive data, making it particularly well-suited for healthcare applications where patient privacy is paramount. This survey provides a comprehensive examination of federated learning techniques specifically tailored to medical contexts, tracing the evolution from foundational implementations to specialized approaches designed to address the unique challenges posed by clinical datasets. We systematically review the core methodologies underpinning healthcare federated learning, with particular emphasis on the Federated Averaging algorithm and its variants, while analyzing the critical obstacles that arise in real-world clinical deployments. Key findings highlight persistent challenges in maintaining both model performance and robust privacy guarantees across heterogeneous, multi-institutional data distributions, as well as the added complexity introduced by integrating auxiliary capabilities such as explainability mechanisms and automated clinical reporting into privacy-preserving pipelines. The survey further identifies promising research directions for developing federated learning systems that can effectively balance the competing demands of privacy, interpretability, and clinical utility at scale. This review underscores the need for continued innovation in federated learning frameworks to fully realize their potential in transforming collaborative healthcare AI while safeguarding patient confidentiality.

# 2 Introduction

Federated learning has rapidly emerged as a transformative paradigm in machine learning, enabling collaborative model training across distributed data sources without the need to centralize sensitive information. In contrast to traditional centralized approaches, federated learning allows participating institutions to retain full control over their local datasets while contributing to the development of a shared global model through the exchange of model updates rather than raw data. This decentralized design has proven particularly compelling in domains where data privacy and security are paramount, and nowhere is this more evident than in healthcare. Medical data, encompassing electronic health records, medical imaging, genomic sequences, and clinical notes, is subject to stringent regulatory protections and ethical considerations that severely limit its movement across institutional boundaries. Federated learning offers a principled solution to this impasse, promising to unlock the potential of large-scale, multi-institutional AI models while respecting the confidentiality requirements that govern patient information. As the field has matured, research efforts have progressively shifted from proof-of-concept demonstrations toward the development of specialized techniques capable of addressing the distinctive complexities inherent in real-world medical environments.

Despite the considerable promise of federated learning in healthcare, its practical deployment in clinical settings remains fraught with unresolved challenges that span multiple dimensions of system design and operation. One central concern lies in the architectural choices that govern how federated networks are structured, including the topology of client-server relationships, the mechanisms for secure aggregation, and the strategies for handling heterogeneous data distributions across participating institutions. The widely adopted Federated Averaging algorithm, while foundational, introduces its own set of limitations when confronted with the statistical heterogeneity, class imbalance, and domain shift that characterize medical datasets collected from diverse clinical sites. Communication efficiency presents another critical bottleneck, as healthcare institutions often operate under bandwidth constraints and computational resource limitations that make the transmission of large model updates impractical. Furthermore, the integration of advanced capabilities such as model explainability, automated clinical reporting, and real-time decision support into privacy-preserving federated pipelines introduces additional layers of complexity that current methodologies have only begun to address. These intersecting demands highlight the need for a comprehensive examination of how federated learning systems can be engineered to simultaneously satisfy the requirements of privacy preservation, clinical interpretability, and operational scalability.

This survey paper makes two primary contributions to the growing body of literature on federated learning in healthcare. First, it provides a systematic and in-depth analysis of the architectural frameworks that underpin privacy-preserving federated systems, with particular attention to how different design choices impact the trade-offs among model performance, communication overhead, and data protection guarantees. By examining a broad spectrum of architectures—ranging from centralized aggregation schemes to fully decentralized and hierarchical topologies—this paper elucidates the conditions under which each approach is most suitable for clinical deployment scenarios. The analysis extends to the communication strategies that enable efficient model synchronization across distributed sites, including gradient compression techniques, asynchronous update protocols, and adaptive aggregation methods that account for the heterogeneous computational capabilities of participating healthcare institutions.

Second, this survey offers a critical assessment of the current state of clinical deployment for federated learning systems, identifying the practical barriers that have hindered the translation of research prototypes into routine healthcare workflows. This includes an examination of regulatory compliance challenges, interoperability requirements with existing health information systems, and the socio-technical factors that influence institutional willingness to participate in federated networks. The paper further investigates emerging solutions that integrate explainability mechanisms and automated reporting tools into federated frameworks, evaluating their effectiveness in enhancing clinician trust and facilitating the interpretation of model outputs in high-stakes medical decision-making contexts. By synthesizing insights from both technical and clinical perspectives, this survey aims to bridge the gap between algorithmic innovation and real-world applicability.

The aim of this survey is to provide researchers, practitioners, and healthcare administrators with a structured overview of the current landscape of federated learning for privacy-preserving healthcare, while also identifying open problems that warrant further investigation. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. We begin by establishing foundational concepts in federated learning and reviewing its evolution within the healthcare domain. Subsequent sections systematically examine architectural design choices, communication efficiency strategies, and privacy-preserving mechanisms, each accompanied by a comparative analysis of existing approaches. We then turn to the practical considerations surrounding clinical deployment, including regulatory frameworks, infrastructure requirements, and evaluation methodologies. Finally, we conclude by outlining promising research directions and discussing the broader implications of federated learning for the future of data-driven medicine.

# 7 Future Directions

The current landscape of federated learning in healthcare is shaped by several persistent limitations that hinder its broader clinical adoption. Foundational algorithms such as Federated Averaging, while effective in controlled settings, struggle to maintain consistent model performance when confronted with the heterogeneous data distributions that characterize real-world multi-institutional medical datasets. Privacy guarantees, though central to the federated paradigm, often rely on assumptions that do not fully account for the sophisticated inference attacks emerging against shared model updates. Moreover, the integration of essential clinical capabilities—particularly explainability mechanisms that allow practitioners to understand and trust model decisions, and automated reporting systems that streamline clinical workflows—remains largely disconnected from the privacy-preserving core of federated frameworks. These gaps collectively reflect a field that has successfully demonstrated feasibility but has not yet achieved the robust, interpretable, and operationally seamless systems required for routine clinical deployment.

Future research should prioritize the development of heterogeneity-aware federated optimization strategies that go beyond simple averaging to dynamically adapt to divergent data distributions across institutions. Such approaches could incorporate personalized layers, adaptive aggregation weights informed by local data characteristics, or meta-learning techniques that enable models to quickly calibrate to site-specific patterns without compromising global performance. Equally important is the need to design privacy mechanisms that are formally resilient against the evolving threat landscape of gradient leakage, membership inference, and model inversion attacks, potentially through tighter integration of differential privacy with secure aggregation protocols and the exploration of information-theoretic bounds on what model updates reveal. A parallel research thrust should focus on natively embedding explainability into federated training pipelines, developing methods that generate local and global interpretations without requiring centralized access to raw data. This could involve federated training of concept-based explanation models, distributed saliency mapping techniques, or collaborative construction of counterfactual explanations that respect the decentralized data governance structure. Finally, bridging the gap between federated model outputs and clinical decision-making demands the creation of end-to-end pipelines that couple trained models with automated reporting modules capable of generating structured, clinically meaningful summaries directly from distributed learning processes.

Advancing these research directions would fundamentally transform federated learning from a promising privacy-preserving technique into a clinically indispensable infrastructure. Robust heterogeneity-aware algorithms would enable equitable model performance across diverse patient populations, reducing the risk of bias against underrepresented groups and fostering trust among participating institutions. Strengthened privacy guarantees would lower the regulatory and ethical barriers to multi-institutional collaborations, accelerating the pace at which rare disease cohorts or geographically dispersed patient data can be harnessed for model development. By embedding explainability and automated reporting directly into the federated workflow, future systems could provide clinicians with transparent, actionable insights without disrupting established care processes, thereby bridging the persistent gap between algorithmic output and bedside decision-making. Collectively, these advances would establish a new standard for decentralized medical AI—one in which privacy, performance, interpretability, and clinical utility are not competing objectives but mutually reinforcing properties of a cohesive learning ecosystem.

# 8 Conclusion

Federated learning has emerged as a transformative paradigm in healthcare, offering a viable solution to the longstanding tension between data utility and patient privacy. This survey has systematically examined the evolution of federated learning in medical contexts, tracing its progression from foundational implementations to increasingly specialized frameworks designed to accommodate the distinctive characteristics of clinical data. The Federated Averaging algorithm remains a cornerstone technique, providing a robust mechanism for collaborative model training across distributed institutions without compromising data confidentiality. However, our analysis reveals that the deployment of federated learning in real-world clinical environments introduces multifaceted challenges that extend beyond the basic privacy-preserving architecture. Issues of data heterogeneity across institutions, the need for consistent model performance under non-identically distributed conditions, and the integration of auxiliary capabilities such as explainability mechanisms and automated clinical reporting represent persistent open problems. The intersection of these demands underscores the complexity inherent in developing federated systems that are simultaneously private, interpretable, and clinically effective at scale.

The significance of this survey lies in its comprehensive synthesis of the current landscape, bridging the gap between theoretical advances in federated learning and their practical application in healthcare settings. By mapping the trajectory from basic implementations to state-of-the-art specialized approaches, this work provides researchers and practitioners with a structured understanding of both the achievements and the limitations that characterize the field today. The survey highlights critical areas where existing methodologies fall short, particularly in balancing the competing requirements of robust privacy guarantees, model interpretability, and seamless integration into clinical workflows. In doing so, it offers a roadmap for identifying high-impact research directions and informs the design of next-generation federated learning systems tailored to the stringent demands of medical applications.

Moving forward, the field must prioritize the development of federated learning frameworks that holistically address the intertwined challenges of privacy preservation, performance reliability, and clinical usability. Future research should focus on advancing techniques for handling heterogeneous data distributions across institutions, embedding explainability directly into the federated training process, and streamlining the generation of clinically meaningful outputs. Collaborative efforts between machine learning researchers, healthcare providers, and regulatory bodies will be essential to translate these technical innovations into trustworthy tools that can be responsibly deployed in patient care. The continued maturation of federated learning in healthcare holds the promise of unlocking the full potential of distributed medical data, ultimately enabling more accurate, equitable, and privacy-respecting artificial intelligence systems that serve the broader goals of medicine.
