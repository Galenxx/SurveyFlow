# 1 Abstract

Molecular property prediction is a fundamental task in cheminformatics, and deep learning approaches based on Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have gained prominence due to their ability to directly capture molecular topological structures by representing molecules as graphs. While substantial research effort has been devoted to designing increasingly powerful neural architectures for graph-level classification and regression, the initial step of constructing molecular graphs from real-world chemical entities has received comparatively limited systematic examination. This survey provides a comprehensive review of the graph construction strategies employed in GNN-based molecular property prediction, critically analyzing how representational choices—including atom typing, bond perception, featurization schemes, and the handling of stereochemistry and three-dimensional information—influence downstream predictive performance. We identify and categorize the diverse modeling considerations that arise during graph construction, highlight common practices and their underlying assumptions, and reveal a significant gap between the sophistication of learning algorithms and the often-overlooked foundational step of graph representation design. Our analysis demonstrates that graph construction is not a trivial preprocessing step but a critical modeling decision that fundamentally shapes the information available to GNNs. By synthesizing insights from cheminformatics and graph representation learning, this survey aims to guide researchers toward more principled and context-aware molecular graph modeling practices.

# 2 Introduction

The prediction of molecular properties stands as a cornerstone of modern cheminformatics and drug discovery, underpinning efforts to identify promising therapeutic candidates and assess compound safety profiles. The ability to accurately forecast physicochemical characteristics, biological activity, and pharmacokinetic behavior directly from molecular structure has the potential to dramatically accelerate the drug development pipeline while reducing experimental costs. Over the past decade, the intersection of deep learning and molecular science has witnessed a paradigm shift, with data-driven approaches increasingly complementing or surpassing traditional physics-based simulations and empirical rules. Among these advances, Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have emerged as a particularly compelling framework, offering a natural and expressive means of encoding molecular information by representing atoms as nodes and chemical bonds as edges. This graph-centric perspective aligns intuitively with the relational nature of chemical structures and has enabled learning algorithms to directly perceive topological patterns that govern molecular behavior.

Despite the remarkable progress achieved through GNN-based approaches, the field continues to grapple with several fundamental challenges that limit both predictive performance and broader applicability. A primary concern revolves around the scarcity of high-quality labeled data, particularly for complex endpoints such as toxicity, metabolic stability, and in vivo efficacy, where experimental measurements remain expensive and time-consuming to obtain. Closely related is the issue of generalization across chemical space, as models trained on narrow compound libraries frequently fail when confronted with structurally novel molecules that diverge from their training distributions. Furthermore, the interpretability of GNN predictions remains an open problem, with many state-of-the-art architectures functioning as black boxes that offer little insight into the underlying structure-property relationships driving their outputs. Perhaps most critically, a significant imbalance has emerged in the research landscape: while substantial effort has been devoted to designing increasingly sophisticated neural architectures, comparatively limited attention has been directed toward the foundational question of how molecules are initially represented as graphs. The choices made during this graph construction step—including atom featurization schemes, bond type assignments, and the incorporation of three-dimensional conformational information—can profoundly influence downstream learning outcomes, yet these considerations have often been treated as peripheral rather than central to methodological development.

This survey makes several distinct contributions to the literature on GNN-based molecular property prediction. First, it provides a comprehensive and systematic taxonomy of graph construction strategies, moving beyond the prevailing architecture-centric narrative to foreground the representational choices that precede model training. By examining how different featurization paradigms, graph topologies, and molecular representations impact learning, this work illuminates a dimension of the modeling pipeline that has been largely underexplored in prior reviews. Second, the survey offers a critical synthesis of architectural innovations in the GNN space, including message-passing frameworks, attention mechanisms, and equivariant networks that incorporate geometric information, while explicitly connecting these design choices to the specific demands of molecular property prediction tasks. This dual focus on representation and architecture enables a more holistic understanding of the factors that contribute to model success or failure.

Third, this paper addresses the practical challenges that arise when deploying GNN models in real-world drug discovery settings, including strategies for handling data scarcity through transfer learning, self-supervised pretraining, and multi-task learning paradigms. The survey further examines emerging approaches for uncertainty quantification and model interpretability, both of which are essential for building trust in computational predictions within high-stakes pharmaceutical decision-making contexts. By consolidating insights from disparate research threads and identifying consistent patterns across benchmark studies, this work provides actionable guidance for practitioners seeking to navigate the rapidly expanding landscape of molecular GNNs.

The aim of this survey is to provide a structured and accessible resource for researchers and practitioners interested in applying GNNs to molecular property prediction, while simultaneously highlighting underexplored directions that warrant future investigation. Section 5 discusses practical considerations, including data efficiency, interpretability, and benchmarking practices. Section 6 identifies open challenges and promising research directions, and Section 7 concludes with a summary of key insights.

# 7 Future Directions

The current landscape of molecular property prediction reveals a pronounced asymmetry in research attention. While substantial efforts have been devoted to designing increasingly sophisticated graph neural network architectures, the foundational step of constructing the molecular graph itself remains comparatively underexplored. This representational stage involves numerous subtle decisions, including how to define nodes and edges, whether to incorporate three-dimensional geometric information, how to handle bond types and aromaticity, and how to represent ionic states or tautomeric forms. The prevailing practice often relies on standardized, heuristic-driven graph construction protocols that may not fully capture the chemical nuances relevant to specific prediction tasks. Consequently, the field lacks a systematic understanding of how initial graph modeling choices propagate through the learning pipeline and affect downstream predictive performance, robustness, and interpretability.

Future research should prioritize the development of principled frameworks for molecular graph construction that are both task-aware and chemically informed. One promising direction involves designing adaptive graph construction methods that can learn to optimize the molecular representation jointly with the prediction objective, rather than treating graph building as a fixed preprocessing step. This could include learning edge existence and edge types directly from quantum mechanical descriptors or experimental measurements, or dynamically adjusting graph topology based on the target property. Another important avenue is the systematic benchmarking of alternative molecular representations—such as multigraphs that distinguish different bond orders, hypergraphs that capture higher-order interactions among functional groups, or graphs augmented with stereochemical information—to quantify their impact across diverse prediction tasks. Additionally, research should explore how domain knowledge from computational chemistry, such as partial charges, bond dissociation energies, or frontier molecular orbital theory, can be explicitly encoded into graph construction rules to produce representations that are both more expressive and more interpretable.

Advancing the study of molecular graph construction holds the potential to substantially improve the accuracy and reliability of deep learning models for molecular property prediction. By establishing a more rigorous foundation for how molecules are represented, the field can reduce the risk that poor representational choices become hidden confounders in model evaluation and comparison. This line of work may also enhance model transferability across different chemical domains and prediction tasks, as representations grounded in chemical principles are likely to generalize better than those derived from architectural tuning alone. Furthermore, a deeper integration of chemical knowledge into the graph construction process can yield models whose internal representations align more closely with established chemical concepts, facilitating scientific discovery and increasing trust among practitioners in drug discovery, materials science, and related fields.

# 8 Conclusion

This survey has examined the foundational yet often overlooked role of graph construction in molecular property prediction using Graph Neural Networks. While the dominant paradigm in cheminformatics has centered on representing molecules as molecular graphs and leveraging advanced GNN architectures to perform graph-level classification or regression, our analysis reveals a pronounced imbalance in the research landscape. The primary finding is that the community has directed substantial effort toward designing increasingly powerful neural architectures, whereas the initial step of modeling molecules as graphs—the very representation upon which all subsequent learning depends—has received comparatively limited systematic attention. This representational stage involves critical decisions, including the definition of nodes and edges, the incorporation of stereochemical information, the treatment of bond types and aromaticity, and the handling of three-dimensional conformational data, all of which can profoundly influence downstream predictive performance. The survey highlights that these choices are frequently made based on convention or convenience rather than through rigorous, problem-specific deliberation, and that the sensitivity of GNN models to alternative graph formulations remains insufficiently explored. By bringing together fragmented discussions from across the literature, this work underscores that the graph model is not a neutral preprocessing step but a substantive modeling decision that shapes the information accessible to even the most sophisticated neural architectures.

The significance of this survey lies in its reframing of the molecular property prediction pipeline to elevate the importance of representational design alongside architectural innovation. By systematically identifying the considerations, assumptions, and trade-offs embedded in molecular graph construction, the survey provides a conceptual foundation for researchers to make more informed and transparent modeling choices. It also draws attention to an underappreciated source of variability in benchmarking and model comparison: differences in graph representations can confound claims about architectural superiority when not carefully controlled. Furthermore, the survey bridges the gap between cheminformatics domain knowledge and graph representation learning methodology, encouraging cross-disciplinary awareness that is essential for building models that faithfully capture chemical reality. In doing so, it opens avenues for future research that treats graph construction as an integral and optimizable component of the learning pipeline rather than a fixed preprocessing routine.

Moving forward, the field would benefit from a concerted effort to develop principled frameworks for molecular graph construction that are both chemically informed and empirically validated. Researchers are encouraged to report their representational choices with the same rigor applied to architectural and hyperparameter details, and to conduct sensitivity analyses that probe how alternative graph formulations affect model performance and generalization. Benchmarking initiatives should incorporate representational diversity as a dimension of evaluation, and new methodologies that learn or adapt graph structures during training warrant further exploration. Ultimately, progress in molecular property prediction depends not only on how we learn from graphs but on which graphs we choose to learn from. A more balanced investment in both representational foundations and neural architectures will be essential for advancing the reliability, interpretability, and real-world impact of deep learning in the molecular sciences.
