# 1 Abstract

Molecular property prediction has become a central task in computational chemistry and drug discovery, where deep learning methods based on Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have gained prominence for their ability to capture molecular topological structures by modeling molecules as graphs. However, the field faces persistent challenges, including the scarcity of labeled data that limits model performance in low-resource settings, and an underexplored focus on the graph models themselves—the ways in which molecules are represented as graphs—despite the inherent substructure complexity of molecular data. This survey provides a comprehensive review of recent advances in GNN-based molecular property prediction, with particular emphasis on two critical yet underinvestigated dimensions: data-efficient learning strategies that address label scarcity, and innovative graph modeling approaches that move beyond conventional molecular graph representations. We systematically examine few-shot learning, self-supervised pretraining, and meta-learning techniques designed to improve generalization from limited examples, while also analyzing emerging graph formulations—including heterogeneous graphs, hypergraphs, and motif-based representations—that better encode the hierarchical and multi-scale nature of molecular structures. Our analysis reveals that integrating data-efficient paradigms with expressive graph models significantly enhances predictive performance and robustness, and we identify key open challenges and promising research directions at this intersection. This survey aims to provide a structured overview that guides future efforts toward more effective and generalizable molecular representation learning.

# 2 Introduction

The intersection of deep learning and the life sciences has produced transformative advances in recent years, with molecular property prediction standing out as a task of both profound scientific importance and considerable practical impact. Accurately predicting properties such as toxicity, solubility, binding affinity, and bioactivity directly from molecular structure is central to accelerating drug discovery pipelines, reducing the reliance on costly and time-consuming wet-lab experiments. Traditional cheminformatics approaches have long relied on handcrafted molecular descriptors and fingerprints, which, while interpretable, often fail to capture the full complexity of molecular behavior. The emergence of graph neural networks has opened a new paradigm in this domain, enabling models to learn directly from the inherent topological structure of molecules. By representing atoms as nodes and bonds as edges, molecular graphs provide a natural and expressive abstraction that aligns closely with chemical intuition. Deep learning methods built upon graph neural networks have consequently attracted substantial attention, as they are capable of perceiving and reasoning over the rich relational information encoded in molecular topologies, recasting property prediction as a graph-level classification or regression problem.

Despite these advances, several fundamental challenges continue to limit the effectiveness and broader applicability of graph neural networks in drug discovery. One persistent obstacle is the scarcity of high-quality labeled data, which is particularly acute in pharmaceutical settings where experimental measurements are expensive and sparse. Deep learning models typically require large volumes of annotated examples to generalize well, yet many molecular property prediction tasks operate in low-data regimes, severely constraining model performance. An equally important but comparatively underexplored issue concerns the representational foundations of the learning pipeline. While the broader field of graph representation learning has devoted considerable effort to designing increasingly sophisticated neural architectures, the initial and critical step of constructing the graph model itself—that is, deciding how real-world molecular entities are abstracted into graph structures—has received only limited scrutiny. This modeling step involves navigating diverse structural considerations, including the treatment of bond types, stereochemistry, conformational flexibility, and non-covalent interactions, all of which can profoundly influence downstream predictive accuracy. The prevailing practice of relying on generic molecular graph formulations may therefore overlook essential substructure complexities inherent to chemical systems.

This survey makes several contributions to the literature on graph neural networks for drug discovery and molecular property prediction. First, it provides a comprehensive and structured overview of the state-of-the-art GNN architectures that have been specifically developed or adapted for molecular learning tasks, systematically categorizing them according to their message-passing mechanisms, readout functions, and strategies for capturing long-range dependencies. By organizing this rapidly expanding body of work into a coherent taxonomy, the survey offers researchers a clear roadmap of existing methodological developments and their underlying design principles. Second, the survey brings focused attention to the often-neglected dimension of molecular graph construction, reviewing approaches that move beyond standard atom-and-bond representations to incorporate richer chemical knowledge, such as pharmacophore graphs, fragment-based graphs, and multi-scale representations that jointly model atomic and functional group-level interactions. This emphasis on the graph model itself, rather than on the neural architecture alone, fills a notable gap in the current discourse.

Third, the survey examines the problem of data-efficient learning in molecular property prediction, reviewing techniques such as transfer learning, self-supervised pretraining, meta-learning, and data augmentation that aim to mitigate the impact of limited labeled data. By connecting these methodological strands with the specific challenges posed by molecular data, the survey highlights promising directions for enabling robust prediction in low-resource drug discovery scenarios. Finally, the paper identifies open challenges and under-explored research opportunities at the intersection of graph representation learning and molecular science, including the integration of three-dimensional structural information, the handling of dynamic and conditional molecular properties, and the need for greater interpretability in model predictions to support decision-making in pharmaceutical research.

The aim of this survey is to serve as both an entry point for newcomers seeking to understand the landscape of GNN-based molecular property prediction and a reference for experienced researchers interested in the frontiers of the field. The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. We begin by introducing the foundational concepts of molecular graph representation and the core principles of graph neural networks. We then present a detailed taxonomy of GNN architectures applied to molecular data, followed by an in-depth discussion of molecular graph construction strategies. Subsequent sections address data-efficient learning paradigms and survey prominent benchmarks and evaluation protocols. The paper concludes with a critical discussion of current limitations and a forward-looking perspective on future research directions.

# 7 Future Directions

The current landscape of molecular property prediction reveals two persistent limitations that constrain progress. First, the scarcity of labeled molecular data remains a fundamental bottleneck, limiting the ability of deep learning models to generalize effectively, particularly in low-resource settings where experimental measurements are expensive or time-consuming to obtain. Second, while substantial effort has been devoted to designing sophisticated graph neural network architectures, the initial step of constructing the graph representation itself has been largely overlooked. Standard molecular graph formulations typically encode atoms as nodes and bonds as edges, yet this rigid modeling paradigm fails to capture the rich hierarchical and multiscale structural complexity inherent in molecular systems, such as functional group interactions, conformational flexibility, and non-covalent interactions that critically influence molecular properties.

Future research should prioritize the development of adaptive and learnable graph construction frameworks that go beyond static, hand-crafted molecular representations. One promising direction involves designing methods that can dynamically adjust graph topology during training, allowing the model to discover task-specific substructures and interaction patterns that are most predictive of target properties. This could include incorporating higher-order structural motifs, such as rings, functional groups, and pharmacophores, as explicit modeling primitives rather than relying on the network to implicitly learn them through message passing. Another avenue lies in integrating multiple complementary graph views of the same molecule, each capturing different levels of granularity or different types of interactions, and developing fusion mechanisms that can leverage these heterogeneous representations jointly. Additionally, addressing data scarcity calls for advancing self-supervised pretraining strategies specifically tailored to molecular graphs, where models learn rich representations from large unlabeled molecular databases by solving pretext tasks grounded in chemical principles, such as predicting masked substructures, bond rotations, or molecular conformations.

The potential impact of advancing in these directions is substantial. More expressive and adaptive graph representations could significantly improve prediction accuracy across a wide range of molecular properties, directly accelerating drug discovery pipelines, materials design, and chemical synthesis planning. By enabling models to extract richer information from limited labeled data through better inductive biases and pretraining, these advances would democratize access to accurate molecular property prediction, benefiting research groups that lack extensive experimental resources. Furthermore, dynamic graph construction methods that reveal which substructures drive property predictions would enhance model interpretability, fostering trust among chemists and pharmaceutical scientists and enabling more informed molecular design decisions. Ultimately, shifting focus from architecture engineering alone toward a holistic reconsideration of molecular graph modeling has the potential to unlock new capabilities in computational chemistry and establish more robust foundations for machine learning in the molecular sciences.

# 8 Conclusion

In this survey, we have examined the landscape of molecular property prediction through the lens of graph-level learning, with a particular focus on the interplay between neural architectures and molecular graph representations. Our analysis reveals that Graph Neural Networks have emerged as the dominant paradigm, owing to their natural capacity to encode molecular topological structures and to recast property prediction as graph-level classification or regression. However, the literature demonstrates a pronounced asymmetry: while substantial effort has been devoted to refining GNN architectures and training strategies, the foundational step of constructing the molecular graph itself remains comparatively underexplored. We further identified persistent challenges arising from limited labeled data, which constrains model performance in low-resource settings and motivates the development of methods capable of generalizing from sparse examples. These findings collectively underscore that progress in molecular property prediction depends not only on architectural innovation but also on a deeper reconsideration of how molecular information is formalized as graph-structured input.

The significance of this survey lies in its structured synthesis of a rapidly evolving interdisciplinary domain that bridges graph representation learning and computational chemistry. By highlighting the architectural advances alongside the often-overlooked question of graph model design, we provide a balanced perspective that can guide both newcomers and experienced researchers in identifying critical bottlenecks. Our emphasis on the dual challenges of data scarcity and graph construction serves to redirect attention toward foundational issues that, if addressed, could unlock substantial improvements in predictive accuracy and generalizability. This work thus contributes a conceptual framework for understanding the current state of the field and for situating future contributions within a broader context of representation-level innovation.

Moving forward, we encourage the research community to pursue two complementary directions. First, greater emphasis should be placed on principled approaches to molecular graph construction that move beyond static, heuristic-based representations toward adaptive or learnable graph models capable of capturing the substructure complexity inherent in chemical data. Second, the development of data-efficient learning paradigms—including meta-learning, self-supervised pretraining, and transfer learning strategies tailored to molecular domains—should be accelerated to mitigate the impact of label scarcity. We envision that progress along these axes will not only advance molecular property prediction but also inform the broader field of graph representation learning, where the relationship between object representation and model architecture remains a fundamental yet underexamined dimension.
