# 1 Abstract

Molecular property prediction has emerged as a critical task in computational chemistry and drug discovery, driven by the ability of deep learning methods to model molecular structures effectively. Graph neural networks have become the dominant paradigm in this domain, representing molecules as molecular graphs and framing property prediction as graph-level classification. This survey systematically examines the landscape of graph-based molecular representation learning, with particular emphasis on the often-overlooked aspect of graph construction and modeling choices. While the research community has devoted substantial effort to advancing neural architectures for processing molecular graphs, the foundational question of how molecules should be represented as graphs in the first place has received comparatively limited attention despite its profound impact on downstream prediction performance. We provide a comprehensive review of existing graph construction strategies, ranging from conventional covalent bond-based graphs to more sophisticated representations incorporating spatial, geometric, and substructural information. Our analysis reveals that the choice of graph model significantly influences model accuracy, robustness, and generalizability across diverse molecular property prediction benchmarks. We identify key challenges including the handling of molecular conformations, the integration of multi-scale structural information, and the development of adaptive graph construction methods. This survey highlights the critical need for principled approaches to molecular graph modeling as an essential complement to architectural innovations in advancing the field of molecular property prediction.

# 2 Introduction

Molecular property prediction stands at the forefront of computational chemistry and drug discovery, serving as a critical component in the identification and optimization of therapeutic candidates. The ability to accurately predict physicochemical properties, biological activities, and pharmacokinetic characteristics of molecules directly influences the efficiency and success rate of drug development pipelines. Traditional approaches relying on experimental assays and physics-based simulations, while reliable, are often prohibitively expensive and time-consuming when applied to the vast chemical space of potential drug-like compounds. The emergence of deep learning has fundamentally transformed this landscape, offering data-driven alternatives that can rapidly screen millions of molecules in silico. Among these approaches, methods that represent molecules as graphs have proven particularly powerful, as they naturally capture the inherent topological structure of chemical compounds—where atoms become nodes and chemical bonds become edges. This graph-based perspective aligns intuitively with the way chemists conceptualize molecular structures, enabling learning algorithms to reason about local atomic environments and global molecular architecture simultaneously.

Despite the remarkable progress achieved by graph neural networks in molecular property prediction, the field continues to grapple with several fundamental challenges that limit broader applicability and reliability. A central tension exists between the substantial research investment directed toward architectural innovation and the comparatively neglected question of how molecules should be modeled as graphs in the first place. The prevailing practice of adopting fixed molecular graph conventions—where atoms are straightforwardly mapped to nodes and covalent bonds to edges—may not adequately capture the nuanced physicochemical phenomena that govern molecular behavior, such as non-covalent interactions, conformational flexibility, and quantum mechanical effects. Furthermore, the reliance on static graph representations introduces difficulties when dealing with dynamic molecular properties or when attempting to transfer knowledge across diverse chemical spaces. Additional challenges include the scarcity of high-quality labeled data for many therapeutically relevant endpoints, the interpretability of predictions in terms of chemically meaningful substructures, and the generalization of models trained on limited chemical domains to novel molecular scaffolds. These interconnected problems collectively underscore the need for a more holistic examination of the graph neural network pipeline for molecular property prediction.

This survey makes several contributions to the systematic understanding of graph neural networks in the context of drug discovery and molecular property prediction. First, we provide a comprehensive taxonomy of existing GNN architectures applied to molecular tasks, categorizing methods based on their message-passing mechanisms, readout operations, and inductive biases that incorporate chemical domain knowledge. This taxonomy reveals the design space explored by researchers and identifies architectural patterns that have proven most effective for different classes of molecular properties. Second, we conduct an in-depth analysis of graph construction strategies, moving beyond the conventional atom-level molecular graph to examine edge construction approaches that incorporate spatial proximity, quantum-derived interactions, and multi-scale representations. By foregrounding this often-overlooked aspect of the modeling pipeline, we illuminate how foundational choices in graph representation fundamentally shape downstream predictive performance.

Beyond architectural and representational considerations, this survey offers a critical examination of evaluation methodologies and benchmarking practices that have shaped progress in the field. We systematically compare the performance of leading GNN models across standardized molecular property prediction benchmarks, highlighting instances where methodological improvements have yielded genuine advances versus cases where gains may be attributable to experimental inconsistencies or data leakage. Additionally, we synthesize insights from recent work on interpretability and explainability techniques tailored to molecular GNNs, discussing how attention mechanisms, gradient-based attribution methods, and counterfactual explanations can provide chemically meaningful rationales for model predictions. This synthesis connects technical developments in graph representation learning with the practical requirements of drug discovery workflows, where understanding why a model makes a particular prediction is often as important as the prediction itself. The survey also identifies underexplored research directions, including the integration of three-dimensional structural information, the handling of molecular conformer ensembles, and the development of uncertainty quantification methods suitable for high-stakes pharmaceutical decision-making.

The primary aim of this survey is to provide researchers and practitioners with a structured overview of the current state of graph neural networks for molecular property prediction while drawing attention to critical yet underappreciated aspects of the modeling pipeline. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: we begin by establishing foundational concepts in molecular representation learning and graph neural network design, then proceed to a detailed taxonomy of GNN architectures organized by their treatment of molecular topology. Subsequent sections examine graph construction strategies, evaluation protocols, and interpretability methods, before concluding with a forward-looking discussion of open challenges and promising research directions. Through this structure, we aim to bridge the gap between methodological innovation in graph representation learning and the practical demands of drug discovery applications.

# 7 Future Directions

The predominant limitation in current molecular property prediction research lies in the asymmetric development of the field. While substantial effort has been invested in designing increasingly sophisticated graph neural network architectures, the foundational step of constructing the molecular graph itself remains underexplored and largely static. Standard practices rely on fixed conventions, such as representing atoms as nodes and covalent bonds as edges, without systematically questioning whether these representations capture the most salient information for a given property. This reliance on rigid graph construction overlooks the inherent complexity of molecular systems, including non-covalent interactions, dynamic conformational changes, and multi-scale phenomena. The lack of principled, adaptive graph modeling strategies means that models may operate on suboptimal representations, potentially limiting predictive accuracy and the ability to generalize across diverse chemical spaces. Addressing this gap requires moving beyond architecture-centric innovation toward a more holistic view where the graph model is treated as a learnable or optimizable component.

A critical future direction involves developing adaptive and substructure-aware graph construction strategies that tailor molecular representations to specific prediction tasks. Rather than imposing a single, universal graph topology, researchers should explore methods that dynamically infer edges based on learned chemical knowledge, such as attention mechanisms that discover non-obvious relationships between distant atoms or functional groups. Equally promising is the integration of multiple levels of granularity, where molecular graphs are augmented with higher-order substructures like pharmacophores, rings, or functional motifs as explicit nodes or hyperedges. This would enable models to reason directly about chemically meaningful fragments, improving both interpretability and data efficiency. Another avenue lies in learning the graph topology itself through differentiable graph generators or by jointly optimizing the graph structure and the downstream predictor, effectively treating graph construction as part of the end-to-end learning process. Such approaches could automatically surface the most predictive connectivity patterns for properties like toxicity, solubility, or binding affinity, which may depend on subtle electronic or steric effects not captured by covalent bonds alone.

Pursuing these directions would fundamentally reshape molecular representation learning by aligning the input representation with the underlying chemical and physical determinants of molecular behavior. Adaptive graph construction could unlock significant gains in prediction accuracy, particularly for complex endpoints where traditional graph conventions fail to encode relevant interactions. Moreover, substructure-aware models would enhance interpretability, allowing chemists to identify which molecular fragments drive a given prediction, thereby accelerating hypothesis generation and compound optimization in drug discovery. By bridging the gap between graph modeling and architectural design, future work can establish a more robust and flexible framework that generalizes across diverse molecular datasets and property landscapes, ultimately enabling more reliable in silico screening and molecular design.

# 8 Conclusion

This survey has examined the evolving landscape of molecular property prediction through the lens of graph neural networks, revealing a pronounced asymmetry in the research community’s attention. The primary finding is that while substantial effort has been invested in designing increasingly sophisticated neural architectures for graph-level representation learning, the foundational step of constructing the molecular graph itself remains comparatively underexplored. The prevailing practice relies heavily on fixed conventions for converting molecular structures into graphs, yet the choice of graph model carries profound implications for downstream predictive performance, as it determines which topological relationships, atomic interactions, and substructural patterns are made visible to the learning algorithm. This imbalance between architectural innovation and graph modeling rigor constitutes a critical bottleneck, as even the most advanced GNN architectures cannot compensate for information that is absent or misrepresented in the underlying graph representation. The survey further highlights that the complexities inherent in molecular systems—such as bond delocalization, conformational flexibility, and multi-scale interactions—are often simplified or overlooked in standard graph construction pipelines, thereby limiting the capacity of GNNs to capture chemically meaningful properties.

The significance of this survey lies in its systematic identification of a gap that has remained largely implicit in the literature. By juxtaposing the extensive body of work on GNN architecture design against the relatively sparse treatment of graph modeling choices, the survey reframes the discourse around molecular representation learning. It underscores that future breakthroughs in molecular property prediction may depend less on incremental architectural refinements and more on principled, domain-informed strategies for constructing molecular graphs. This perspective is particularly timely given the growing application of GNNs in drug discovery, materials science, and chemical engineering, where predictive accuracy and interpretability are paramount. The survey serves as a call to rebalance research priorities, encouraging the community to treat graph construction as a first-class problem rather than a preprocessing afterthought.

Moving forward, the field must embrace a more holistic view of molecular representation learning in which graph construction and neural architecture design are co-optimized. Researchers are urged to develop adaptive, substructure-aware, and context-dependent graph modeling frameworks that can capture the nuanced chemical and physical properties of molecules. This includes exploring dynamic graph formulations that account for conformational changes, incorporating domain knowledge about functional groups and electronic effects, and designing benchmarks that explicitly evaluate the impact of graph construction choices. Interdisciplinary collaboration between machine learning researchers and domain experts in chemistry will be essential to ensure that graph representations remain chemically faithful while being computationally tractable. By addressing the graph modeling gap with the same rigor and creativity that has been applied to neural architectures, the community can unlock the full potential of GNNs for molecular property prediction and accelerate progress in the molecular sciences.
