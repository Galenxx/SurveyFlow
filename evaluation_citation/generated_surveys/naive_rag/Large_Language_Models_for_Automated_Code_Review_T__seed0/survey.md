# 1 Abstract

The automation of software engineering tasks has been profoundly reshaped by the emergence of deep learning, with transformer-based large language models (LLMs) recently driving a new wave of innovation in code review and automated program repair. This survey systematically examines the trajectory of neural and LLM-based approaches for automating code-related tasks, tracing the evolution from early attention-based models to contemporary pre-trained architectures. We review and synthesize the key techniques that underpin this progression, including the foundational use of attention mechanisms in models like DeepFix, the introduction of the FConv architecture in CoCoNut, and the subsequent adoption of LLMs for both code review automation and review-driven repair. Our analysis identifies critical gaps in the current literature, highlighting unresolved challenges related to evaluation standardization, cross-project generalizability, and the practical integration of these models into developer workflows. We find that while LLMs demonstrate promising capabilities, the field lacks a cohesive understanding of their failure modes and the specific obstacles encountered during fine-tuning and prompting for domain-specific repair contexts. This survey provides a structured overview of the state-of-the-art, clarifies the open problems limiting practical adoption, and outlines directions for future research to advance the reliability and utility of automated software engineering tools.

# 2 Introduction

The automation of software engineering tasks has undergone a profound transformation with the advent of deep learning, fundamentally reshaping how developers approach quality assurance and code maintenance. Among the most significant recent developments is the emergence of large language models, which are deep learning architectures built upon the transformer framework and trained on vast corpora of natural language and source code. These models have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in understanding, generating, and manipulating code, opening new frontiers for automating activities that have traditionally demanded substantial human effort and expertise. As software systems grow increasingly complex and development cycles accelerate, the need for efficient, scalable, and reliable automated assistance has never been more pressing. Code review, a cornerstone of software quality assurance, represents a particularly compelling target for automation given its critical role in detecting defects, ensuring adherence to coding standards, and facilitating knowledge transfer among team members. The intersection of large language models and code review automation thus constitutes a rapidly evolving research landscape with profound implications for both academic inquiry and industrial practice.

Despite the considerable promise of large language models in this domain, the field faces a constellation of interconnected challenges that hinder progress toward robust and deployable systems. A fundamental difficulty lies in the absence of standardized evaluation benchmarks that can comprehensively assess model performance across the diverse dimensions of code review, including correctness verification, security vulnerability detection, style consistency checking, and actionable feedback generation. The generalizability of models trained or fine-tuned on specific projects or programming languages to unfamiliar codebases and paradigms remains poorly understood, raising questions about the practical utility of current approaches in heterogeneous industrial environments. Furthermore, the integration of automated review systems into established developer workflows presents substantial socio-technical hurdles, as practitioners must develop trust in machine-generated feedback and adapt their processes accordingly. Additional complexities arise from the need to handle multiple programming languages, accommodate project-specific conventions, and balance the trade-offs between computational cost and review quality. These challenges are compounded by the rapid pace of model evolution, which often outstrips the capacity of the research community to conduct rigorous, reproducible evaluations.

This survey makes several distinctive contributions to the systematic understanding of large language models for automated code review. First, it provides a comprehensive and structured taxonomy of the techniques that have been proposed for adapting and applying these models to code review tasks, encompassing fine-tuning strategies, prompt engineering approaches, retrieval-augmented generation methods, and hybrid architectures that combine neural models with traditional static analysis tools. By organizing the technical landscape into coherent categories, this survey enables researchers and practitioners to identify the strengths and limitations of different methodological families and to recognize promising directions for future investigation. The taxonomy also illuminates the relationships between code review automation and adjacent areas such as automated program repair, test generation, and vulnerability detection, revealing opportunities for cross-pollination and integrated solutions.

Second, this survey offers an in-depth examination of the benchmarks and evaluation frameworks that have been developed to assess model performance, critically analyzing their coverage, realism, and ecological validity. It identifies gaps in existing evaluation practices, such as the overemphasis on syntactic correctness at the expense of semantic soundness and the limited attention to non-functional properties including performance implications and maintainability. Third, the survey bridges the gap between academic research and industrial application by documenting real-world deployment experiences, distilling lessons learned from organizations that have integrated large language models into their code review pipelines. This industrial perspective highlights practical considerations that are often underrepresented in the research literature, including latency requirements, cost constraints, privacy concerns, and the importance of human-in-the-loop designs. By synthesizing these technical, evaluative, and practical dimensions, the survey provides a holistic view of the current state of the art and its trajectory.

The primary aim of this survey is to serve as a definitive reference for researchers, practitioners, and tool builders seeking to understand the capabilities, limitations, and future potential of large language models in automated code review. The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. We begin by establishing foundational concepts, including the transformer architecture, the code review process, and the historical evolution of automation efforts in this domain. We then present our taxonomy of techniques, systematically categorizing approaches based on their underlying methodologies and architectural choices. Subsequent sections provide a detailed analysis of benchmarks and evaluation methodologies, followed by case studies of industrial deployment that highlight practical challenges and solutions. We conclude by identifying open problems and outlining a research agenda that addresses the most pressing gaps in current knowledge, with the goal of guiding future efforts toward more capable, trustworthy, and widely adopted automated code review systems.

# 7 Future Directions

While large language models have shown promise for automating software engineering tasks such as code review and program repair, their current application remains nascent and under-characterized. The existing literature highlights the architectural evolution from early attention-based and convolutional neural repair systems to contemporary transformer-based models, yet it stops short of identifying the concrete failure modes, evaluation gaps, and practical integration challenges that limit real-world adoption. Without a clear understanding of how these models perform across diverse codebases, programming languages, and review contexts, the community lacks the empirical foundation needed to move beyond proof-of-concept demonstrations toward robust, dependable tools.

Future research should prioritize the development of rigorous, multi-dimensional evaluation benchmarks that go beyond syntactic correctness to assess semantic equivalence, maintainability, and alignment with developer intent in both code review and automated repair scenarios. Such benchmarks must span multiple programming languages, project domains, and review workflows to expose generalizability limitations and reveal brittle behaviors that remain hidden in narrow experimental settings. In parallel, researchers should investigate fine-tuning strategies and prompting techniques specifically tailored to software engineering tasks, exploring how domain-adaptive pre-training, retrieval-augmented generation, and structured reasoning mechanisms can improve the factual accuracy and contextual appropriateness of model outputs. Studies that systematically compare zero-shot, few-shot, and fully fine-tuned regimes across repair and review subtasks will be essential for establishing reliable best practices.

A further direction involves human-centered studies that examine how these models can be integrated into developer workflows without disrupting established practices or introducing unacceptable cognitive overhead. This includes designing interfaces that present model suggestions as collaborative recommendations rather than authoritative fixes, studying the impact of explainability features on developer trust and review efficiency, and measuring long-term effects on code quality and team dynamics. Such investigations should also address the socio-technical risks of over-reliance on automated suggestions, including the potential erosion of debugging skills and the propagation of subtle, model-specific error patterns.

Addressing these gaps will have a substantial impact on both research and practice. Robust benchmarks and systematic evaluation protocols will enable reproducible progress and meaningful comparisons across competing approaches, accelerating the maturation of the field. Improved fine-tuning and prompting strategies will directly enhance the utility of large language models for everyday development tasks, reducing the manual effort required for code review and defect resolution while maintaining high quality standards. Finally, human-centered integration studies will ensure that these technical advances translate into genuine productivity gains and improved developer satisfaction, rather than introducing new sources of friction or risk. Collectively, these efforts will help bridge the gap between promising laboratory results and dependable, widely adopted automation in software engineering.

# 8 Conclusion

This survey has traced the evolution of deep learning architectures applied to software engineering automation, with a particular focus on code review and automated program repair. The findings reveal a clear trajectory from early attention-based neural models, such as DeepFix, which targeted specific error types in constrained programming environments, to the adoption of more flexible architectures like the FConv model introduced by CoCoNut. This progression has culminated in the contemporary use of large language models built upon transformer architectures, which are now being leveraged for both code review automation and the generation of program fixes guided by review feedback. The transition highlights a field moving rapidly toward general-purpose, pre-trained models that promise greater adaptability and contextual understanding, yet the surveyed evidence also underscores that this migration is still nascent. The concrete limitations, failure modes, and optimal application strategies for these large-scale models remain largely undocumented, leaving a gap between the demonstrated potential of these architectures and their validated, reliable deployment in real-world software engineering pipelines.

The significance of this survey lies in its synthesis of a fragmented research landscape, connecting foundational neural repair techniques with the latest LLM-based approaches to code review and repair. By contextualizing contemporary work within the historical progression of deep learning architectures, the survey clarifies that current innovations are not isolated breakthroughs but rather the latest iteration in a continuous effort to automate cognitive aspects of software development. This perspective is crucial for both researchers and practitioners, as it cautions against treating LLMs as a monolithic solution and instead encourages a nuanced understanding of their lineage and inherent challenges. The identified absence of detailed empirical characterizations of LLM limitations points to a critical need for rigorous benchmarking, the development of standardized evaluation frameworks, and deeper investigation into the generalizability of these models across diverse codebases and programming languages.

Moving forward, the research community must prioritize a systematic examination of the open challenges that currently impede the practical adoption of LLMs in software engineering. Future work should move beyond proof-of-concept demonstrations to conduct in-depth analyses of failure cases, investigate effective strategies for fine-tuning and prompt engineering within domain-specific contexts, and design robust metrics that assess not only syntactic correctness but also semantic appropriateness and integration cost within developer workflows. A concerted effort to address these fundamental questions will be essential to bridge the gap between the theoretical promise of large language models and their dependable, everyday use in automating the complex, nuanced tasks of code review and program repair.
