# 1 Abstract

The automation of software engineering tasks has become a focal point of research with the rise of deep learning, particularly through the application of large language models based on the transformer architecture. This survey systematically examines the evolution and current landscape of automated code review and program repair, tracing the transition from early task-specific neural architectures to contemporary general-purpose pre-trained models. We analyze the foundational contributions of neural approaches, such as attention-based mechanisms for predicting fixes in C programs and the introduction of specialized convolutional architectures that addressed the limitations of prior neural machine translation methods. Our review highlights how these pioneering techniques established the groundwork for the subsequent adoption of large language models, which are now being leveraged to repair code directly from review feedback. The survey synthesizes the key methodological shifts and identifies the capabilities that have enabled this progression toward more flexible and powerful automated software maintenance tools. Ultimately, this work provides a structured overview of the field’s trajectory, offering insights into the architectural innovations that have shaped the current state-of-the-art in learning-based program repair and code review automation.

# 2 Introduction

The automation of software engineering tasks has emerged as a central research frontier in recent years, driven by the remarkable capabilities of deep learning models to understand and generate source code. Among the various activities that constitute the software development lifecycle, code review stands out as a critical quality assurance practice that ensures code correctness, maintainability, and adherence to project standards. Traditionally, code review has been a manual, time-consuming process requiring significant human effort and expertise. However, the advent of large language models—deep learning architectures built upon the transformer framework and trained on vast corpora of natural language and code—has opened new possibilities for automating substantial portions of this workflow. These models have demonstrated unprecedented proficiency in comprehending code semantics, identifying defects, and suggesting improvements, fundamentally reshaping the landscape of automated software maintenance and inspiring a rapidly growing body of research dedicated to their application in code review contexts.

Despite the promising trajectory of large language model adoption for code review automation, the field faces a constellation of significant challenges that hinder both academic progress and practical deployment. A primary concern lies in the absence of standardized evaluation protocols and benchmarks, which makes it difficult to meaningfully compare different approaches and assess their real-world effectiveness. The generalizability of these models across diverse programming languages, project domains, and review scenarios remains poorly understood, with many existing studies limited to narrow experimental settings. Furthermore, the computational cost associated with fine-tuning and deploying large language models at scale poses substantial barriers to industrial adoption, particularly for organizations with constrained resources. Issues of data privacy, model interpretability, and the seamless integration of automated review suggestions into established developer workflows further complicate the transition from laboratory prototypes to production-ready systems. These multifaceted problems underscore the need for a comprehensive synthesis of current knowledge that can illuminate both the achievements and the persistent gaps in the literature.

This survey paper makes three primary contributions to the field of automated code review using large language models. First, it provides a systematic and in-depth taxonomy of the techniques that have been proposed for leveraging large language models in code review tasks, categorizing approaches based on their architectural foundations, training paradigms, and the specific review activities they target—ranging from defect detection and code quality assessment to automated comment generation and repair suggestion. By organizing the fragmented landscape of existing methods into a coherent framework, this survey enables researchers and practitioners to identify the dominant trends, understand the evolutionary trajectory from earlier neural architectures to contemporary transformer-based models, and recognize underexplored areas ripe for future investigation.

Second, this paper delivers a critical analysis of the benchmarks and evaluation methodologies currently employed to measure the performance of automated code review systems. We examine the datasets, metrics, and experimental designs that underpin empirical assessments in the literature, highlighting their strengths and limitations. This analysis reveals significant inconsistencies in how success is defined and measured across studies, and it identifies key gaps in benchmark coverage—such as the underrepresentation of certain programming languages and review scenarios. By foregrounding these methodological issues, the survey provides actionable guidance for developing more robust and ecologically valid evaluation frameworks that can accelerate progress toward reliable automated code review tools.

The aim of this survey is to offer a comprehensive and structured overview of the state of the art in large language model-based code review automation, serving as both a reference for experienced researchers and an entry point for newcomers to the field. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. We begin by establishing foundational concepts, including the code review process and the evolution of neural architectures from task-specific models to general-purpose large language models. We then present our taxonomy of techniques, followed by a detailed examination of benchmarks and evaluation practices. Subsequent sections address the challenges and strategies associated with industrial deployment, including considerations of scalability, privacy, and human-computer interaction. Finally, we synthesize key findings and outline promising directions for future research, concluding with a summary of the current landscape and its implications for both academia and industry.

# 7 Future Directions

The current landscape of LLM-based code review and automated program repair reveals several important gaps that limit both the reliability and practical adoption of these techniques. Existing studies demonstrate promising capabilities but do not systematically characterize failure modes, leaving practitioners uncertain about when and why LLM-generated suggestions may be incorrect or unsafe. Evaluation practices remain inconsistent across studies, with varying metrics, benchmarks, and baselines that hinder meaningful comparison and obscure progress. Generalizability across programming languages, codebases, and project domains is largely unexplored, as most work focuses on narrow settings such as C programs or specific error types. Additionally, the computational cost and latency of deploying large models in real-time developer workflows present practical barriers that have not been adequately addressed. The integration of LLM-based tools into existing code review processes raises open questions about developer trust, interpretability of model outputs, and the appropriate division of responsibility between automated systems and human reviewers. Finally, data requirements for fine-tuning and adapting these models to specific organizational contexts remain poorly understood, limiting the ability of teams to customize tools for their particular coding standards and defect patterns.

Future research should prioritize developing comprehensive benchmarks and evaluation frameworks that capture the multifaceted nature of code review and repair quality. Such frameworks should assess not only syntactic correctness but also semantic appropriateness, adherence to project-specific conventions, security implications, and the clarity of generated explanations accompanying suggested changes. Controlled studies that systematically probe failure modes across different model architectures, sizes, and prompting strategies would provide valuable guidance for practitioners selecting and configuring these tools. Another critical direction involves investigating lightweight adaptation techniques that allow organizations to tailor LLMs to their codebases without prohibitive computational costs, potentially through parameter-efficient fine-tuning or retrieval-augmented generation that incorporates project-specific context. Research should also explore hybrid approaches that combine the strengths of LLMs with traditional static analysis and formal methods, using each technique where it is most reliable. Longitudinal field studies examining how developers interact with LLM-based review tools in authentic settings would illuminate the human factors that determine successful adoption, including trust calibration, workflow integration, and the impact on reviewer attention and diligence. Finally, work on interpretability and uncertainty quantification could enable models to communicate confidence levels and rationale, allowing developers to make informed decisions about when to accept or scrutinize automated suggestions.

Addressing these research directions would substantially advance the practical viability of LLM-based code review and repair systems. Robust evaluation frameworks would enable the research community to measure progress more reliably and help organizations make evidence-based decisions about tool adoption. A deeper understanding of failure modes and adaptation strategies would reduce the risk of deploying these systems in safety-critical contexts, where incorrect automated changes could introduce subtle defects. Improved integration of LLMs with complementary techniques and developer workflows could enhance productivity while maintaining the quality assurance benefits of human code review. By tackling the human factors and interpretability challenges, future work could foster appropriate trust and effective collaboration between developers and automated tools, ultimately leading to more reliable software and more efficient development processes.

# 8 Conclusion

This survey has traced the trajectory of automated software engineering, specifically within the interconnected domains of code review and program repair, revealing a decisive paradigm shift from specialized neural architectures to general-purpose large language models. The main findings highlight a clear evolutionary path, beginning with task-specific models like DeepFix, which employed an attention mechanism to correct common errors in C programs, and CoCoNut, which introduced the FConv architecture to overcome the limitations of earlier neural machine translation methods. These foundational approaches established the viability of neural networks for program repair but were inherently constrained by their narrow problem scopes and reliance on curated datasets. The survey then documents the pivotal transition to LLM-based methods, which leverage the transformer architecture to process and generate code within a broader contextual understanding. This shift is evident in recent work that utilizes pre-trained models and LLMs not only for automated program repair but also for the more nuanced task of automating code review, where the model must comprehend coding standards, identify logical flaws, and suggest improvements. The core finding is that the field is rapidly consolidating around the versatility of LLMs, which promise to unify previously disparate tasks under a single, powerful paradigm, moving beyond simple error correction toward a holistic automation of software quality assurance.

The significance of this survey lies in its synthesis of two historically parallel research streams, demonstrating that the challenges of code review automation and program repair are converging under the capabilities of large language models. By charting the progression from early neural models to contemporary LLM-based systems, this work provides a structured historical context that clarifies the motivations behind the current methodological shift. It underscores that the transition is not merely a change in model scale but a fundamental re-conceptualization of the problem: from training a model for a single, well-defined task to adapting a pre-trained, general-purpose model to a complex, context-dependent software engineering activity. This perspective is crucial for researchers and practitioners alike, as it frames the current state-of-the-art not as an isolated breakthrough but as the logical culmination of a decade of research. The survey thus serves as a critical roadmap, contextualizing the proliferation of LLM applications and highlighting that the principal challenge has evolved from model architecture design to the effective adaptation, evaluation, and integration of these powerful, yet opaque, models into the software development lifecycle.

In conclusion, the field stands at a critical juncture where the potential of large language models to revolutionize code review and program repair is palpable, yet their path to reliable, real-world deployment is fraught with unresolved challenges. The community must now direct its collective effort beyond demonstrating feasibility on benchmark datasets and toward a rigorous, multi-faceted investigation of the open problems that impede practical adoption. Future work must prioritize the establishment of robust, standardized evaluation frameworks that measure not just syntactic correctness but also semantic soundness, functional equivalence, and alignment with developer intent. Furthermore, research must systematically address the failure modes of LLMs, including the generation of plausible but incorrect patches, the propagation of latent biases, and the prohibitive computational costs associated with large-scale deployment. A concerted call to action is necessary to bridge the gap between promising research prototypes and dependable industrial tools, demanding a deep collaboration between academia and industry to ensure that the next generation of automated software engineering tools is not only powerful but also trustworthy, explainable, and seamlessly integrated into the human-centric process of software development.
