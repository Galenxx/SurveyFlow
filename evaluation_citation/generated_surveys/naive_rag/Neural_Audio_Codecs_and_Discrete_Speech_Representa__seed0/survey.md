# 1 Abstract

The representation of speech as discrete tokens has become a foundational paradigm for neural codec-based speech-language models, enabling the compression and discretization of continuous audio signals into compact symbolic sequences. This survey provides a comprehensive review of recent advances in neural codec architectures, with a particular focus on methods that learn disentangled discrete representations at multiple levels of granularity. We examine encoder-quantizer frameworks that compress input waveforms into latent representations before discretization, and analyze approaches that incorporate self-supervised learning representations to enhance token quality. Key contributions include the synthesis of techniques for jointly modeling global and local speech characteristics within low-bitrate codecs, the evaluation of representational capacity across multilingual speech understanding and emotion recognition tasks, and the identification of open challenges in semantic grounding and contextual information preservation. Our analysis reveals that while significant progress has been made in disentangling hierarchical speech features, fundamental questions remain regarding the integration of external representations without compromising generative fidelity and the extent to which discrete tokens capture sufficient semantic content for downstream applications. This survey consolidates current knowledge and highlights promising directions for future research in neural codec-based speech representation learning.

# 2 Introduction

Neural audio codecs have emerged as a cornerstone technology in modern speech processing, enabling the efficient compression and reconstruction of high-fidelity audio signals through learned discrete representations. Unlike traditional signal processing pipelines that rely on handcrafted features and perceptual heuristics, neural codecs leverage deep neural networks to map continuous speech waveforms into compact latent spaces, where information is organized as sequences of discrete tokens. This paradigm shift has been propelled by advances in self-supervised learning, vector quantization, and end-to-end optimization, which together allow these models to capture both acoustic detail and higher-level linguistic structure. The resulting discrete representations serve as a bridge between raw audio and symbolic language, opening new possibilities for generative modeling, voice conversion, text-to-speech synthesis, and spoken language understanding. As the field progresses, the design of neural codecs has moved beyond mere compression efficiency toward the pursuit of semantically meaningful and structurally interpretable tokenizations that can support a wide range of downstream tasks.

Despite these advances, several fundamental challenges remain unresolved in the development and application of neural audio codecs. One central problem is the difficulty of learning disentangled discrete representations that separately encode global speaker characteristics, such as timbre and identity, and local linguistic content, including phonetic and prosodic detail. The joint modeling of these hierarchical factors within a compact bitrate budget poses significant optimization and architectural challenges, often resulting in entangled tokens that conflate speaker and content information. A second critical issue concerns the semantic grounding of discrete tokens, namely the extent to which quantized latent codes preserve the contextual and semantic properties of the original speech signal. Without explicit mechanisms to align discrete representations with higher-level linguistic or paralinguistic features, the tokens may lack the richness required for generative tasks that demand fine-grained control and interpretability. Furthermore, the integration of external knowledge sources, such as self-supervised representations, introduces open questions about how to balance the fidelity of the codec’s reconstruction with the semantic utility of the learned tokens.

This survey makes two primary contributions to the understanding and organization of the rapidly evolving literature on neural audio codecs and discrete speech representations. First, it provides a comprehensive and structured overview of neural codec architectures, with a particular focus on the encoder-quantizer paradigm that underpins most contemporary systems. We systematically examine the design choices involved in building these models, including the types of encoders, quantization strategies, and decoder configurations that have been proposed, and we analyze how these components interact to shape the properties of the resulting discrete tokens. By tracing the evolution from early vector-quantized variational autoencoders to recent low-bitrate and multi-scale frameworks, we identify the key architectural innovations that have driven progress in compression efficiency and representation quality.

Second, this survey offers an in-depth analysis of tokenization methods and their implications for generative applications. We investigate the mechanisms through which discrete tokens are learned and evaluate their capacity to capture both acoustic fidelity and semantic content, drawing on empirical evidence from multilingual speech understanding, emotion recognition, and other downstream tasks. A distinctive aspect of our contribution is the critical examination of disentanglement techniques and semantic enrichment strategies, including the incorporation of self-supervised learning representations and contextual fusion mechanisms. By synthesizing findings across disparate lines of research, we highlight the trade-offs between compactness, disentanglement, and semantic expressiveness, and we delineate the conditions under which discrete neural codec tokens can most effectively serve as a foundation for speech-language models and other generative systems.

The aim of this survey is to equip researchers and practitioners with a clear and systematic understanding of the current landscape of neural audio codecs, from foundational architectures to cutting-edge tokenization and generative applications. The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. We begin by establishing the necessary background on neural codec architectures and the encoder-quantizer paradigm, followed by a detailed treatment of discrete representation learning and the associated challenges of disentanglement and semantic grounding. We then survey the use of discrete tokens in generative speech models, examining their role in tasks such as text-to-speech, voice conversion, and spoken language generation. Finally, we discuss open problems and promising directions for future research, offering a forward-looking perspective on the development of more capable and interpretable neural codec systems.

# 7 Future Directions

Current neural codec-based speech-language models face several interrelated limitations that constrain their broader adoption and effectiveness. The primary challenge remains the difficulty of learning truly disentangled representations that capture both global speaker characteristics and local phonetic content without sacrificing compactness or generative fidelity. While recent frameworks have made progress by incorporating self-supervised learning representations, the optimal strategy for integrating external knowledge remains unresolved, often introducing tension between representational richness and codec simplicity. A further critical gap lies in the semantic grounding of discrete tokens. The standard encoder-quantizer pipeline compresses speech waveforms into latent sequences, but ensuring that these discretized units retain sufficient semantic and contextual information for downstream tasks continues to be an open problem. Current evidence points to active experimentation without a settled solution, particularly regarding how to preserve fine-grained prosodic, emotional, and contextual cues through the discretization bottleneck. Additionally, the multilingual generalizability of these representations, while explored in limited settings, has not been systematically validated across diverse linguistic typologies, low-resource languages, or varied acoustic environments.

Future research should prioritize the development of novel training objectives and architectural innovations that explicitly enforce disentanglement between global and local speech attributes while maintaining low bitrates. One promising direction involves designing hierarchical quantization schemes with dedicated codebooks for speaker identity, linguistic content, and paralinguistic features, coupled with contrastive or adversarial objectives that encourage separation without relying solely on external self-supervised models. This could reduce the dependency on pretrained SSL representations and allow the codec to learn more self-contained, interpretable latent structures. Equally important is advancing semantic grounding through context-aware discretization mechanisms. Rather than treating each frame independently, future models could incorporate temporal attention or memory modules that condition token selection on broader utterance-level context, thereby embedding richer semantic and prosodic information directly into the discrete representations. Multilingual and cross-lingual evaluation should become a standard benchmark, with systematic testing across tonal languages, morphologically rich languages, and code-switching scenarios to ensure that learned tokens generalize beyond the high-resource settings in which they are typically developed. Exploring lightweight adapter modules that fine-tune discrete representations for specific downstream tasks without retraining the entire codec could also bridge the gap between generic speech tokenization and application-specific semantic requirements.

The proposed directions carry significant potential to advance both the theoretical understanding and practical deployment of neural codec-based speech-language models. Improved disentanglement would yield more interpretable and controllable representations, enabling applications such as voice conversion, emotion-preserving speech synthesis, and privacy-preserving speaker anonymization. Stronger semantic grounding in discrete tokens would directly enhance the performance of downstream generative models, including text-to-speech systems and spoken dialogue agents, by providing them with richer, more contextually aware input representations. Robust multilingual validation would democratize access to speech technology, extending the benefits of codec-based modeling to underserved language communities and broadening the impact of spoken language understanding systems. Collectively, these advances would move the field closer to a unified framework where compact, discrete speech tokens serve as a reliable and versatile foundation for the next generation of speech-language models.

# 8 Conclusion

The Conclusion section of this survey has synthesized key developments in neural codec-based speech-language modeling, with particular emphasis on the role of discrete tokenization and disentangled representation learning. The main findings reveal a coherent trajectory in the literature: the encoder-quantizer architecture has become the standard paradigm for compressing speech waveforms into discrete latent sequences, serving as the foundational representation for downstream tasks. Within this framework, recent advances have focused on learning disentangled tokens that separately capture global and local speech characteristics, often by integrating knowledge from self-supervised learning representations. The surveyed works demonstrate that these disentangled discrete tokens exhibit strong representational capacity, as validated through multilingual experiments on speech understanding and emotion recognition. At the same time, the analysis has identified persistent open problems, including the difficulty of jointly modeling hierarchical structures while preserving compactness and generative fidelity, and the unresolved challenge of ensuring that discretized representations retain sufficient semantic and contextual grounding for generative applications. These insights collectively map the current frontier of neural codec research and delineate the technical tensions that continue to shape the field.

The significance of this survey lies in its structured examination of a rapidly evolving subfield at the intersection of neural audio coding and speech-language modeling. By tracing the conceptual lineage from standard discretization pipelines to sophisticated disentanglement frameworks, the survey provides researchers with a clear understanding of how representational choices in the codec directly condition performance on downstream tasks. The explicit identification of open problems—particularly the semantic grounding of discrete tokens and the integration of external SSL knowledge without compromising generative quality—offers a focused agenda for future investigation. Furthermore, the comparative perspective on global and local token disentanglement highlights design principles that may generalize beyond speech to other modalities requiring hierarchical discrete representations. In an era where speech-language models increasingly serve as components in interactive systems, understanding the representational foundations provided by neural codecs is both timely and practically consequential.

Looking forward, the field stands to benefit from concerted efforts to bridge the gap between acoustic compression and semantic representation. Researchers are encouraged to develop evaluation protocols that directly measure the semantic fidelity of discrete tokens, moving beyond reconstruction quality to assess how well these representations support language understanding and generation. Equally important is the exploration of training objectives that jointly optimize codec fidelity and downstream task performance, potentially through multi-task or meta-learning frameworks. The integration of self-supervised representations, while promising, demands careful theoretical and empirical scrutiny to avoid introducing biases or undermining the compactness that makes discrete tokenization attractive in the first place. As the community continues to push toward low-bitrate, semantically rich codecs, collaborative benchmarking efforts and open-source implementations will be essential to ensure reproducible progress. The path ahead is challenging but rich with opportunity, and it is our hope that this survey serves as both a map of the current landscape and a catalyst for the next generation of innovations in neural codec-based speech-language modeling.
