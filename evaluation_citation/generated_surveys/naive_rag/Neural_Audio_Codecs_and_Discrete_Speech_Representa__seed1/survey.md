# 1 Abstract

Neural codec-based speech-language models have emerged as a powerful paradigm for processing continuous speech by discretizing audio signals into compact token sequences, enabling the application of language modeling techniques to spoken language. This survey provides a comprehensive overview of recent advances in neural audio codecs designed for generative speech modeling, with a particular focus on tokenization strategies, representational learning, and downstream task evaluation. We systematically examine encoder-quantizer architectures that compress speech waveforms into discrete latent representations, and analyze frameworks that learn disentangled tokens at multiple granularities by incorporating self-supervised learning representations. Key findings reveal that while low-bitrate codecs achieve impressive compression efficiency, a persistent tension exists between maintaining acoustic fidelity and capturing higher-level semantic content that generalizes across languages and paralinguistic tasks. The survey further identifies critical open challenges, including the integration of semantic-contextual supervision into the discretization pipeline and the validation of token representations across diverse multilingual and emotion recognition benchmarks. These insights highlight the need for codec representations that jointly optimize compression, fidelity, and semantic transferability, pointing to promising directions for future research in speech-language modeling.

# 2 Introduction

The rapid advancement of deep learning has transformed the landscape of speech processing, enabling systems that can synthesize, recognize, and understand spoken language with unprecedented fidelity. Central to this progress is the development of neural audio codecs, which compress continuous audio signals into compact, discrete representations. Unlike traditional signal-processing-based codecs designed primarily for efficient transmission, neural audio codecs learn data-driven representations that can be directly utilized by downstream models. By converting raw waveforms into sequences of discrete tokens, these codecs bridge the gap between continuous speech and discrete language models, opening the door to a new generation of generative speech systems. This paradigm shift has sparked a surge of interest in designing architectures that not only achieve high compression rates but also preserve the linguistic, paralinguistic, and acoustic properties essential for high-quality speech resynthesis and understanding.

Despite these advances, the field faces several fundamental challenges that hinder the broader adoption and effectiveness of neural audio codecs. One core problem is learning discrete representations that are simultaneously compact, disentangled, and semantically meaningful across diverse languages and tasks. Current approaches often struggle to balance the trade-off between bitrate and representational quality, and the resulting tokens may lack the higher-level semantic structure needed for robust speech understanding and emotion recognition. A second, closely related challenge is the integration of contextual and semantic information into the tokenization process. Standard encoder-quantizer pipelines typically prioritize acoustic reconstruction, which can lead to discrete units that capture surface-level signal characteristics while neglecting deeper linguistic content. Furthermore, the generalizability of these representations across multilingual settings and paralinguistic tasks remains an open question, as most existing work evaluates codec tokens on a narrow set of conditions. These intertwined issues define a central tension in the field: how to design neural codecs that achieve efficient compression while producing rich, transferable representations suitable for both generative modeling and discriminative tasks.

This survey provides a comprehensive and structured overview of recent advances in neural audio codecs and discrete speech representations, with a particular focus on the architectural innovations, tokenization strategies, and generative applications that define the current state of the art. Our primary contribution is a systematic taxonomy that organizes existing work along three key dimensions: the design of encoder-quantizer architectures, the methods for learning disentangled and semantically informed discrete tokens, and the integration of these representations into generative speech-language models. By examining frameworks such as low-bitrate codecs that leverage self-supervised learning representations and encoder-quantizer pipelines that form the backbone of modern speech discretization, we highlight both the shared principles and the divergent design choices that characterize contemporary research.

In addition to mapping the architectural landscape, this survey identifies and discusses the persistent open problems that shape the trajectory of the field. We analyze the trade-offs between compression efficiency, acoustic fidelity, and semantic richness, and we examine the extent to which current tokenization methods capture paralinguistic and cross-lingual information. Special attention is given to the underexplored challenge of fusing semantic-contextual supervision into the discretization process, a direction that holds promise for producing more robust and interpretable discrete speech representations. By synthesizing insights from recent empirical studies on multilingual speech understanding and emotion recognition, we delineate the limitations of existing approaches and outline promising avenues for future research.

The aim of this survey is to serve as a unified reference for researchers and practitioners seeking to understand the evolving landscape of neural audio codecs and their role in generative speech modeling. We begin by establishing foundational concepts in neural audio compression and discrete tokenization, followed by a detailed examination of architectural paradigms and training objectives. Subsequent sections explore the application of discrete representations in speech-language models, evaluate representational quality across diverse tasks, and discuss unresolved challenges. By providing a holistic view of the field, this survey seeks to clarify the current state of knowledge and to stimulate further innovation in the design of neural codecs that are both efficient and semantically expressive.

Collectively, these works point to the need for codec representations that balance compression efficiency with rich, transferable semantic content—a tension that defines a central open problem for the field.

# 7 Future Directions

The current landscape of neural codec-based speech-language models is shaped by a persistent tension between compression efficiency and semantic richness. While recent frameworks have made strides in learning compact, discrete representations, they often prioritize acoustic fidelity over higher-level linguistic and paralinguistic content. Approaches that introduce global and local disentanglement or incorporate self-supervised learning representations have begun to bridge this gap, yet the generalizability of these tokens across diverse languages, speakers, and downstream tasks remains incomplete. The standard encoder-quantizer pipeline, though foundational, is not inherently designed to capture semantic structure, contextual nuance, or speaker intent. As a result, existing codec tokens may underperform in tasks that require deep understanding, such as emotion recognition, sarcasm detection, or cross-lingual transfer, revealing a fundamental limitation in how these models represent speech beyond its surface acoustics.

Future research should prioritize the development of semantically grounded tokenization schemes that fuse acoustic compression with contextual and linguistic supervision. One promising direction is the explicit integration of large-scale pretrained language or speech understanding models into the codec training loop, allowing discrete units to encode not only spectral detail but also word-level semantics, prosodic cues, and discourse-level structure. This could be achieved through multi-task objectives that jointly optimize reconstruction fidelity and performance on high-level benchmarks such as intent classification, sentiment analysis, or spoken question answering. Additionally, exploring dynamic tokenization strategies that adapt the granularity of discretization based on the informational density of the speech signal—finer tokens for content-heavy regions and coarser tokens for redundant segments—could yield more efficient and interpretable representations. Cross-lingual and cross-domain generalization must also be explicitly targeted by training on massively multilingual and multi-genre corpora, with evaluation protocols that go beyond monolingual or read-speech benchmarks to include spontaneous, noisy, and low-resource language scenarios.

Advancing along these directions would fundamentally transform the role of neural codecs from low-level compression engines to semantic-aware frontends for spoken language understanding. By producing discrete tokens that are both compact and deeply meaningful, future codecs could enable a new generation of speech-language models that perform robustly across languages, accents, and emotional contexts without task-specific fine-tuning. This would lower the barrier to building inclusive speech interfaces, improve accessibility technologies, and facilitate seamless human-machine communication in multilingual and emotionally nuanced settings. Ultimately, closing the gap between acoustic discretization and semantic representation would unlock the full potential of discrete speech tokens as a universal medium for both generative and understanding tasks in spoken language processing.

# 8 Conclusion

This survey has examined the evolving landscape of neural codec-based speech-language models, with a particular focus on the discretization of continuous speech signals into compact token sequences. The analysis reveals a clear convergence toward encoder-quantizer architectures, in which input waveforms are compressed into latent representations and subsequently quantized into discrete units suitable for language modeling. Central to this paradigm is the tension between compression efficiency and semantic richness, a challenge that recent low-bitrate frameworks address by learning disentangled tokens at multiple temporal granularities and by incorporating knowledge from self-supervised learning representations. The reviewed approaches demonstrate that such tokens can support a range of downstream tasks, including speech understanding and emotion recognition across multilingual settings, yet the findings also underscore persistent limitations in generalizability and in the capacity of purely acoustic discretization to capture higher-level semantic and contextual information.

The significance of this survey lies in its synthesis of current design principles and open challenges within a rapidly advancing subfield of spoken language processing. By tracing the common architectural foundations shared across recent codec proposals and by highlighting the emerging emphasis on semantically informed tokenization, the survey provides a structured understanding of where the field stands and where critical gaps remain. It clarifies that the transition from acoustic compression to semantically grounded discretization is not merely a technical refinement but a fundamental shift that will determine the utility of codec-based models for complex, real-world speech applications. In doing so, the survey offers researchers a conceptual framework for evaluating existing systems and for identifying promising directions for future investigation.

Moving forward, the research community must prioritize the development of codec representations that more tightly integrate semantic and contextual supervision into the tokenization pipeline, moving beyond the limitations of purely acoustic objectives. This will require systematic benchmarking across a broader range of languages, paralinguistic phenomena, and downstream tasks to ensure that advances in compression do not come at the expense of representational depth and transferability. Equally important is the need for interdisciplinary collaboration that bridges signal processing, self-supervised learning, and linguistic theory to inform the design of next-generation codecs. By addressing these challenges, the field can unlock the full potential of discrete speech representations, enabling more robust, interpretable, and versatile speech-language models that operate effectively across diverse communicative contexts.
