# 1 Abstract

Neural audio codecs have emerged as a fundamental technology in modern speech processing, enabling the conversion of continuous speech waveforms into discrete token sequences that power generative speech language models. This survey provides a comprehensive examination of recent advances in neural audio codec architectures, with particular emphasis on representation learning strategies and tokenization mechanisms that bridge the gap between raw audio and discrete symbolic representations. We systematically review approaches that leverage encoder-quantizer paradigms to produce compact discrete representations, focusing on frameworks that incorporate self-supervised learning knowledge to enhance token quality and disentanglement at multiple granularity levels. Key findings reveal a growing convergence toward learning disentangled discrete tokens operating at both global and local levels, with validation across multilingual datasets demonstrating effectiveness in speech understanding and emotion recognition tasks. The survey highlights critical challenges in balancing compression efficiency with representational expressiveness, particularly regarding semantic and contextual fidelity preservation during quantization. These developments underscore the pivotal role of neural codec representations in advancing robust speech-language modeling for diverse downstream applications.

# 2 Introduction

Neural audio codecs have emerged as a transformative technology in modern speech processing, fundamentally reshaping how continuous speech signals are represented and manipulated in computational systems. At their core, these codecs perform a critical function: the conversion of continuous, high-dimensional speech waveforms into compact sequences of discrete tokens. This discretization process enables speech to be treated in a manner analogous to text, opening the door to powerful generative language modeling techniques that have revolutionized natural language processing. By compressing raw audio into symbolic representations, neural codecs bridge the gap between the continuous nature of acoustic signals and the discrete architectures that underpin state-of-the-art generative models. The encoder-quantizer paradigm has become the dominant architectural framework in this domain, wherein an encoder network first compresses the input waveform into a sequence of latent representations, and a subsequent quantization mechanism discretizes these representations into a finite set of codewords. This paradigm has proven remarkably effective, enabling applications ranging from high-fidelity speech synthesis to low-bitrate transmission and serving as the backbone for an emerging class of speech language models that operate directly on learned discrete units.

Despite the significant progress in neural audio codec design, several fundamental challenges remain unresolved. A primary difficulty lies in learning discrete representations that are simultaneously compact enough for efficient transmission and storage, yet sufficiently expressive to capture the rich acoustic, phonetic, and paralinguistic information required for diverse downstream tasks. The tension between compression efficiency and representational fidelity is particularly acute in multilingual and emotionally nuanced speech, where subtle variations carry critical communicative meaning. Furthermore, the design of quantization strategies that preserve semantic and contextual integrity while operating at low bitrates continues to pose open research questions. The challenge of disentanglement—learning discrete tokens that separate global speaker characteristics from local phonetic content, for instance—adds another layer of complexity, as does the integration of knowledge from self-supervised learning representations into the codec training process. These interconnected problems highlight the need for codec architectures that go beyond simple signal reconstruction, instead producing structured, interpretable token sequences suitable for both understanding and generative tasks.

This survey paper makes two primary contributions to the field. First, it provides a comprehensive and structured overview of neural audio codec architectures, with particular emphasis on the encoder-quantizer paradigm and its variants. We systematically examine the design choices involved in building these systems, including encoder network topologies, vector quantization techniques, and training objectives. By organizing the rapidly expanding literature along architectural and methodological axes, we offer researchers a clear map of the design space and the trade-offs inherent in different approaches. Special attention is devoted to recent advances that incorporate self-supervised learning representations and disentanglement objectives, reflecting the field's movement toward more semantically meaningful discrete tokens.

Second, this paper offers an in-depth analysis of tokenization strategies and their implications for generative speech language models. We investigate how different discretization methods—ranging from residual vector quantization to semantic-contextual fusion techniques—affect the quality and utility of the resulting token sequences. The survey synthesizes insights from recent multilingual and emotion recognition evaluations, providing a comparative perspective on the representational capacity of various codec tokens. By connecting architectural decisions to downstream performance, we aim to illuminate the principles that govern effective discrete speech representation and identify promising directions for future research in this rapidly evolving area.

The aim of this survey is to serve as a comprehensive reference for researchers and practitioners interested in neural audio codecs and their role in generative speech modeling. The paper is structured as follows. We begin by establishing the foundational concepts underlying neural audio codecs and discrete speech representations. We then present a detailed taxonomy of codec architectures, examining encoder designs, quantization mechanisms, and training paradigms. Subsequent sections delve into tokenization strategies, covering both traditional and emerging approaches, and explore the integration of these codecs into generative speech language models. We further discuss evaluation methodologies, benchmark datasets, and the performance of state-of-the-art systems across various tasks. The survey concludes by identifying open challenges and outlining potential avenues for future investigation, with the goal of guiding the next generation of research in this dynamic field.

# 7 Future Directions

The current landscape of neural audio codecs for speech-language modeling reveals several persistent limitations that warrant further investigation. Despite recent advances in learning disentangled discrete tokens at multiple granularities, existing frameworks struggle to consistently balance compression efficiency with representational expressiveness across diverse linguistic and paralinguistic phenomena. The validation of token representations remains largely confined to a narrow set of downstream tasks, primarily speech understanding and emotion recognition, leaving their broader applicability underexplored. Moreover, quantization strategies employed in encoder-quantizer architectures often fail to preserve fine-grained semantic and contextual fidelity, particularly when operating at extremely low bitrates. The challenge of designing codec representations that maintain robust performance across multilingual settings, varied acoustic conditions, and heterogeneous downstream applications remains fundamentally unresolved. Current approaches also lack systematic investigation into how different levels of disentanglement interact with specific task requirements, and whether global and local token representations can be dynamically adapted rather than statically assigned.

Future research should prioritize the development of adaptive quantization mechanisms that can dynamically adjust their discretization strategies based on the acoustic and semantic properties of the input signal. Rather than employing fixed codebook sizes or uniform quantization schemes, these mechanisms could learn to allocate representational capacity where it is most needed, preserving fine details for perceptually salient segments while compressing redundant regions more aggressively. This direction could be complemented by exploring hierarchical token structures that explicitly model the relationships between global speaker characteristics, local phonetic content, and paralinguistic cues, enabling more nuanced control over the trade-off between compression and expressiveness. Another promising avenue involves the integration of multimodal supervision signals during codec training, leveraging aligned text, visual, or contextual information to guide the learning of more semantically grounded discrete representations. Additionally, future work should expand the evaluation landscape beyond conventional speech understanding and emotion recognition tasks to include speaker diarization, language identification, pathological speech analysis, and cross-modal generation, thereby establishing more comprehensive benchmarks for assessing token quality.

The proposed directions hold significant potential to advance both the theoretical understanding and practical deployment of neural audio codecs. Adaptive quantization and hierarchical tokenization could dramatically improve the efficiency of speech-language models, enabling their operation on resource-constrained devices without sacrificing generative quality or downstream task performance. By incorporating multimodal supervision, codec representations may become more aligned with human-like semantic understanding, facilitating more natural and context-aware speech interactions. The expansion of evaluation protocols would provide the research community with clearer guidance on the strengths and weaknesses of different tokenization strategies, accelerating progress toward unified codec frameworks that generalize across languages, domains, and applications. Ultimately, these advances could democratize access to high-quality speech processing technologies, supporting applications ranging from low-resource language preservation to assistive communication devices, while simultaneously reducing the computational footprint of state-of-the-art speech-language models.

# 8 Conclusion

The conclusion of this survey synthesizes the central developments and ongoing challenges in the design of neural audio codecs for speech-language modeling. The main findings reveal a clear convergence toward learning compact, discrete representations that convert continuous speech waveforms into token sequences suitable for generative tasks. Recent frameworks emphasize the importance of disentangling information at multiple granularities, with low-bitrate architectures incorporating self-supervised learning representations to capture both global and local speech characteristics. The survey highlights that representational quality is validated through multilingual and multi-task evaluations, including speech understanding and emotion recognition, demonstrating the versatility of these discrete tokens. Furthermore, the encoder-quantizer paradigm remains foundational, yet the challenge of preserving semantic and contextual fidelity during quantization persists as a critical open problem. These discussions underscore that the field is actively balancing compression efficiency with the expressive power needed for robust downstream performance.

The significance of this survey lies in its structured examination of how discrete tokenization strategies are reshaping the landscape of speech processing. By tracing the evolution from basic encoder-quantizer architectures to advanced frameworks that integrate self-supervised knowledge, the survey provides a clear map of the technical trajectory and identifies the core tensions that drive current research. It foregrounds the dual demand for compactness and expressiveness, a tension that is central to the practical deployment of speech-language models in real-world, multilingual, and emotionally aware applications. The survey further contributes by framing the validation of representational capacity not as a secondary concern but as a primary design criterion, thereby guiding future work toward more rigorous and task-diverse evaluation protocols. In doing so, it offers a cohesive perspective that connects codec design choices with their downstream impact on generative speech modeling.

Looking forward, the field must continue to address the unresolved challenge of designing quantization mechanisms that do not sacrifice the rich, disentangled information necessary for nuanced speech understanding and generation. Future research should prioritize the development of codec architectures that are inherently interpretable and adaptable across a broader spectrum of languages, speaking styles, and emotional contexts. There is a pressing need for standardized benchmarks that go beyond traditional metrics to capture semantic fidelity, speaker identity preservation, and paralinguistic detail in discrete token sequences. As neural audio codecs become increasingly integrated into large-scale generative systems, the community must also attend to the ethical implications of highly compressed speech representations, ensuring that they do not amplify biases or erode privacy. Ultimately, sustained progress will depend on interdisciplinary collaboration that bridges signal processing, representation learning, and linguistic theory, fostering codecs that are not only efficient but also truly understanding-oriented.
