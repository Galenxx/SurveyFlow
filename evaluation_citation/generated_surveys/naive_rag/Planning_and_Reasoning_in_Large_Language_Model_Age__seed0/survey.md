# 1 Abstract

The emergence of autonomous agents powered by large language models has opened new frontiers in artificial intelligence, enabling systems that can perceive, reason, and act within complex environments. Central to these capabilities is planning, a multifaceted cognitive process that demands sophisticated understanding, logical reasoning, and sequential decision-making. This survey provides a comprehensive and structured analysis of LLM-based autonomous agents through the specific lens of planning abilities, systematically categorizing existing research into five principal directions. We examine foundational techniques that enhance reasoning interpretability and reliability, including rationale-based approaches that leverage human-annotated data and chain-of-thought methods that generate explicit textual explanations of inference steps, as well as recent advances aimed at strengthening the robustness of these reasoning processes. Beyond planning-centric evaluations, we also consider broader benchmarking efforts that assess LLM agents across multiple real-world dimensions such as professional domain adaptability and practical application performance. Our analysis reveals persistent challenges in achieving consistent and transparent reasoning across diverse environments, highlighting the need to move beyond few-shot demonstrations toward more robust planning frameworks. This survey synthesizes current progress, identifies open problems, and outlines promising directions for developing autonomous agents with more reliable and interpretable planning capabilities.

# 2 Introduction

Planning and reasoning are fundamental pillars of intelligent behavior, enabling autonomous agents to perceive dynamic environments, deliberate over possible courses of action, and execute tasks with precision. The emergence of large language models has dramatically reshaped the landscape of artificial intelligence, endowing systems with unprecedented capabilities in natural language understanding, generation, and interaction. Within this transformative shift, a growing body of research has focused on embedding large language models into autonomous agent architectures, where they serve as the cognitive core responsible for interpreting complex instructions, maintaining contextual awareness, and producing coherent action sequences. Planning, in particular, has emerged as a critical competency that distinguishes simple reactive systems from genuinely autonomous agents, as it demands sophisticated reasoning, long-horizon foresight, and adaptive decision-making under uncertainty. The intersection of planning and reasoning in large language model agents thus represents a vibrant and rapidly evolving research frontier, drawing insights from classical artificial intelligence, cognitive science, and machine learning to build agents capable of robust real-world operation.

Despite the impressive progress, several fundamental challenges persist in the design and deployment of planning-capable large language model agents. One central problem concerns the interpretability and reliability of the reasoning processes that underpin planning. While chain-of-thought prompting and rationale-based generation have enabled models to articulate intermediate reasoning steps, these explanations do not always faithfully reflect the model’s actual decision mechanisms, raising concerns about transparency and trustworthiness. A second challenge involves the integration of external tools and environmental feedback into the planning loop, where agents must not only reason internally but also ground their plans in actionable interactions with digital or physical tools. Furthermore, existing evaluation methodologies often focus on narrow, synthetic benchmarks that fail to capture the multi-dimensional demands of real-world applications, such as adaptability to professional domains, robustness to distributional shifts, and consistent performance across diverse task categories. These gaps highlight the need for a systematic examination of how planning methods, tool-use strategies, and evaluation frameworks collectively shape the capabilities and limitations of large language model agents.

This survey makes two primary contributions to the field. First, it provides a structured and comprehensive taxonomy of planning and reasoning methods employed in large language model agents, organizing the literature along key dimensions including chain-of-thought and rationale-based reasoning, plan generation and refinement strategies, and the integration of external tools for grounded action execution. By categorizing existing approaches into coherent methodological streams, the survey clarifies the relationships between seemingly disparate techniques and identifies the underlying principles that govern effective agent planning. This taxonomy not only serves as a navigational aid for researchers entering the field but also highlights underexplored areas ripe for future investigation, such as the unification of reasoning transparency with tool-augmented execution and the development of more robust plan verification mechanisms.

Second, the survey offers a critical analysis of evaluation benchmarks and metrics used to assess planning and reasoning in large language model agents, spanning both narrow task-specific evaluations and broader real-world application contexts. By examining how different benchmarks operationalize planning success, the survey reveals significant gaps between current evaluation practices and the multifaceted nature of autonomous agent performance. It further discusses the implications of these gaps for the development of agents that must operate reliably in complex, dynamic environments where planning failures carry meaningful consequences. Through this dual focus on methods and evaluation, the survey aims to foster a more integrated understanding of how planning capabilities can be systematically advanced and rigorously assessed.

The aim of this survey is to provide researchers and practitioners with a clear, structured overview of the current state of planning and reasoning in large language model agents, encompassing core methodologies, tool-use paradigms, and evaluation benchmarks. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. We begin by formalizing the conceptual foundations of planning and reasoning in the context of large language model agents, establishing a shared vocabulary and framing the key research questions that motivate the field. We then present our taxonomy of planning methods, examining rationale-based reasoning, chain-of-thought variants, and hybrid approaches that combine internal deliberation with external tool invocation. Subsequently, we survey the landscape of evaluation benchmarks, analyzing their design principles, coverage, and limitations. Finally, we synthesize insights from the literature to identify open challenges and promising directions for future research, with the goal of guiding the development of more capable, transparent, and reliable autonomous agents.

# 7 Future Directions

Current work on LLM-based autonomous agents has made meaningful progress in planning, reasoning, and task execution, yet several fundamental limitations persist. The reasoning processes that underlie agent planning often lack transparency and reliability, even when augmented with rationale-based or chain-of-thought methods. These approaches can generate plausible-sounding explanations, but they do not guarantee that the internal decision pathway is faithful to the model’s actual computation. Moreover, existing agents frequently rely on few-shot demonstrations or narrowly scoped prompts, which makes their planning brittle when transferred to dynamic, real-world environments. The gap between controlled benchmarks and practical deployment remains wide, as agents struggle to maintain consistent performance across diverse domains, adapt to novel situations without catastrophic failures, and provide verifiable guarantees about their reasoning. Without addressing these gaps, the deployment of autonomous agents in high-stakes settings will remain limited.

Future research should prioritize the development of inherently interpretable planning architectures that move beyond post-hoc explanation toward verifiable reasoning traces. This could involve designing agent frameworks where planning steps are not only expressed in natural language but also grounded in formal, machine-verifiable representations such as symbolic logic, causal graphs, or constraint-based models. By coupling the generative flexibility of large language models with structured reasoning substrates, agents could produce plans that are auditable, debuggable, and resistant to hallucinated justifications. A second critical direction involves building agents that learn to plan from sparse, multimodal, and continuously evolving real-world feedback rather than static few-shot examples. This requires advances in online adaptation, where agents refine their planning strategies through interaction, detect distribution shifts, and self-correct before committing to unsafe actions. Research should also explore cross-domain generalization, enabling a single agent to reason fluently across professional fields, cultural contexts, and physical environments without extensive retraining. Finally, evaluation frameworks must evolve to measure not just task success but also reasoning fidelity, robustness under adversarial perturbations, and alignment with human values over extended time horizons.

Pursuing these directions would fundamentally reshape the trustworthiness and utility of autonomous agents. Verifiable planning architectures would make agent behavior auditable in regulated domains such as healthcare, law, and finance, where opaque reasoning is unacceptable. Agents capable of robust real-world adaptation could operate reliably in assistive technologies, scientific discovery pipelines, and infrastructure management, reducing the need for constant human oversight. Improved cross-domain generalization would lower the barrier to deploying intelligent agents in low-resource settings and interdisciplinary applications. Collectively, these advances would accelerate the transition from agents that merely perform well in curated demonstrations to systems that earn sustained human trust through transparent, resilient, and ethically aligned planning in the open world.

# 8 Conclusion

This survey has provided a structured examination of large language model (LLM)-based autonomous agents, with a particular emphasis on the central role of planning. The analysis reveals that planning is not merely a peripheral function but constitutes the cognitive backbone that integrates perception, reasoning, and execution within these intelligent systems. Through a systematic categorization of the literature, the survey highlights five principal research directions that collectively shape the current landscape. Foundational to these directions are efforts to enhance the interpretability and reliability of model reasoning, with rationale-based approaches and chain-of-thought techniques emerging as pivotal methods for rendering internal inference steps more transparent. The review further underscores a discernible shift in evaluation paradigms, moving beyond isolated benchmarks toward multi-dimensional assessments that probe adaptability, robustness, and performance in authentic professional and real-world contexts. Collectively, the findings indicate that while substantial progress has been made in equipping agents with sophisticated planning abilities, persistent challenges remain in ensuring consistent, failure-resistant reasoning across diverse and dynamic environments.

The significance of this survey lies in its focused synthesis of planning as the defining competency of LLM-based autonomous agents. By consolidating fragmented research threads into a coherent analytical framework, the survey clarifies the conceptual boundaries and technical underpinnings of agent planning, offering a reference point for both newcomers and established researchers. It draws attention to the critical interplay between reasoning transparency and practical deployment, a relationship that is often underexplored in benchmark-driven studies. Furthermore, the survey captures a pivotal moment in the evolution of the field, where the emphasis is transitioning from narrow task execution to holistic, real-world applicability. In doing so, it not only maps the state of the art but also implicitly identifies the gaps that must be bridged to achieve truly autonomous and trustworthy agents. This integrative perspective is essential for guiding future inquiry and for aligning technical innovation with the demands of complex application domains.

Moving forward, the research community must prioritize the development of planning mechanisms that are not only powerful but also inherently robust, interpretable, and generalizable. The path ahead requires transcending reliance on few-shot demonstrations and static benchmarks, instead embracing evaluation protocols that stress-test agents in open-ended, unpredictable settings. Advancing the field will depend on a concerted effort to fuse the transparency afforded by rationale-based reasoning with the adaptability needed for real-world grounding. Researchers are called upon to design agents whose planning processes can be audited, trusted, and seamlessly integrated into high-stakes professional environments. Ultimately, the maturation of LLM-based autonomous agents hinges on a collective commitment to building systems that reason with clarity and resilience, transforming them from impressive prototypes into indispensable collaborators in human endeavors.
