# 1 Abstract

Autonomous agents have emerged as intelligent systems capable of perceiving environments, planning, and executing actions to accomplish complex tasks, with planning standing out as a particularly critical capability that demands sophisticated understanding, reasoning, and decision-making. This survey provides a comprehensive and structured analysis of large language model-based autonomous agents, specifically examining them through the lens of planning abilities. We systematically categorize existing works into five main streams and evaluate representative methods across multiple benchmarks, offering the first dedicated synthesis of this rapidly evolving research area. Our analysis reveals that while chain-of-thought reasoning and rationale-based methods have significantly advanced the capacity of agents to generate textual explanations and make informed decisions, the robustness and reliability of these planning processes remain open challenges. Furthermore, we examine emerging evaluation paradigms that assess agent performance in dynamic multi-agent environments, emphasizing dimensions such as adaptability to professional domains, real-world applicability, and resilience in complex situated contexts. The survey highlights that advancing agent planning from its current state to reliably handle intricate, multi-step tasks represents a key frontier for the field, requiring continued innovation in both reasoning methodologies and evaluation frameworks.

# 2 Introduction

The emergence of large language models has fundamentally transformed the landscape of artificial intelligence, enabling machines to process and generate human-like text with unprecedented sophistication. These models, trained on vast corpora of diverse data, have demonstrated remarkable capabilities that extend far beyond simple text completion, encompassing complex reasoning, contextual understanding, and creative problem-solving. As the field has matured, researchers have increasingly sought to embed these powerful language models within autonomous agent architectures—intelligent systems that can perceive their environment, formulate goals, and execute actions to accomplish specific tasks. This shift from passive language processing to active, goal-directed behavior represents a pivotal evolution in artificial intelligence research, with implications spanning robotics, virtual assistants, scientific discovery, and numerous other domains. The development of such agents requires the integration of multiple cognitive capabilities, including perception, memory, reasoning, and perhaps most critically, planning—the ability to devise sequences of actions that transform an initial state into a desired goal state while navigating constraints and contingencies.

Despite the impressive advances in large language model capabilities, several fundamental challenges persist in the development of truly autonomous agents. The primary obstacle lies in the inherent complexity of planning, which demands sophisticated understanding of causal relationships, temporal reasoning, and the ability to evaluate multiple hypothetical action sequences before committing to a course of action. Current approaches struggle with the robustness and reliability of reasoning processes, particularly when agents must handle intricate, multi-step tasks that require maintaining coherence across extended planning horizons. Chain-of-thought reasoning methods have emerged as a promising direction, enabling models to generate intermediate textual explanations that illuminate their decision-making processes, yet these approaches remain vulnerable to logical inconsistencies, hallucinated facts, and failures in compositional reasoning. Furthermore, the integration of external tools and resources—essential for grounding agent actions in real-world contexts—introduces additional layers of complexity in both planning and execution. The evaluation of agent performance presents its own set of challenges, as traditional benchmarks often fail to capture the multifaceted nature of autonomous behavior in dynamic, situated environments. These interconnected problems underscore the pressing need for systematic analysis and comprehensive understanding of the current state of planning and reasoning in large language model-based agents.

This survey makes several significant contributions to the field of autonomous agent research. First, it provides the first comprehensive analysis of large language model-based agents specifically through the lens of planning capabilities, offering a structured taxonomy that categorizes existing work into five distinct methodological streams. By organizing the rapidly expanding literature along clear conceptual dimensions, this survey enables researchers to identify the relationships between different approaches, understand the historical development of key ideas, and recognize promising directions for future investigation. The taxonomy encompasses rationale-based methods that leverage human-annotated data to explain model outputs, chain-of-thought reasoning approaches that facilitate the generation of textual explanations, and recent innovations designed to enhance the robustness and reliability of these reasoning processes. This systematic organization reveals both the diversity of current methodologies and the underlying commonalities that unite seemingly disparate research threads.

Second, this survey advances the understanding of agent evaluation by examining representative methods across multiple benchmarks and assessment dimensions. Beyond isolated task performance, the analysis considers adaptability to professional domains, real-world applicability, and robustness in dynamic multi-agent environments. This comprehensive evaluation perspective reflects the growing recognition that autonomous agents must be assessed not merely on their ability to complete discrete tasks, but on their capacity to function effectively in complex, situated contexts that more closely mirror the challenges of practical deployment. By synthesizing insights from diverse evaluation paradigms—including those that examine agent behavior in competitive and cooperative multi-agent settings—this survey provides a holistic view of current capabilities and limitations. The analysis highlights critical gaps between laboratory performance and real-world requirements, identifying specific areas where methodological advances are most urgently needed to bridge this divide.

The aim of this survey is to provide researchers and practitioners with a thorough understanding of the current landscape of planning and reasoning in large language model-based agents, encompassing both methodological innovations and evaluation frameworks. The paper is structured as follows: it begins by establishing foundational concepts and defining the scope of planning within autonomous agent architectures, then proceeds to systematically examine the five main streams of existing work, analyzing their theoretical underpinnings, practical implementations, and empirical performance. Subsequent sections explore the role of tool use in extending agent capabilities and the various benchmarks developed for evaluating planning and reasoning abilities. The survey concludes by identifying open challenges and promising research directions, offering a roadmap for future advances in this rapidly evolving field.

# 7 Future Directions

The current landscape of LLM-based autonomous agents reveals persistent limitations in planning robustness, despite advances in chain-of-thought reasoning and rationale-based explanation methods. While these agents can generate plausible textual explanations and handle structured reasoning tasks, they frequently falter when confronted with multi-step problems requiring deep causal understanding, long-horizon consistency, or adaptation to novel constraints. The gap between generating coherent reasoning traces and executing reliable plans in dynamic, real-world environments remains substantial. Additionally, existing evaluation frameworks tend to assess planning in controlled or narrowly scoped settings, leaving open questions about how agents perform under the ambiguity, partial observability, and shifting goals characteristic of situated, interactive contexts. The field lacks systematic understanding of failure modes in complex planning scenarios and has yet to establish benchmarks that capture the full spectrum of challenges agents face when deployed outside laboratory conditions.

Future research should prioritize developing planning architectures that integrate multiple reasoning modalities rather than relying solely on sequential text generation. Combining chain-of-thought approaches with structured world models, symbolic planning components, and memory-augmented retrieval could yield more robust decision-making pipelines. These hybrid systems would benefit from explicit mechanisms for detecting and recovering from reasoning errors, enabling agents to revise plans when intermediate steps prove inconsistent or when environmental feedback contradicts initial assumptions. A second critical direction involves creating more ecologically valid evaluation frameworks that assess planning across diverse, dynamic, and multi-agent scenarios. Such benchmarks should measure not only task completion rates but also adaptability to unexpected perturbations, efficiency under resource constraints, and the ability to maintain coherent long-term objectives while handling competing sub-goals. Research should also investigate how agents can learn to calibrate their planning depth based on task complexity and uncertainty, avoiding both over-planning in simple situations and insufficient deliberation in high-stakes contexts.

Advancing planning capabilities along these dimensions would fundamentally expand the practical utility of autonomous agents. More robust planning architectures would enable reliable deployment in domains such as healthcare coordination, legal reasoning, scientific discovery, and autonomous robotics, where errors carry significant consequences. Improved evaluation frameworks would accelerate progress by providing clearer signals about which approaches genuinely advance the field rather than overfitting to narrow benchmarks. Ultimately, agents capable of sophisticated, adaptive planning could serve as collaborative partners in complex intellectual work, augmenting human decision-making in ways that current systems cannot reliably achieve. This progression would mark a meaningful step from tools that assist with isolated cognitive tasks toward systems that can independently navigate the intricate, multi-step challenges that define real-world problem-solving.

# 8 Conclusion

This survey has provided a structured examination of planning as a foundational capability for LLM-based autonomous agents, revealing a research landscape marked by rapid innovation and persistent challenges. The analysis has traced the evolution from rationale-based methods and chain-of-thought prompting to more sophisticated reasoning frameworks, highlighting how these approaches enable agents to decompose complex tasks, generate interpretable action sequences, and adapt to dynamic environments. A central finding is that while current techniques have substantially improved the coherence and logical structure of agent-generated plans, the robustness and reliability of these planning processes remain incomplete. The categorization of existing work into distinct methodological streams has illuminated both the diversity of strategies employed and the common bottlenecks they encounter, particularly in handling multi-step dependencies, recovering from erroneous intermediate steps, and maintaining consistency across extended planning horizons. Furthermore, the evaluation of representative methods across multiple benchmarks has demonstrated that performance varies considerably depending on task complexity, environmental dynamics, and the specific reasoning demands imposed on the agent.

The significance of this survey lies in its explicit framing of planning as the lens through which LLM-based agent capabilities should be understood and assessed. By consolidating fragmented research threads into a coherent taxonomy, the survey offers researchers a systematic reference point for situating new contributions and identifying under-explored areas. The emphasis on planning as a composite of understanding, reasoning, and decision-making provides a conceptual scaffold that can guide the design of next-generation agents, encouraging a shift away from treating these dimensions in isolation toward more integrated architectures. For practitioners seeking to deploy autonomous agents in real-world settings, the survey clarifies which planning paradigms are most suited to particular application profiles and what failure modes to anticipate. The parallel consideration of multi-agent evaluation frameworks further underscores the practical importance of benchmarking agents not merely on static tasks but on their capacity to function adaptively in situated, interactive contexts.

Moving forward, the field must confront the gap between the impressive yet brittle planning behaviors exhibited by current agents and the robust, generalizable reasoning required for reliable autonomy. Researchers are encouraged to prioritize investigations into error recovery mechanisms, plan verification strategies, and methods for maintaining reasoning fidelity over long sequences of interdependent actions. The development of more rigorous and ecologically valid benchmarks, particularly those that capture the open-ended and collaborative nature of real-world tasks, will be essential for measuring genuine progress. Equally important is the cultivation of interdisciplinary dialogue between the natural language processing, robotics, and cognitive science communities, as insights from each domain can inform more principled approaches to agent planning. Ultimately, the maturation of LLM-based autonomous agents depends on sustained collective effort to transform planning from a source of fragility into a pillar of dependable intelligence.
