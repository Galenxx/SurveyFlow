# 1 Abstract

The integration of affective computing into remote and hybrid work environments has catalyzed a growing interest in unobtrusively sensing worker cognitive load and emotional states through audio-visual signals. This survey provides a structured examination of recent advances in multimodal sensing methodologies designed for ecologically valid distributed collaboration settings. We systematically review emerging datasets that combine video conferencing recordings with dual annotations of cognitive load and affect, highlighting a critical methodological shift toward naturalistic task designs that elicit genuine psychological responses during collaborative activities. A central contribution of this analysis is the identification of persistent gaps in the literature concerning the socio-demographic and cross-cultural determinants that shape worker attitudes toward emotional artificial intelligence, revealing that current sensing models are predominantly developed and validated within narrow demographic boundaries. The survey further synthesizes ethical considerations and worker-centered perspectives, articulating the need for fairness-aware algorithms and inclusive dataset collection that account for diverse populations. By bridging technical capabilities with human and societal dimensions, this work underscores the imperative of developing sensing systems that are not only robust and generalizable but also equitable, transparent, and aligned with the values of a global workforce.


# 2 Introduction

The rapid digital transformation of the workplace, accelerated by the global shift toward remote and hybrid work models, has fundamentally altered how organizations monitor, manage, and support their employees. In parallel, advances in affective computing—the interdisciplinary field concerned with the detection, interpretation, and simulation of human emotions—have opened new frontiers for understanding worker states in distributed environments. Remote workplace monitoring now extends beyond traditional productivity metrics to encompass the sensing of cognitive load, emotional well-being, and affective states through audio-visual signals captured during everyday work activities. This convergence of remote work infrastructure and affective sensing technologies promises enhanced support for employee mental health, more adaptive collaboration tools, and improved team dynamics. However, it also raises profound questions about privacy, consent, and the ethical boundaries of emotional surveillance in professional settings. As organizations increasingly deploy tools capable of inferring internal states from facial expressions, vocal patterns, and behavioral cues, the need for a comprehensive understanding of the technological landscape, its capabilities, and its societal implications becomes ever more pressing.

Despite the growing body of research in affective computing, several critical challenges remain unresolved in the context of remote workplace monitoring. First, the technical problem of reliably sensing and inferring cognitive load and affective states in ecologically valid, real-world settings presents significant difficulties. Laboratory-based studies often fail to capture the noisy, unconstrained conditions of home offices, where variable lighting, background noise, and diverse hardware configurations complicate signal acquisition. Second, the inference models themselves face questions of accuracy, generalizability, and bias across different demographic groups and cultural contexts. Third, the ethical dimensions of deploying such technologies in the workplace remain underexplored, particularly regarding the tension between organizational interests in productivity monitoring and individual rights to emotional privacy. Furthermore, worker attitudes toward emotional artificial intelligence are shaped by socio-demographic and cross-cultural factors that are not yet fully understood, complicating the design of universally acceptable monitoring frameworks. These interconnected problems demand a systematic examination that bridges the technical, human-centric, and ethical dimensions of affective computing for remote work.

This survey paper makes three primary contributions to the field. First, it provides a structured and comprehensive review of the sensing modalities employed in remote workplace affective computing, with particular emphasis on audio-visual approaches that have demonstrated feasibility in ecologically valid settings. By examining the types of signals captured—ranging from facial expressions and eye gaze to vocal prosody and speech patterns—this paper delineates the current state of the art in unobtrusive affective sensing during distributed collaboration. The review encompasses both the hardware and software pipelines that enable continuous monitoring without disrupting natural work activities, offering researchers and practitioners a clear map of available techniques and their respective strengths and limitations. Special attention is devoted to recent multimodal datasets that combine video conferencing recordings with rich annotations of cognitive load and affective states, highlighting the methodological shift toward naturalistic task designs that elicit genuine psychological responses during collaborative activities.

Second, this paper critically analyzes the inference methods used to translate raw sensor data into meaningful estimates of cognitive load and affective states. Beyond merely cataloging machine learning architectures, the survey interrogates the validity and reliability of these inference models when applied to diverse populations and uncontrolled environments. The analysis examines the challenges of annotation, ground truth establishment, and the inherent subjectivity of emotional labeling, while also assessing how recent datasets designed for authentic remote work tasks are advancing the development of more robust and generalizable models. The third contribution lies in the paper’s thorough exploration of the ethical considerations and worker-centered perspectives that must accompany any technological deployment. The survey investigates the socio-demographic and cross-cultural determinants that shape worker attitudes toward emotional artificial intelligence, recognizing that acceptance and impact vary significantly across different populations. By synthesizing findings on privacy concerns, consent mechanisms, and the potential for discriminatory outcomes, the paper articulates a framework for responsible innovation in this domain, treating ethical analysis not as an afterthought but as an integral component of the technological review.

# 5 Future Directions

The current body of research on audio-visual sensing of cognitive load and affect in ecologically valid settings remains constrained by several critical limitations. Most existing datasets, while valuable, are collected within narrow demographic and cultural boundaries, limiting the generalizability of sensing models. The reliance on homogeneous participant pools obscures how socio-demographic variables such as age, gender, education, and cultural background shape both the expression and perception of cognitive and affective states. Furthermore, the absence of cross-cultural comparisons means that models developed in one context may fail or introduce bias when deployed globally. The lack of integrated evidence on worker attitudes toward emotional artificial intelligence compounds this issue, leaving a significant gap in understanding how acceptance, trust, and perceived fairness vary across populations and influence the real-world adoption of these technologies.

Future research should prioritize the collection of large-scale, demographically diverse multimodal datasets that explicitly sample across multiple cultural contexts, socioeconomic strata, and occupational roles. Such efforts must move beyond convenience samples to include participants from underrepresented regions and non-Western cultures, ensuring that variations in emotional expression, communication norms, and attitudes toward monitoring are captured. Longitudinal studies are also needed to understand how cognitive load and affective responses evolve over extended periods of remote and hybrid work, rather than in isolated experimental sessions. On the modeling side, researchers should develop fairness-aware algorithms that can detect and mitigate performance disparities across demographic groups, moving toward inclusive sensing systems that do not amplify existing inequalities. Additionally, qualitative and mixed-methods investigations into worker perceptions of emotional artificial intelligence should be conducted alongside technical development, directly examining how trust, privacy concerns, and cultural values mediate acceptance. This dual focus on technical robustness and human factors will be essential for creating systems that are both accurate and socially sustainable.

The proposed directions carry significant potential to transform the field. Building inclusive and culturally aware sensing systems can enable more equitable workplace technologies that support employee well-being across diverse global teams, rather than benefiting only a narrow subset of users. Addressing the socio-demographic and cross-cultural determinants of attitudes toward emotional artificial intelligence will help organizations deploy these tools responsibly, fostering transparency and trust rather than resistance. Ultimately, this line of work can bridge the gap between laboratory-validated models and real-world applications, ensuring that the next generation of cognitive and affective sensing technologies is grounded in a robust understanding of human diversity.


# 6 Conclusion

**Conclusion**

This survey has examined the current state of audio-visual sensing technologies for capturing cognitive load and affective states in ecologically valid remote work environments. The primary finding reveals a significant methodological evolution toward multimodal datasets that prioritize naturalistic task designs and authentic collaborative contexts. Recent efforts have demonstrated the feasibility of collecting rich audio-visual recordings during distributed work activities—including open discussions and collaborative decision-making exercises—while simultaneously gathering annotations for both cognitive load and affective dimensions. This dual annotation approach represents a meaningful advancement, enabling the development of models that can disentangle the often intertwined constructs of mental workload and emotional state. The survey further reveals that the field has made considerable progress in capturing complex mental states through video conferencing platforms, reflecting a broader recognition that modern workplaces demand sensing solutions capable of operating in distributed, technology-mediated settings. However, critical gaps persist in understanding how socio-demographic and cross-cultural factors shape worker attitudes toward emotional artificial intelligence, with existing research constrained by narrow demographic sampling and limited cross-cultural comparisons. These limitations underscore the need for more inclusive and representative investigations that account for the diverse populations comprising the global workforce.

The significance of this survey lies in its integrated analysis of technical progress and human-centered considerations within affective computing for workplace settings. By documenting the shift toward ecologically valid multimodal datasets, this work provides researchers and practitioners with a clear understanding of the methodological standards necessary for building robust, generalizable sensing systems. The emphasis on naturalistic task design and comprehensive annotation frameworks offers a blueprint for future data collection efforts seeking to bridge the persistent gap between laboratory performance and real-world applicability. Equally important, by foregrounding the socio-demographic and cross-cultural dimensions of worker attitudes toward emotional artificial intelligence, the survey articulates the ethical imperative of developing sensing technologies that are not only technically accurate but also equitable and culturally informed. As organizations increasingly explore affective computing for productivity monitoring, well-being assessment, and team dynamics analysis, the findings synthesized here serve as both a technical foundation and a cautionary framework, emphasizing that sensing accuracy cannot be pursued in isolation from considerations of fairness, transparency, and worker acceptance.

Moving forward, the research community must prioritize the creation of large-scale, demographically diverse multimodal datasets that explicitly sample across multiple cultural contexts, socioeconomic strata, and occupational roles. Such efforts should move beyond convenience samples to include participants from underrepresented regions and non-Western cultures, ensuring that variations in emotional expression, communication norms, and attitudes toward monitoring are adequately captured. Future work should also focus on developing fairness-aware algorithms capable of detecting and mitigating performance disparities across demographic groups, alongside transparent and privacy-preserving sensing architectures that can earn worker trust. The integration of participatory design approaches, wherein workers themselves contribute to defining appropriate sensing boundaries and acceptable use cases, will be essential for aligning technological capabilities with societal values. Longitudinal studies examining how cognitive load and affective responses evolve over extended periods of remote and hybrid work are equally necessary. Ultimately, the goal should not be to maximize sensing accuracy at any cost, but to cultivate a human-centered paradigm in which audio-visual sensing enhances collaboration, supports mental well-being, and respects the dignity of every individual in the digital workplace.
