# 1 Abstract

The shift toward remote and hybrid work arrangements has intensified interest in the role of cognitive load and affective states in shaping collaboration, well-being, and productivity within distributed teams. Audio-visual channels, as the primary conduits of communication in video conferencing, offer a rich yet technically challenging lens through which these internal states can be studied. This survey provides a structured examination of the emerging landscape of multimodal datasets and methodologies specifically designed to capture and analyze cognitive and emotional phenomena in remote work settings, with an emphasis on resources that enable naturalistic, interaction-based research. Our analysis reveals that while purpose-built datasets have recently been developed to address this domain, the field remains constrained by a scarcity of richly annotated, ecologically valid resources that encompass diverse task types, participant demographics, and real-world collaboration scenarios. We identify critical methodological considerations for future dataset design, including the need for transparent annotation frameworks that jointly address cognitive and affective dimensions, the importance of larger and more representative participant samples, and the integration of cross-cultural perspectives that remain notably absent from current resources. The findings underscore that progress in computational modeling of cognitive load and affect recognition for distributed work is fundamentally dependent on the creation of such purpose-built, multimodal corpora.


# 2 Introduction

The rapid digital transformation of modern workplaces has fundamentally altered the nature of collaboration, communication, and organizational management. Remote and hybrid work arrangements, once peripheral practices, have become central to the global economy, driven by technological advances in video conferencing, cloud-based productivity tools, and high-speed connectivity. This shift has generated unprecedented interest in understanding how employees experience their work in distributed environments, particularly their cognitive load, stress levels, engagement, and emotional well-being. Affective computing, an interdisciplinary field dedicated to the detection, interpretation, and simulation of human emotions, has emerged as a promising lens through which to address these questions. By leveraging multimodal data streams—including audio, video, text, and physiological signals—affective computing systems aim to infer internal states that are otherwise invisible in remote interactions. These technologies hold the potential to support managerial decision-making, enhance employee mental health, and optimize collaborative workflows. Yet their deployment in workplace settings also raises profound ethical concerns related to privacy, consent, fairness, and the normalization of continuous emotional surveillance, placing affective computing at a critical juncture between technological promise and societal risk.

The application of affective computing to remote workplace monitoring is fraught with unresolved challenges that span technical, methodological, and ethical domains. From a sensing perspective, the modalities available in typical remote work settings—primarily audio and video captured through standard conferencing platforms—suffer from significant quality constraints, including variable lighting, background noise, compression artifacts, and the absence of specialized physiological sensors. These limitations complicate the accurate capture of subtle affective cues that are essential for reliable state inference. From an inferential standpoint, existing models for detecting cognitive load and emotional states often struggle to generalize across diverse populations, task types, and cultural contexts, raising concerns about their robustness and fairness. A further critical obstacle is the scarcity of purpose-built multimodal datasets that reflect the specific conditions of remote collaboration, which hampers both model development and rigorous validation. Finally, the ethical landscape remains inadequately charted: there is limited understanding of how demographic and cultural differences shape employee attitudes toward emotional artificial intelligence, and regulatory frameworks have yet to catch up with the capabilities of these emerging technologies.

This survey paper makes two primary contributions to the field of affective computing for remote workplace monitoring. First, it provides a systematic and integrated review of the sensing modalities and inference techniques currently employed in this domain. The paper examines audio, visual, and multimodal approaches, synthesizing the state of the art in detecting cognitive load, stress, engagement, and emotional valence from data streams commonly available in video conferencing and digital collaboration platforms. Particular attention is devoted to the technical limitations imposed by real-world remote work conditions and to the critical role of datasets in advancing empirical research. The review includes a detailed examination of available resources, such as the AVCAffe dataset, which was explicitly designed to capture cognitive load and affect in remote collaboration scenarios involving over one hundred participants engaged in realistic tasks. By cataloging and analyzing existing datasets, the survey identifies significant gaps in annotation practices, participant diversity, and ecological validity that must be addressed to enable meaningful progress.

Second, the paper offers a thorough examination of the ethical, social, and organizational implications of deploying affective computing systems for workplace monitoring. This includes an analysis of privacy risks associated with continuous emotional surveillance, the potential for algorithmic bias to disproportionately impact marginalized groups, and the challenges of obtaining meaningful consent within hierarchical employment relationships. The survey further explores how socio-demographic and cross-cultural factors influence employee perceptions of emotional artificial intelligence, mapping the contours of public and worker attitudes where evidence permits. By situating technical capabilities within their broader societal context, the paper provides a balanced assessment that moves beyond purely engineering-focused perspectives to consider the human consequences of these technologies. This ethical framework is intended to guide researchers, practitioners, and policymakers in developing responsible approaches to affective workplace monitoring.

## 3.1 Audio-Visual Datasets for Cognitive Load and Affect in Remote Work

A notable contribution to audio-visual datasets targeting cognitive load and affect in remote work is the AVCAffe dataset introduced by Bussolan et al. (2025). This dataset was explicitly designed to study cognitive load and affect within remote work scenarios, capturing data from 106 participants who engaged in seven distinct tasks conducted via video conferencing. The tasks included open discussions and collaborative decision-making exercises, which were structured to elicit varying levels of cognitive load (Bussolan et al., 2025). The dataset provides annotations for the recorded audio-visual data, although the specific annotation dimensions are not detailed in the available evidence. By offering multimodal recordings from a substantial participant sample in realistic remote collaboration settings, AVCAffe represents a targeted resource for investigating how cognitive load and affective states manifest through audio-visual channels during distributed work.

# 5 Future Directions

The current landscape of audio-visual cognitive load and affect research is constrained by several fundamental limitations that impede progress toward robust, real-world applications. Existing datasets remain narrow in scope and ecological validity, with resources such as AVCAffe providing valuable but limited snapshots of remote work scenarios while leaving vast domains of collaborative and daily life unexplored. Annotation practices across available resources lack transparency and standardization, with cognitive load and affective dimensions often vaguely defined or entirely undisclosed, undermining reproducibility and making cross-dataset comparisons nearly impossible. The field suffers from a conspicuous absence of socio-demographic and cross-cultural perspectives, as empirical evidence provides little insight into how factors such as age, gender, cultural background, or socioeconomic status shape the manifestation and interpretation of internal states through audio-visual signals. Furthermore, current resources predominantly capture single-session interactions, failing to account for temporal dynamics, habituation effects, and the evolving nature of collaborative relationships over extended periods. These gaps collectively prevent the development of generalized, fair, and robust models capable of functioning reliably across diverse populations and contexts.

Future research must prioritize the creation of large-scale, ecologically diverse datasets that extend beyond controlled remote work environments into naturalistic settings such as in-person collaboration, learning environments, healthcare consultations, and daily life activities. These datasets should capture longitudinal data over weeks or months to reveal temporal patterns and adaptive behaviors that single-session recordings cannot expose. Equally important is the establishment of rigorous, transparent annotation frameworks that clearly define cognitive load and affective dimensions, incorporate multi-rater agreement metrics, and leverage both self-report instruments and behavioral ground truth measures. Researchers should systematically investigate how socio-demographic variables and cultural contexts modulate the audio-visual expression of internal states, requiring deliberate oversampling of underrepresented populations and comprehensive cross-cultural validation studies. Methodologically, the field should explore unsupervised and self-supervised learning approaches that leverage large volumes of unlabeled multimodal data, reducing dependency on costly manual annotations while learning more generalizable representations of human cognitive and emotional states. Interdisciplinary collaboration among computer scientists, psychologists, and organizational behavior researchers will be essential to co-design resources that capture the collaborative, often ambiguous, and culturally situated nature of human communication.

The pursuit of these directions holds transformative potential for both theory and application. By building more representative and rigorously annotated resources, the research community can develop computational models that are not only more accurate but also demonstrably fair and inclusive across demographic and cultural boundaries. Such advances would directly enable the next generation of empathetic computing systems capable of adapting to users’ cognitive and emotional needs in diverse contexts, from intelligent tutoring systems that respond equitably to learner confusion regardless of background, to mental health tools that recognize distress signals across populations, to collaborative technologies that optimize team performance by sensing collective cognitive load in global, multicultural teams. Ultimately, addressing these gaps will move the field from narrow, laboratory-bound demonstrations toward robust, real-world solutions that respect and reflect the full complexity of human diversity while maintaining ethical integrity and worker autonomy.


# 6 Conclusion

This survey has systematically examined the landscape of multimodal datasets designed to capture cognitive load and affective states through audio-visual channels, with particular emphasis on their applicability to remote and distributed work environments. The central finding reveals a pronounced gap between the accelerating demand for ecologically valid, richly annotated resources and their current availability. The AVCAffe dataset emerges as a noteworthy contribution, explicitly targeting the intersection of cognitive load and affect within video conferencing contexts through structured collaborative tasks and a substantial participant sample. This focused design represents meaningful progress toward capturing the complexities of real-world remote interactions, moving beyond decontextualized laboratory paradigms. However, the broader field remains constrained by several critical limitations, including the scarcity of datasets that incorporate diverse task types, transparent and standardized annotation frameworks, and comprehensive demographic documentation. Furthermore, the review highlights that while socio-demographic and cross-cultural factors are widely acknowledged as essential determinants in the perception and manifestation of emotional artificial intelligence, empirical evidence linking these variables to audio-visual markers of cognitive load in remote work remains fragmented and insufficiently integrated into dataset design and evaluation practices.

The significance of this survey resides in its identification and synthesis of an under-addressed nexus within affective computing and human-computer interaction research. By mapping the current state of specialized datasets against the practical requirements of remote work analysis, this work provides a clear diagnostic of the field’s limitations and opportunities. It underscores that the validity and generalizability of future cognitive load and affect recognition systems are fundamentally dependent on the ecological richness and representativeness of their training data. The analysis demonstrates that progress is not solely a function of algorithmic innovation but is equally contingent upon the availability of purpose-built, multimodal corpora that faithfully represent the collaborative, often ambiguous, and culturally situated nature of remote communication. For researchers and practitioners, this survey serves as both a benchmark and a roadmap, clarifying why generic affect datasets are insufficient proxies and articulating the specific design principles—including task-induced cognitive load variation, authentic collaborative dynamics, and rigorous demographic documentation—that must be prioritized to enable meaningful advances.

Moving forward, the research community must undertake a concerted effort to close the identified resource gap through the creation and open dissemination of next-generation audio-visual datasets. These efforts should be guided by a commitment to ecological validity, embedding realistic remote work tasks, multi-dimensional annotations that capture both cognitive and affective dimensions with high inter-rater reliability, and deliberate sampling strategies that encompass diverse socio-demographic and cultural backgrounds. Such datasets will not only enable the development of more accurate and equitable models but also facilitate the critical investigation of how individual differences shape the expression and perception of cognitive load in digital workspaces. The ultimate call to action is for sustained interdisciplinary collaboration among computer scientists, psychologists, and organizational behavior researchers to co-design these resources, ensuring that the resulting systems are both technologically sophisticated and ethically grounded. Only through such foundational work can the field build emotionally intelligent systems that genuinely enhance remote collaboration, well-being, and productivity without perpetuating bias or oversimplifying the complexity of human experience.
