# 1 Abstract

The shift toward remote and hybrid work models has created an urgent need for unobtrusive, real-time methods to assess cognitive load and affective states, as traditional in-person supervisory mechanisms become unavailable. Audio-visual sensing, leveraging standard home-office equipment such as webcams and microphones, has emerged as a promising approach for capturing behavioral signals indicative of stress, engagement, and emotional valence during computer-mediated collaboration. This survey provides a comprehensive examination of the data resources and computational techniques underpinning audio-visual cognitive and affective sensing for remote work contexts. We delineate the essential criteria for ecologically valid datasets, including task diversity, annotation granularity, and robustness to the environmental variability characteristic of home offices. A central contribution is an in-depth analysis of a recently introduced benchmark dataset that offers synchronized audio-visual recordings from a large participant sample engaged in realistic video-conferencing tasks designed to systematically elicit varied cognitive demands and emotional responses. We highlight how this resource addresses critical gaps in prior datasets, which were often limited to controlled laboratory settings, and discuss its role in enabling the development of more generalizable multimodal models. Furthermore, we identify persistent challenges related to socio-demographic and cross-cultural variability in affective expression and technology acceptance, emphasizing the risk of algorithmic bias in the absence of diverse, empirically grounded data. This survey consolidates the current state of knowledge and outlines a roadmap for future research that integrates technical innovation with ethical considerations for the responsible deployment of affective computing in distributed workplaces.


# 2 Introduction

The rapid transformation of work from traditional office settings to remote and hybrid models has fundamentally altered how organizations understand and support their workforce. As face-to-face interactions give way to video conferences, instant messaging, and asynchronous collaboration platforms, the subtle interpersonal cues that managers and colleagues once relied upon to gauge engagement, well-being, and cognitive strain have become largely invisible. In this evolving landscape, affective computing has emerged as a compelling technological response to the challenges of distributed work. By integrating computer vision, speech analysis, and machine learning, affective computing systems aim to sense and interpret human emotional and cognitive states from behavioral signals captured through everyday devices such as webcams and microphones. The promise of these technologies lies in their potential to create more responsive, empathetic digital work environments that can detect early signs of burnout, cognitive overload, or disengagement and offer timely support. Yet the application of affective computing to remote workplace monitoring is not merely a technical endeavor; it exists at a complex intersection of sensing capabilities, algorithmic inference, and profound ethical considerations that touch upon privacy, autonomy, and the very nature of the employer-employee relationship.

The deployment of affective computing in remote work contexts is beset by a series of interconnected challenges that span the entire pipeline from data collection to real-world application. On the sensing front, the uncontrolled and heterogeneous nature of home office environments introduces substantial variability in lighting, background noise, camera angles, and audio quality, which can severely degrade the performance of models trained on clean laboratory data. Inference algorithms must grapple with the inherent ambiguity of emotional expression, the cultural and individual variability in how cognitive load and affect manifest behaviorally, and the difficulty of distinguishing transient states from enduring traits. Beyond these technical hurdles, a deeper set of ethical problems remains largely unresolved. The same technologies that could identify employee distress and prompt supportive interventions might also enable invasive surveillance, algorithmic bias against certain demographic groups, and the normalization of emotional monitoring as a condition of employment. Furthermore, the field suffers from a critical scarcity of ecologically valid, multimodal datasets that capture the richness and messiness of real-world remote collaboration, as well as a striking absence of substantive research on how socio-demographic and cross-cultural factors shape both the expression of affective states and attitudes toward being monitored by such systems.

This survey offers several distinct contributions to the study and practice of affective computing for remote workplace monitoring. First, it presents a comprehensive and structured taxonomy of the sensing modalities currently employed in this domain. This taxonomy encompasses visual signals such as facial expressions, eye gaze patterns, and head pose dynamics; auditory cues including speech prosody, paralinguistic features, and conversational turn-taking; and emerging behavioral signals such as keystroke dynamics and mouse movement trajectories. For each modality, the survey critically assesses its suitability for remote work scenarios, with particular attention to unobtrusiveness, robustness to environmental variation, and compatibility with standard home office equipment. This systematic categorization provides researchers and practitioners with a clear map of the technical landscape and highlights critical gaps where current sensing capabilities fall short of the demands of real-world deployment.

Second, the survey provides an in-depth analysis of inference methodologies, tracing the evolution from classical machine learning approaches to contemporary deep learning architectures, with a specific focus on multimodal fusion techniques that integrate heterogeneous signals to improve the accuracy and reliability of affective state estimation. The discussion extends to the challenges of personalization, context-awareness, and the temporal dynamics of cognitive and emotional processes. Crucially, this survey accords equal weight to the ethical, legal, and social dimensions of remote affective monitoring, presenting a detailed framework that addresses privacy-preserving computation, algorithmic fairness, transparency, and the psychological impacts of continuous monitoring on employee autonomy and trust. By synthesizing insights from human-computer interaction, organizational psychology, and technology ethics, the paper foregrounds the critical tension between the benefits of proactive well-being support and the risks of institutionalizing emotional surveillance. A central element of this analysis is an examination of recently introduced benchmark datasets, including multimodal resources that capture synchronized audio-visual recordings from participants engaged in realistic video-conferencing tasks designed to elicit varied cognitive demands and emotional responses, highlighting how such resources address critical gaps in prior data collections.

The primary aim of this survey is to provide researchers, practitioners, and policymakers with a holistic resource that bridges the divide between technical innovation and responsible deployment. The paper is structured to first establish the foundations of affective computing and the specific demands imposed by remote work monitoring, followed by a detailed review of sensing modalities and inference techniques. Subsequent sections are dedicated to the ethical dimensions, including a critical examination of existing regulatory instruments and proposed design guidelines for ethical affective systems. The survey concludes by identifying open research challenges and outlining a roadmap for future work that balances technological progress with the protection of fundamental human values in the evolving landscape of remote work.

## 3.1 Audio-Visual Sensing for Cognitive Load and Affect in Remote Work

A key resource for audio-visual sensing of cognitive load and affect in remote work is the AVCAffe dataset introduced by Bussolan et al. (2025). This dataset was specifically designed to study cognitive load and affect in remote work scenarios, capturing multimodal data from 106 participants who engaged in seven distinct tasks delivered via video conferencing. The tasks, which included open discussions and collaborative decision-making exercises, were constructed to elicit varying levels of cognitive load (Bussolan et al., 2025). The dataset provides annotations that enable the investigation of both cognitive load and affective states, making it a valuable foundation for developing and evaluating audio-visual models in this domain.

# 5 Future Directions

The current landscape of audio-visual sensing for cognitive load and affect in remote work is constrained by several notable limitations. Foremost among these is the scarcity of purpose-built, multimodal datasets that capture the full complexity of remote collaboration. Existing resources remain limited in scope, involving a relatively small number of participants and a constrained set of tasks. This narrow focus restricts the generalizability of models trained on such data, as they may not adequately reflect the diversity of real-world remote work scenarios, including variations in cultural communication styles, home office environments, and spontaneous, unscripted interactions. Furthermore, the field suffers from a significant gap in understanding how socio-demographic and cross-cultural factors shape both the expression and the algorithmic interpretation of cognitive and affective states. Without robust, empirically grounded insights into these determinants, there is a substantial risk that sensing systems will encode biases, performing unevenly across different populations and potentially misinterpreting the emotional cues of individuals from underrepresented groups. The absence of longitudinal studies also means that little is known about how these signals evolve over time or adapt to changing work patterns and individual baselines.

To address these gaps, future research must prioritize the creation of large-scale, ecologically valid datasets that encompass a broad spectrum of remote work activities, cultural contexts, and demographic profiles. These efforts should move beyond controlled, task-based recordings to capture longitudinal, naturalistic interactions in genuine home office settings, thereby incorporating the environmental and contextual noise that characterizes daily professional life. A critical parallel direction involves conducting rigorous, interdisciplinary studies to map the socio-demographic and cross-cultural dimensions of emotional AI. This research should produce primary, substantive findings that can directly inform the design of fair and culturally adaptive models, moving beyond mere acknowledgment of diversity to active integration of these insights into system architecture. On the technical front, the development of novel multimodal fusion architectures is essential. These models should be capable of disentangling transient cognitive load from baseline affective states while explicitly accounting for individual and cultural variability, perhaps through the integration of personalized adaptation layers or culture-aware encoders. Additionally, the field would benefit from exploring self-supervised and few-shot learning paradigms that can reduce the reliance on extensively labeled, demographically balanced data, allowing models to quickly adapt to new users and contexts with minimal supervision.

The pursuit of these future directions holds the potential to fundamentally transform the quality and equity of remote work. By grounding audio-visual sensing in diverse, real-world data and a nuanced understanding of human expressivity, future systems can move beyond generic, one-size-fits-all analytics toward truly personalized and context-aware support. This could lead to tools that not only accurately detect states of overload or disengagement but also provide culturally sensitive interventions, such as suggesting breaks, adjusting communication interfaces, or facilitating asynchronous collaboration when synchronous fatigue is detected. Ultimately, this work can foster a more inclusive and psychologically safe digital workplace, where technology adapts to the human, rather than forcing a diverse global workforce to conform to a narrow, and potentially biased, algorithmic standard. The impact would be a measurable improvement in employee well-being, team cohesion, and sustainable productivity across the distributed fabric of modern work.


# 6 Conclusion

The convergence of remote work expansion and advances in affective computing has created both opportunities and imperatives for unobtrusive cognitive and affective state assessment through audio-visual sensing. This survey has systematically examined the current landscape of multimodal data resources and computational approaches tailored to this domain, revealing a field in its formative stages yet marked by clear technical and ethical priorities. The central finding underscores the foundational role of purpose-built, ecologically valid datasets, with the recently introduced AVCAffe benchmark emerging as a pivotal resource. By capturing synchronized audio-visual recordings from a substantial participant sample engaged in realistic video-conferencing tasks designed to elicit varied cognitive demands and emotional responses, this dataset directly addresses the limitations of prior laboratory-constrained resources. The analysis demonstrates that structured task design, encompassing open discussion and collaborative decision-making, provides the annotation granularity and ecological validity necessary for training models capable of generalizing to the noisy, unpredictable conditions of genuine home office environments. However, the survey also exposes a critical lacuna: the near-total absence of empirically grounded research on how socio-demographic and cross-cultural factors shape both the expression of cognitive-affective states and individual attitudes toward Emotional AI in the workplace, a gap that threatens to perpetuate algorithmic bias and undermine user trust.

The significance of this survey lies in its dual function as a technical consolidation and an ethical intervention. By providing a structured taxonomy of sensing modalities, inference methodologies, and evaluation protocols, the paper equips researchers and practitioners with a coherent framework for navigating a fragmented literature. The identification of the AVCAffe dataset as a common benchmark serves to align future efforts around a challenging, representative standard, thereby accelerating progress toward robust and generalizable models. More critically, the survey foregrounds the unresolved tension between the benefits of proactive well-being support and the risks of normalizing emotional surveillance. By explicitly mapping the ethical, legal, and social dimensions—including privacy, consent, fairness, and the psychological impacts of continuous monitoring—alongside technical capabilities, the paper insists that responsible deployment cannot be an afterthought. This integrated perspective is essential as these technologies transition from experimental prototypes to workplace tools, where their design choices will carry concrete consequences for employee autonomy and organizational power dynamics.

The path forward demands a concerted, interdisciplinary research agenda that balances algorithmic innovation with inclusive, ethically grounded inquiry. The immediate technical priority is to leverage existing benchmarks to develop multimodal fusion architectures robust to the signal degradations characteristic of remote communication platforms, exploring techniques that dynamically adapt to variable audio-visual quality. Equally urgent, however, is the need for primary research that rigorously investigates how individual differences—spanning cultural background, gender, age, and professional context—modulate both the accuracy of cognitive load detection and the fundamental acceptability of algorithmic monitoring. Future dataset construction must move beyond convenience sampling to encompass longitudinal, naturalistic recordings across diverse populations and genuine home office settings. Only through such deliberate, human-centered effort can the field produce sensing systems that are not merely accurate in aggregate but equitable and supportive across the full spectrum of the global workforce. The ultimate goal is a digital workplace where technology adapts to human variability, fostering well-being and sustainable productivity without compromising the values of dignity, privacy, and trust.
