# 1 Abstract

The automation of software maintenance tasks, particularly code repair and review, has been significantly advanced by the emergence of large language models pre-trained on both natural and programming languages. These models are increasingly deployed within structured workflows, ranging from prompt-engineered single-model systems to cooperative multi-agent frameworks, to generate patches, identify defects, and align outputs with established review standards. This survey systematically examines the recent convergence of parameter-efficient fine-tuning, prompt engineering, and multi-agent architectures in the context of automated program repair and code review. We analyze how techniques such as identifier-aware pre-training and iterative test-and-fix loops improve repair accuracy, and how dedicated reviewer agents provide verification and governance within multi-step development pipelines. Furthermore, we investigate the transition from static fine-tuning paradigms to continual instruction tuning, which enables models to adapt to evolving codebases and project-specific conventions without catastrophic forgetting. The survey also synthesizes findings from large-scale industrial evaluations, revealing practical performance metrics and the impact of quality filtering on deployed systems. Our analysis highlights a clear trajectory toward self-evolving, LLM-based systems that integrate repair generation with rigorous, adaptive review mechanisms to enhance software quality assurance.


# 2 Introduction

The automation of software maintenance tasks has emerged as one of the most pressing challenges in modern software engineering, driven by the relentless growth in system complexity and the accelerating pace of development cycles. Code review, a fundamental practice for ensuring software quality, security, and maintainability, has traditionally relied on manual, human-intensive processes that demand substantial developer effort to inspect changes, identify defects, and suggest improvements. As software projects expand in scale, the limitations of purely manual review—including reviewer fatigue, inconsistent feedback quality, and bottlenecks in continuous integration pipelines—have become increasingly apparent. In parallel, the field of artificial intelligence has undergone transformative advances through large language models pre-trained on vast corpora of both natural and programming languages. These models have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in understanding and generating code, offering a promising foundation for automating aspects of software engineering that have long resisted conventional program analysis techniques. The convergence of these two trends has given rise to a rapidly expanding research area: the application of large language models to automated code review, encompassing tasks from defect detection and repair suggestion to the generation of comprehensive, context-aware review comments.

Despite the growing body of work in this domain, several critical challenges remain unresolved and hinder both academic progress and industrial adoption. First, the technical approaches for adapting large language models to code review are highly diverse, spanning prompt engineering, parameter-efficient fine-tuning, multi-agent frameworks, and continual learning paradigms, yet there exists no systematic synthesis of their relative strengths, weaknesses, and applicable contexts. Second, the evaluation landscape is fragmented: benchmarks vary widely in their definitions of functional correctness, the metrics they employ, and the degree to which they reflect real-world review scenarios, making meaningful comparison across studies exceptionally difficult. Third, while industrial deployments of large language model-based code review systems have begun to emerge, the practical challenges of sustaining model performance in dynamic, evolving codebases—such as catastrophic forgetting and adaptation to new project-specific conventions—are only starting to receive systematic attention. Finally, the role of code review information itself as a training signal for improving automated program repair, as opposed to treating review generation and repair as separate, disconnected tasks, remains underexplored in a unified and principled manner.

This survey makes two primary contributions to the field, addressing the fragmentation and gaps identified above. First, we provide a comprehensive and structured taxonomy of the techniques that have been applied to large language model-based automated code review. We categorize existing work along multiple dimensions, including the underlying model architecture, the adaptation strategy employed—ranging from prompt engineering and parameter-efficient fine-tuning to multi-agent orchestration and continual instruction tuning—and the specific code review subtask being targeted, such as defect detection, repair generation, or review comment synthesis. By systematically analyzing approaches such as identifier-aware pre-training for repair, cooperative multi-agent debugging frameworks, and retrieval-augmented generation, we highlight how different design choices influence review quality, alignment with human expectations, and computational efficiency. This taxonomy not only organizes a rapidly expanding and often disjointed literature but also reveals gaps and opportunities for cross-fertilization between related subfields, most notably the integration of review-aware training signals into general-purpose code generation models and the potential for tighter coupling between code review and automated program repair.

Second, we conduct an in-depth examination of evaluation methodologies and industrial deployment practices, bridging the divide between laboratory research and production reality. We survey the benchmarks and metrics used to assess large language model performance on code review tasks, with a particular focus on how functional correctness is operationalized across different evaluation infrastructures and the limitations of current assessment frameworks. Beyond offline benchmarks, we analyze real-world deployment case studies, including large-scale online evaluations of systems deployed in industrial settings, and discuss the emerging role of continual instruction tuning techniques—such as adversarial expert architectures with dedicated parameter-efficient modules—in enabling self-evolving code review models that can adapt to shifting project conventions without catastrophic forgetting. By synthesizing findings from both academic research and industrial practice, we identify the key engineering and organizational factors that determine the success or failure of large language model-based review automation in production environments, including the critical impact of quality filtering on deployed system performance.

The aim of this survey is to provide researchers and practitioners with a clear, structured overview of the state of the art in large language model-based automated code review, while also charting concrete directions for future work that address the most pressing open challenges. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. We begin by establishing foundational concepts and defining the scope of automated code review in the context of large language models, clarifying the key tasks and terminology that underpin the field. We then present our taxonomy of techniques, systematically categorizing approaches from single-model prompting to cooperative multi-agent systems, followed by a detailed analysis of evaluation benchmarks and metrics. Subsequent sections examine industrial deployment experiences and the emerging paradigm of self-evolving review models enabled by continual instruction tuning. We conclude with a discussion of open challenges and promising research directions, including the need for standardized evaluation protocols that extend beyond functional correctness, the integration of federated learning for privacy-preserving review adaptation across organizational boundaries, and the potential for tighter coupling between code review and automated program repair to create truly adaptive, developer-centered quality assurance systems.


# 3 Fine-Tuning and Multi-Agent LLMs for Code Review Automation

## 3.1 Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning and Prompting for Code Review and Repair

Recent work has begun to explore how parameter-efficient fine‑tuning and prompt engineering can advance automated code repair. Paul et al. (2023) argue that there is a clear need for new techniques to improve the state of the art in repairing buggy code, and they open a path that uses prompt engineering to craft clear and concise prompts, enabling large language models to produce coherent and relevant repair responses. Their study further validates that code repair can be significantly improved by using large language models pre‑trained on both natural language and programming language, and they examine outputs from fine‑tuned models and prompted LLMs to assess how well the repaired programs align with code review expectations (Paul et al., 2023).

Beyond single‑model prompting, multi‑agent frameworks have also been applied to program repair. Khanzadeh (2025) introduces AgentMesh, a cooperative multi‑agent system in which a Debugger agent uses an LLM to analyze failures—such as reading an error traceback or identifying a logical mistake—and then proposes a fix. The agent applies the repair by generating a patch or revised code, and this process operates in an iterative test‑and‑fix loop that continues until the code meets the acceptance criteria of the subtask (Khanzadeh, 2025). Together, these efforts illustrate a growing interest in leveraging lightweight adaptation and structured prompting strategies to support both automated code repair and alignment with review standards.

## 3.2 Multi-Agent LLM Frameworks for Automated Code Review

Recent work has begun to explore how large language models (LLMs) can be integrated into multi-agent frameworks to automate aspects of the code review and repair process. Paul et al. (2023) investigated the use of different pre-trained models and LLMs to repair code based on code reviews, demonstrating that learning-based models can improve their performance when leveraging code review information rather than relying solely on source code to predict proper fixes. Their work extended prior studies by fine-tuning models, prompting LLMs, and manually analyzing the results (Paul et al., 2023). In a parallel direction, Khanzadeh (2025) introduced AgentMesh, a cooperative multi-agent framework for software development automation. Within this framework, a dedicated Debugger agent uses an LLM to analyze failures—such as reading an error traceback or spotting a logical mistake—and proposes a fix, which is then applied by generating a patch or revised code through an iterative test-and-fix loop (Khanzadeh, 2025). These contributions illustrate a growing trend toward employing LLM-based agents not only to identify defects but also to generate and refine corrections in a structured, multi-step workflow.

## 3.3 Federated Learning and Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Code Review


The integration of code review into automated program repair has been explored through fine-tuning and prompt engineering of large language models. Paul et al. (2023) introduced identifier-aware pre-training and bi-modal dual generation techniques, demonstrating their usability in generating repaired code based on code review. Their work found that CodeT5, when fine-tuned, exhibited slightly better performance than PLBART in most cases, attributing this to its comparatively stronger natural language and programming language comprehension capabilities, which enabled higher accuracy in predicting correct fixes with the aid of code review (Paul et al., 2023). The study also investigated zero-shot unit test generation using the GPT-3.5-Turbo model with appropriate prompt instructions, focusing on automated code repair through zero-shot and few-shot learning-based prompt engineering (Paul et al., 2023).

In multi-agent frameworks, the role of a reviewer agent has been identified as a mechanism for verification and governance. Khanzadeh (2025) describes a Reviewer agent that provides an extra safety net by reviewing every commit, aligning with findings in frameworks such as ChatDev where multi-turn interactions—particularly those using program output as feedback—significantly enhanced debugging success (Khanzadeh, 2025). This cooperative approach suggests that dedicated review agents can complement generation-focused models, though the specific intersection of federated learning or retrieval-augmented generation with code review remains unexplored in the available evidence.

The specific way correctness is evaluated depends on what each benchmark provides. The authors review the quality attributes that LLMs are assessed against and the metrics used to measure them, framing functional correctness as the central concern in code generation evaluation.

In evaluating LLM-based coding, functional correctness stands as the primary quality attribute for assessing model responses to programming tasks (Paul et al., 2024). The specific metrics and evaluation approaches employed depend heavily on what each benchmark provides. Paul et al. (2024) note that correctness assessment varies according to the infrastructure available within a given benchmark, though the evidence does not detail particular metric formulas or evaluation frameworks beyond this foundational observation. The centrality of functional correctness underscores that, regardless of the evaluation infrastructure, the ability of generated code to meet specified task requirements remains the core concern in LLM coding assessment.

# 5 Industrial Deployment and Self-Evolving LLMs for Code Review

## 5.1 Large-Scale Online Evaluation of LLM Code Review in Industrial Settings

**5.1 Large-Scale Online Evaluation of LLM Code Review in Industrial Settings**

Pornprasit and Tantithamthavorn (2024) present a large-scale online evaluation of an LLM-based code review automation system, RovoDev Code Reviewer, deployed at Atlassian. The system automatically generates review comments for a given code change, as illustrated by an example comment in their work. In a recent one-week evaluation period between July 10 and July 15, 2025, the authors report that RovoDev Code Reviewer achieved a code resolution rate of 38.70%, compared to human-written comments. The study also provides post-generation and post-quality-check metrics, showing that after generation, 22% of pull requests had human-authored comments (PR_HAC) and 90% had no human-authored comments (PR_!HAC), while after quality checking, these figures shifted to 7% and 53%, respectively. These results offer direct insight into how an LLM-based reviewer performs in a real industrial workflow, capturing both the initial generation output and the impact of subsequent quality filtering.

While the RovoDev evaluation focuses on a specific deployment snapshot, the broader challenge of sustaining model performance in evolving industrial environments is addressed by Kang et al. (2025). They introduce MoE-CL, a continual instruction tuning approach designed for large-scale industrial LLM deployments. MoE-CL employs an adversarial LoRA expert architecture that allocates a dedicated LoRA expert to each task, ensuring that training on a new task does not overwrite prior parameters and thereby addressing catastrophic forgetting while enabling useful knowledge transfer. Although Kang et al. do not evaluate code review directly, their framework is directly relevant to the long-term viability of systems like RovoDev, where the underlying model must adapt to new codebases, languages, or review policies without degrading existing capabilities.

## 5.2 Self-Evolving LLMs via Continual Instruction Tuning for Code Review

**5.2 Self-Evolving LLMs via Continual Instruction Tuning for Code Review**

The automation of code review through large language models (LLMs) has become an active research area, driven by the need to improve software quality and reviewer productivity (Pornprasit & Tantithamthavorn, 2024). Recent work has explored fine-tuning models such as CodeT5, Llama, and GPT-4o on task-specific data to generate review comments for code changes (Pornprasit & Tantithamthavorn, 2024). While these approaches have demonstrated effectiveness, they typically rely on static fine-tuning paradigms that do not adapt to evolving code review practices or new project-specific knowledge over time.

A promising direction for addressing this limitation is continual instruction tuning, a paradigm in which LLMs are dynamically adapted through sequential task-specific instruction tuning (Kang et al., 2025). Kang et al. (2025) describe this approach as enabling models to incrementally incorporate new knowledge from evolving instructions and adapt to diverse tasks while retaining performance on previously learned tasks. Applied to code review, such a self-evolving LLM could continuously refine its review capabilities as it encounters new code changes, feedback from developers, or shifting project conventions, rather than requiring full retraining on accumulated data. This represents a natural evolution beyond the fine-tuning strategies currently dominant in LLM-based code review automation (Pornprasit & Tantithamthavorn, 2024).

# 6 Future Directions

Despite the rapid progress in applying large language models to automated code repair and review, several fundamental limitations persist. Current systems predominantly rely on static fine‑tuning paradigms that cannot adapt to evolving codebases, shifting project conventions, or new developer feedback without costly full retraining. Multi‑agent frameworks have demonstrated the value of dedicated reviewer and debugger agents, yet these systems operate in isolation from the continuous learning loops that characterize real‑world software development. The intersection of privacy‑preserving techniques such as federated learning with code review automation remains entirely unexplored, leaving open questions about how organizations can collaboratively improve repair models without exposing proprietary source code. Evaluation practices also lag behind, with functional correctness serving as the primary quality attribute while broader dimensions—such as review comment actionability, developer trust, and alignment with team‑specific standards—receive comparatively little systematic attention. Furthermore, the role of code review information itself as a training signal for improving automated program repair, rather than treating review generation and repair as separate tasks, remains underexplored in a unified manner.

Future research should pursue three interconnected directions. First, continual instruction tuning must be adapted specifically for code review and repair workflows, enabling models to incrementally absorb new project‑specific knowledge, incorporate developer feedback on generated fixes, and adapt to evolving coding standards without catastrophic forgetting. Architectures that allocate dedicated parameter‑efficient modules per task or per project offer a promising foundation, but they require validation in longitudinal industrial deployments where code review practices genuinely drift over time. Second, federated learning frameworks should be designed to enable cross‑organizational model improvement for program repair while preserving code privacy. Such frameworks would allow multiple teams to contribute to a shared repair model by exchanging only gradient updates or learned representations, never raw source code, thereby addressing a fundamental barrier to collaborative improvement in proprietary environments. Third, evaluation methodologies must expand beyond functional correctness to encompass the full lifecycle of automated code review, including metrics for developer acceptance rates, review comment clarity, and the long‑term maintainability of AI‑generated patches. Integrating retrieval‑augmented generation into these pipelines could further ground repair suggestions in project‑specific historical fixes and institutional knowledge, improving relevance and trustworthiness.

Pursuing these directions would fundamentally reshape how automated code repair systems are developed and sustained in practice. Continual learning capabilities would transform static repair tools into living systems that grow more valuable over time, reducing the maintenance burden on engineering teams. Federated learning would unlock collaborative model improvement across organizational boundaries, democratizing access to high‑quality repair assistance even for teams with limited training data. Expanded evaluation frameworks would bridge the gap between laboratory benchmarks and industrial reality, ensuring that future systems are judged not only on whether they produce correct code but on whether developers actually adopt and benefit from their suggestions. Together, these advances would move the field beyond one‑shot generation toward adaptive, privacy‑aware, and developer‑centered code review automation that aligns with the dynamic, collaborative nature of real‑world software engineering.


# 7 Conclusion

This survey has systematically examined the convergence of parameter-efficient fine-tuning, prompt engineering, and multi-agent architectures in the domain of automated program repair and code review, revealing a clear trajectory from static, single-model approaches toward dynamic, self-evolving systems. The reviewed evidence demonstrates that leveraging code review information—whether through identifier-aware pre-training, bi-modal generation, or carefully structured prompts—consistently improves the functional correctness of generated repairs. Multi-agent frameworks further extend these capabilities by introducing specialized roles, such as Debugger and Reviewer agents, that structure the repair process into iterative test-and-fix loops and provide an additional layer of verification, thereby aligning automated outputs more closely with human code review expectations. Industrial deployments confirm the practical viability of LLM-based code review, while emerging paradigms like continual instruction tuning and mixture-of-experts architectures address the critical challenge of sustaining model performance as codebases, languages, and review policies evolve. The synthesis of these findings underscores a field in transition, where the integration of repair generation with rigorous, adaptive review mechanisms is becoming the dominant architectural pattern.

The significance of this survey lies in its unification of two historically separate research streams—automated program repair and automated code review—into a cohesive perspective on LLM-driven software quality assurance. By mapping the progression from prompt-engineered single models to cooperative multi-agent systems and from static fine-tuning to continual learning paradigms, the survey identifies the key architectural patterns, evaluation priorities, and adaptation strategies that define the current state of the art. It highlights functional correctness as the central evaluation criterion while exposing underexplored areas, most notably the intersection of federated learning and retrieval-augmented generation with code review, and the long-term sustainability of deployed systems in the face of evolving project conventions. The structured taxonomy and critical analysis of industrial deployment experiences provide researchers and practitioners with a foundational reference for understanding where the field stands, which techniques have proven effective, and which gaps remain most pressing for future investigation.

Looking ahead, the community must move beyond isolated benchmarks and short-term deployments to embrace evaluation frameworks that capture the evolving, collaborative, and context-dependent nature of real-world code review and repair. Future work should prioritize the integration of continual learning mechanisms that allow models to adapt without catastrophic forgetting, the development of multi-agent coordination protocols that balance generation fluency with review rigor, and the exploration of privacy-preserving techniques such as federated learning to enable cross-organizational knowledge sharing without exposing proprietary source code. Industrial platforms demonstrate that LLM-based code review is no longer a theoretical prospect but an operational reality; the challenge now is to ensure these systems remain robust, fair, and aligned with developer workflows over time. By pursuing these directions, the research community can advance automated code review and repair from promising prototypes to dependable, self-improving components of the software engineering lifecycle, ultimately reducing the maintenance burden on development teams and enhancing software quality at scale.
