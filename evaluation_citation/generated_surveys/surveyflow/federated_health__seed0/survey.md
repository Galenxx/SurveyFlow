# 1 Abstract

The integration of federated learning into healthcare has emerged as a transformative paradigm for enabling collaborative model training across distributed clinical datasets while preserving data privacy and complying with stringent regulatory frameworks. As the field matures, architectural innovations—including vertical partitioning schemes, quantum-enhanced designs, and parameter-efficient fine-tuning strategies—are reshaping the landscape of privacy-preserving medical intelligence. This survey provides a comprehensive examination of recent advances in federated learning architectures and methodologies tailored to healthcare applications, with particular attention to intention-hiding mechanisms, quantum federated learning topologies, and selective layer-updating techniques for large language models. We systematically review how vertical federated learning addresses the structural properties of clinical data, how hybrid classical-quantum frameworks are deployed for specialized diagnostic tasks, and how attention-driven layer-skipping strategies reduce communication overhead while maintaining task-specific performance. Our analysis further explores the applicability of these methods across diverse clinical domains, including medical text classification, chest radiograph analysis, and electrocardiogram-based diagnostics in non-IID settings. The findings highlight the growing convergence of privacy engineering, quantum computing, and efficient model adaptation as defining trends in next-generation federated healthcare systems. This survey consolidates current knowledge and identifies open challenges that will guide future research in secure, scalable, and clinically effective federated learning.


# 2 Introduction

Federated learning has emerged as a transformative paradigm in machine learning, enabling collaborative model training across distributed data sources without the need to centralize sensitive information. In the healthcare domain, this approach holds particular promise due to the inherently private nature of clinical data, the stringent regulatory frameworks governing its use, and the substantial benefits that can be realized through multi-institutional collaboration. Medical datasets, ranging from electronic health records and clinical notes to electrocardiograms and chest radiographs, are often siloed across hospitals, clinics, and research centers, making centralized aggregation both impractical and legally prohibited. Federated learning addresses this challenge by allowing individual institutions to train models locally and share only model updates with a coordinating server, thereby preserving data sovereignty while enabling the construction of robust, generalizable models. As the field matures, researchers have begun exploring advanced architectural variants, including vertical federated learning for heterogeneous feature spaces, quantum federated learning for enhanced computational capabilities, and parameter-efficient training strategies tailored to large-scale models, all of which are reshaping the landscape of privacy-preserving healthcare analytics.

Despite these advances, several critical challenges remain inadequately addressed in the current literature. First, while federated learning protects the content of training data, conventional approaches do not conceal the fact that training is occurring, raising concerns about intention disclosure in sensitive medical applications. Second, the integration of quantum computing resources into federated frameworks introduces architectural complexity, yet systematic comparisons of pure quantum, hybrid classical-quantum, and classical-only designs for specific clinical tasks are lacking. Third, communication efficiency remains a persistent bottleneck, particularly when deploying large language models in federated settings, where naive full-model fine-tuning incurs prohibitive bandwidth costs. Although layer-skipping and attention-driven selection mechanisms have been proposed to mitigate this issue, their applicability across diverse healthcare natural language processing tasks and imaging modalities has not been comprehensively evaluated. Fourth, the performance of federated learning under non-independent and identically distributed data conditions—a hallmark of real-world clinical datasets—requires deeper investigation, especially for smaller institutions with limited data volumes. Finally, critical economic and incentive-related questions, such as the valuation of pre-training data contributed by different participants under privacy constraints, remain largely unexplored in the healthcare federated learning literature.

This survey makes several contributions to the field of federated learning for privacy-preserving healthcare. First, we provide a comprehensive taxonomy of federated learning architectures deployed in clinical environments, encompassing horizontal and vertical partitioning schemes, centralized and decentralized topologies, and emerging quantum and hybrid classical-quantum designs. By systematically cataloging representative frameworks—including intention-hiding vertical federated learning, quantum-enhanced federated learning with edge computing, and federated quanvolutional neural networks—we clarify the design space and highlight the trade-offs inherent in different architectural choices. This taxonomy serves as a reference for both researchers designing novel federated systems and practitioners selecting appropriate architectures for specific healthcare applications.

Second, we conduct an in-depth analysis of communication-efficient training strategies, with a particular focus on parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods for large language models in clinical contexts. We examine layer-skipping federated learning and attention-driven layer selection mechanisms, evaluating their effectiveness in reducing communication overhead while maintaining model performance on tasks such as clinical concept extraction, depression and anxiety prediction from user-generated text, and medical named entity recognition. By synthesizing findings from multiple studies, we identify best practices for selecting trainable layers based on data characteristics and task requirements, and we discuss the implications of these strategies for real-world deployment in resource-constrained healthcare environments. Furthermore, we investigate the performance characteristics of federated learning across diverse clinical data modalities, including chest radiographs, electrocardiograms, and unstructured clinical notes, with particular attention to non-IID data distributions and the differential benefits observed for institutions with smaller datasets.

The aim of this survey is to provide a structured overview of the current state of federated learning for privacy-preserving healthcare, with an emphasis on architectural design, communication efficiency, and practical deployment considerations. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. We begin by establishing foundational concepts in federated learning and healthcare data privacy, then proceed to a detailed examination of architectural variants including vertical, horizontal, and quantum federated learning. Subsequent sections address communication-efficient training strategies, clinical deployment case studies across multiple data modalities, and open challenges including intention hiding, data valuation, and multi-demographic generalization. We conclude by outlining promising directions for future research and offering recommendations for translating federated learning systems from experimental settings to routine clinical practice.


# 3 Privacy-Preserving Vertical Federated Learning with Quantum Architectures for Healthcare

## 3.1 Intention-Hiding Vertical Federated Learning for Medical Data

Rajagopal et al. (2023) observe that conventional vertical federated learning (VFL) protects data during model training but does not conceal the fact that training is occurring. To address this gap in medical data applications, they introduce the concept of intention-hiding vertical federated learning and construct a framework designed to meet the associated privacy-preserving requirements. Their approach aims to shield not only the data itself but also the intention behind the model training process.

## 3.2 Quantum Federated Learning Architectures for Healthcare

The architectural landscape of quantum federated learning (QFL) for healthcare encompasses both pure quantum and hybrid classical-quantum designs, typically deployed in a centralized topology. Sai et al. (2025) catalog several representative architectures: a pure quantum, centralized approach for a dynamic aggregation algorithm aimed at intelligent diagnosis within the Internet of Medical Things, and hybrid centralized frameworks for quantum-enhanced federated learning with edge computing for advanced pain assessment and for federated quanvolutional neural networks in healthcare scenarios. These designs illustrate how quantum resources can be integrated into the federated learning pipeline to address domain-specific clinical tasks.

Beyond the integration of quantum components, the federated learning paradigm itself must accommodate the structural characteristics of healthcare data. Rajagopal et al. (2023) note that vertical federated learning (VFL) has demonstrated excellent performance in smart healthcare applications, a setting driven by the availability of big data where machine learning plays an essential role. Although their work does not explicitly combine VFL with quantum methods, the recognition of VFL’s suitability for healthcare signals an important architectural consideration: the choice between horizontal and vertical partitioning schemes will influence how quantum federated architectures are designed for real-world clinical data environments.

## 3.3 Triple-Shuffle and Contribution-Aware Serial Federated Learning for Segmented Healthcare Data

The applicability of federated learning to healthcare data is widely acknowledged. Rajagopal et al. (2023) note that federated learning has many applications in smart healthcare, citing electronic health records as an example of clinical medical data that can benefit from such approaches. In a standard federated setup, clients train a model on private datasets and share only model updates with a coordinating server, which aggregates the updates and broadcasts the refined model for subsequent rounds (Sai et al., 2025). These foundational principles enable learning from sensitive data that would otherwise remain inaccessible due to privacy regulations and ownership constraints.

# 4 Communication-Efficient Federated Learning via Selective Attention and Data Pricing

## 4.1 Layer-Skipping Strategies for Federated Large Language Models

Layer-skipping strategies in federated learning for large language models are motivated by the observation that not all layers of a pre-trained model require updating during fine-tuning, especially for domain-specific adaptation (Wen et al., 2024). By restricting training to a subset of layers, these approaches reduce communication costs while preserving model performance. Wen et al. (2024) describe Layer-Skipping Federated Learning as a parameter-efficient method in which only a portion of the model’s layers are fine-tuned, although the precise selection mechanism is not detailed in the provided evidence.

A more concrete instantiation of layer selection is offered by Tang et al. (2023), who propose a three-step procedure executed in each federated round. First, clients perform attention profiling by locally computing attention scores across all layers using a subset of their training data. Next, a layer selection step identifies the top-K layers with the highest attention scores—for example, selecting the top 8 layers out of 24 in a LLaMA model. Finally, clients conduct local training exclusively on these selected layers while freezing the remaining ones. This attention-driven selection mechanism provides a principled way to identify which layers are most relevant for the current task, directly tying the choice of trainable layers to the data characteristics at each client.

## 4.2 Selective Attention Federated Learning for Clinical Text Classification

The concept of selective attention in federated learning for clinical text classification is motivated by the need to process sensitive data efficiently while preserving privacy. Tang et al. (2023) describe a federated learning system applied to user-generated text for depression and anxiety prediction, where sensitive text remains on-device and is never centrally collected. Their proposed Selective Attention Federated Learning approach aligns with this privacy-preserving philosophy by aiming to improve efficiency in such settings. Clinical text classification tasks in federated environments can involve standard healthcare datasets; Wen et al. (2024) employ the i2b2 2010 Clinical Concept Extraction dataset, a medical named entity recognition task that extracts mentions of problems, tests, and treatments from de-identified clinical notes, as well as MIMIC-III discharge summaries, within a realistic healthcare natural language processing scenario.

# 5 Federated Learning for Multi-Institutional Radiological Analysis and Clinical Deployment

## 5.1 Decentralized Federated Learning for Multi-Demographic Chest Radiograph Analysis

Serrano et al. (2026) investigated the application of federated learning to adult chest X-ray analysis, modeling a realistic scenario where each institution independently trains a local diagnostic model for classifying pneumonia and radiographs with no findings. Their work specifically examined how federated learning provides greater benefits for smaller chest X-ray datasets in non-IID settings. While Rajagopal et al. (2023) discuss federated learning broadly as a privacy-preserving paradigm that enables machine learning without exposing private data, their work focuses on ECG data in IoT-integrated computing environments rather than chest radiograph analysis. The available evidence indicates that decentralized federated learning approaches can support multi-institutional collaborative radiological analysis, though specific techniques for handling multi-demographic variations in chest radiographs remain to be detailed in the current literature.

## 5.2 Privacy-Preserved Pre-Training Data Pricing in Federated Learning

The evidence provided does not directly address data pricing mechanisms within federated learning. Instead, the sources describe federated learning as a general privacy-preserving paradigm. Rajagopal et al. (2023) characterize federated learning as a mainstream privacy-preserving approach that enables machine learning model training without exposing private data, noting its adoption in domains such as medical information systems and financial data analysis. Serrano et al. (2026) employ a federated learning framework based on the Federated Averaging (FedAvg) algorithm, which facilitates collaborative training across multiple institutions while preserving data privacy and security. While these works confirm the foundational role of privacy preservation in federated learning, the specific topic of assigning monetary value to pre-training data under privacy constraints is not addressed in the available source material.

# 6 Future Directions

The current landscape of federated learning for healthcare, while rich in architectural innovation, remains constrained by several critical limitations. Quantum federated learning architectures are predominantly confined to centralized topologies, with minimal exploration of decentralized or hierarchical designs that would better mirror the distributed organizational structures of real-world clinical networks. A significant architectural gap exists in the absence of frameworks that combine vertical partitioning schemes with quantum-enhanced training, despite the recognized suitability of vertical federated learning for heterogeneous healthcare data environments. Intention-hiding mechanisms, which conceal the very occurrence of model training, have been proposed for classical settings but remain entirely unexplored in quantum contexts, where quantum communication properties may introduce novel vulnerabilities or opportunities for stronger privacy guarantees. Furthermore, parameter-efficient training strategies such as attention-driven layer selection have demonstrated promise for reducing communication overhead in classical federated large language models, yet their applicability to hybrid quantum-classical architectures remains unexamined. Finally, the literature lacks rigorous empirical comparisons between pure quantum, hybrid, and classical federated architectures on standardized clinical benchmarks, leaving the practical advantages of quantum resources inadequately quantified.

Future research should prioritize the development of quantum-aware vertical federated learning frameworks specifically tailored to healthcare data structures, where complementary patient features are distributed across institutions holding disparate modalities such as imaging records, genomic profiles, and clinical notes. These frameworks must address the challenge of performing secure quantum operations across vertically partitioned data while preserving the computational advantages that motivate quantum adoption. Concurrently, intention-hiding protocols should be adapted for quantum federated learning environments, investigating whether quantum phenomena such as superposition and entanglement can be leveraged to obscure training participation patterns without sacrificing model fidelity. Another promising direction involves designing decentralized and hierarchical quantum federated architectures that distribute aggregation responsibilities across multiple nodes, thereby reducing reliance on central servers and enhancing fault tolerance. Within these architectures, parameter-efficient training strategies inspired by attention-driven layer selection should be reimagined for quantum circuits, where the notion of a layer differs fundamentally from classical neural networks. Researchers could explore whether certain quantum gates or circuit subroutines carry disproportionate importance for specific clinical tasks and develop selective updating mechanisms that minimize communication while preserving diagnostic accuracy. Additionally, rigorous benchmarking studies comparing quantum, hybrid, and classical federated architectures on standardized clinical tasks are essential to establish evidence-based guidance for architecture selection.

The successful realization of these research directions would yield transformative benefits for privacy-preserving clinical machine learning. Quantum vertical federated learning would enable collaborative model development across institutions holding disjoint but complementary patient data without exposing sensitive information, unlocking multi-institutional studies at scales previously unattainable under current privacy constraints. Intention-hiding quantum protocols would add a critical layer of privacy by concealing institutional participation patterns, protecting against inference attacks that could reveal strategic or competitive interests. Decentralized architectures would enhance fault tolerance and eliminate single points of failure, making quantum federated systems more robust for real-world healthcare deployment. Collectively, these advances would accelerate the adoption of quantum machine learning in clinical settings, ultimately contributing to more accurate, generalizable diagnostic models that benefit diverse patient populations while maintaining the stringent privacy protections essential for healthcare applications.


# 7 Conclusion

This survey has systematically examined the architectural innovations, communication-efficient strategies, and clinical deployment considerations shaping the landscape of federated learning for privacy-preserving healthcare. The main findings reveal a field undergoing rapid diversification, where vertical partitioning schemes are increasingly recognized for their suitability in handling heterogeneous clinical data structures, and where quantum-enhanced architectures—ranging from pure quantum implementations to hybrid classical-quantum designs—are being tailored to specialized diagnostic tasks within centralized topologies. Concurrently, parameter-efficient fine-tuning strategies, particularly attention-driven layer-skipping mechanisms, have emerged as critical enablers for deploying large language models in federated clinical settings, allowing selective updating of task-relevant layers while substantially reducing communication overhead. The survey further highlights the demonstrated utility of federated learning across diverse medical data modalities, including clinical text classification, chest radiograph analysis, and electrocardiogram-based diagnostics, with particular benefits observed for smaller institutions operating under non-IID data distributions. Notably, the review identifies persistent gaps in intention-hiding mechanisms, data valuation frameworks under privacy constraints, and the integration of vertical partitioning with quantum resources, underscoring the nascent yet rapidly evolving nature of this interdisciplinary domain.

The significance of this survey lies in its integrative synthesis of three converging technological currents—federated learning, quantum computing, and healthcare artificial intelligence—within a unified analytical framework. By systematically cataloging architectural variants, training methodologies, and application scenarios, this work provides both researchers and practitioners with a structured understanding of the design space and trade-offs inherent in building privacy-preserving clinical learning systems. The survey bridges previously disconnected threads of research, connecting architectural choices in quantum federated topologies with the practical requirements of medical data governance, and illuminates how communication-efficient strategies can address the bandwidth constraints that have historically impeded federated deployment of large-scale models. Furthermore, by explicitly identifying underexplored areas such as intention-hiding protocols, decentralized quantum architectures, and fair data pricing mechanisms, this work establishes a research agenda that directly addresses the tension between collaborative model development and the stringent privacy demands of clinical environments. The translational relevance of these findings is substantial, as advances in this domain hold the potential to unlock multi-institutional medical studies at scales previously unattainable under current regulatory and technical constraints.

Realizing the transformative potential of federated learning in healthcare demands concerted effort across several fronts. The research community must prioritize rigorous empirical benchmarking of quantum, hybrid, and classical federated architectures on standardized clinical tasks to establish evidence-based guidance for architectural selection. Equally critical is the development of formal privacy guarantees and intention-hiding protocols adapted to quantum communication channels, ensuring that institutional participation patterns remain opaque to adversaries. The integration of adaptive layer selection strategies with quantum circuit design warrants systematic investigation, as the fundamental differences between classical neural network layers and quantum gate sequences may yield novel efficiency-accuracy trade-offs. Finally, the establishment of transparent and equitable data valuation frameworks will prove indispensable for fostering sustainable multi-institutional collaborations, ensuring that contributors of pre-training data are fairly incentivized while maintaining uncompromising privacy protections. By pursuing these directions with methodological rigor and clinical sensitivity, the field can advance toward robust, scalable, and ethically grounded federated learning systems that meaningfully improve diagnostic accuracy, treatment personalization, and ultimately patient outcomes across diverse healthcare settings.
