# 1 Abstract

Artificial intelligence is reshaping the landscape of drug discovery by enabling rapid virtual screening, de novo molecular design, and accurate property prediction. Central to this transformation are graph neural networks, which represent molecules as topology-aware graphs and learn predictive features through message passing mechanisms. This survey provides a systematic examination of recent advances in graph-based molecular representation learning, with a focus on message passing architectures, attention-enhanced aggregation, and adaptive substructure-aware expert models for molecular property prediction. We trace the evolution from classical molecular descriptors to bond-based directed message passing frameworks, analyze the role of attention in refining readout functions, and assess the sensitivity of mixture-of-experts architectures to hyperparameter configurations. Key limitations of current graph representations are identified, including their difficulty in capturing structural symmetry, long-range dependencies, and three-dimensional geometric information. By synthesizing findings across molecular featurization strategies, message passing algorithms, and adaptive expert models, this work clarifies the methodological trajectory of AI-aided drug discovery and delineates open challenges that must be addressed to realize the full potential of graph neural networks in computational chemistry.


# 2 Introduction

The discovery of novel therapeutic agents has long been a cornerstone of biomedical research, yet traditional drug development pipelines remain prohibitively time-consuming and costly, often requiring over a decade of research and billions of dollars in investment to bring a single drug to market. In recent years, artificial intelligence has emerged as a transformative force in this domain, fundamentally reshaping how researchers approach the identification and optimization of drug candidates. Virtual screening and de novo drug design, two pillars of early-stage drug discovery, are now routinely augmented by machine learning and deep learning architectures that can predict molecular properties, estimate synthetic feasibility, and prioritize compounds with unprecedented speed and accuracy. These computational advances have not only accelerated the exploration of vast chemical spaces but have also enabled a more systematic integration of data-driven insights into medicinal chemistry workflows. As a result, the intersection of artificial intelligence and drug discovery has become a vibrant and rapidly evolving field, attracting sustained attention from both computational and experimental communities seeking to reduce the attrition rates that have historically plagued pharmaceutical research.

Despite these advances, several fundamental challenges persist in the application of artificial intelligence to molecular modeling. One central problem concerns the representation of molecular structures in a form that machine learning models can effectively process. While graph neural networks have emerged as a natural choice—representing molecules as graphs where atoms are nodes and bonds are edges—this approach is not without limitations. Topology-aware graph representations excel at capturing local connectivity patterns but often struggle to encode higher-level geometric features, long-range dependencies, and structural symmetries that are critical for accurate property prediction. Furthermore, the design of message passing algorithms, which govern how information propagates through the molecular graph, remains an open research question. The choice between atom-based and bond-based message passing, the integration of attention mechanisms, and the handling of substructure-level information all introduce trade-offs that are not yet fully understood. Additionally, the sensitivity of adaptive expert models to hyperparameter configurations and the lack of systematic empirical comparisons between graph-based featurization and classical molecular descriptors further complicate the landscape. These unresolved issues underscore the need for a comprehensive synthesis of current methodologies and their relative merits, particularly as the field moves toward more sophisticated architectures that must balance representational expressiveness with computational tractability.

This survey paper makes two primary contributions to the literature on graph neural networks for drug discovery. First, it provides a structured and in-depth examination of molecular graph representation learning, with particular emphasis on the evolution from classical hand-crafted descriptors to modern graph-based approaches. We analyze the foundational principles of message passing neural networks, including both atom-based and bond-based variants, and discuss how directed message passing frameworks have been adapted to improve the fidelity of molecular encodings. Special attention is devoted to the role of attention mechanisms in refining message aggregation and readout functions, as well as to the emerging paradigm of substructure-aware expert models that seek to capture chemically meaningful fragments in a data-driven manner. By tracing these developments, we illuminate the conceptual underpinnings that have shaped the current generation of molecular graph neural networks and provide a coherent narrative of how methodological innovations have progressively addressed the limitations of earlier approaches.

Second, this survey offers a critical assessment of the practical challenges and open problems that define the frontier of this research area. We examine the inherent tension between topology-aware representations and the need to incorporate three-dimensional geometric information, such as bond lengths and angles, which are often essential for tasks like binding site prediction and quantum chemical property estimation. The survey further investigates the sensitivity of adaptive expert architectures to hyperparameter choices, the trade-offs involved in scaling message passing to large molecular libraries, and the comparative performance of graph-based methods relative to traditional descriptor-based models in quantitative structure–activity relationship modeling and activity-cliff prediction. By synthesizing insights from diverse methodological contributions, we aim to provide a balanced perspective that highlights both the demonstrated capabilities of graph neural networks and the limitations that warrant further investigation. This critical lens is essential for guiding future research efforts toward the most pressing unresolved challenges in the field.

The overarching aim of this survey is to serve as a comprehensive resource for researchers and practitioners seeking to understand the role of graph neural networks in molecular property prediction and drug discovery. We structure the paper as follows. We begin by introducing the fundamentals of molecular graph representation and the message passing framework, establishing the technical vocabulary necessary for subsequent discussion. We then review key algorithmic innovations, including directed message passing, attention-enhanced architectures, and substructure-aware expert models, situating each within the broader context of molecular modeling tasks. Following this, we examine the application of these methods to virtual screening, binding site prediction, and molecular property prediction, with a focus on the practical considerations that influence model selection and performance. Finally, we identify open challenges and outline promising directions for future research, emphasizing the need for more robust benchmarking, the integration of geometric information, and the development of adaptive mechanisms that can dynamically balance local and global molecular features. Through this structured exposition, we hope to provide clarity and guidance in a field that stands at the intersection of graph representation learning and computational drug discovery.


# 3 Graph Neural Networks for Molecular Modeling in Drug Discovery

## 3.1 Virtual Screening and De Novo Drug Design

Virtual screening and de novo drug design are increasingly being transformed by artificial intelligence, which is accelerating the identification and optimization of novel therapeutics. Tripathy et al. (2024) note that AI-driven approaches now enable predictive synthesis planning, reaction outcome prediction, and property estimation with unprecedented precision, thereby streamlining synthetic route design and the optimization of molecular properties for diverse applications. These methodological advancements support the broader integration of AI into computational chemistry workflows.

Graph neural networks have also cemented their role in modern molecular modeling within this domain. Zhang et al. (2025) provide a comprehensive overview of such methods, with their review dedicating specific attention to virtual screening and binding site prediction. While the available evidence from these sources does not detail specific algorithmic architectures or case-study results, it collectively underscores a landscape in which AI—particularly through graph-based representations—is becoming central to both screening large compound libraries and designing novel molecules with desired properties.

## 3.2 Topology-Aware Molecular Graph Representations

Topology-aware molecular graph representations form a foundational component of modern AI-aided drug discovery, as they explicitly encode the connectivity of atoms and bonds within a molecule. Zhang et al. (2025) note that these representations capture molecular topology, which facilitates compatibility with sequence-based models; however, they also observe that such representations inherently struggle to capture structural symmetry, reversibility, and long-range dependencies. This limitation highlights a key tension in representation design: while graph-based encodings preserve the natural relational structure of molecules, they may omit higher-level geometric or electronic properties that are critical for certain predictive tasks. The broader context in which these representations are deployed is one where AI techniques, including machine learning algorithms, neural networks, and deep learning architectures, have substantially improved the accuracy and efficiency of molecular structure prediction, quantum chemistry calculations, and molecular dynamics simulations (Tripathy et al., 2024).

## 3.3 GNNs vs Classical Featurisation for QSAR and Activity-Cliff Prediction

The application of artificial intelligence has broadly advanced molecular modeling tasks relevant to drug discovery. Tripathy et al. (2024) note that AI techniques, including machine learning and deep learning architectures, have improved the accuracy and efficiency of molecular structure prediction and quantum chemistry calculations, and they facilitate virtual screening and de novo drug design. Within this landscape, graph neural networks have become a central methodology. Zhang et al. (2025) provide a review of GNNs in drug discovery, covering their use in molecular property prediction and virtual screening. However, the available evidence does not offer a direct empirical comparison between GNN-based featurization and classical descriptors for quantitative structure–activity relationship modeling or activity-cliff prediction.

# 4 Message Passing and Meta-Learning for Molecular Property Prediction

## 4.1 Bond-Based Message Passing in D-MPNN

In bond-based message passing, as employed in Directed Message Passing Neural Networks (D-MPNN), the message passing algorithm operates on bonds rather than atoms. Gong and Maday (2023) apply this bond-based message passing algorithm, inspired by D-MPNN, to update both bond states and atom states. Their approach incorporates a scaled dot-product attention mechanism into the message passing process, and for molecule-level prediction tasks, they further employ an attention-based readout function (Gong & Maday, 2023).

## 4.2 Adaptive Substructure-Aware Expert Models

The development of adaptive substructure-aware expert models has been explored as a direction for improving molecular property prediction. Nguyen et al. (2020) introduced a mixture-of-experts approach, TopExpert, in which the number of experts and a key parameter ψ must be carefully tuned for each scenario using a validation set. Their evidence shows that on the ClinTox and BBBP datasets, excessively large ψ values introduce redundant or noisy substructures that degrade performance. This highlights the sensitivity of substructure-aware expert architectures to hyperparameter choices and the need for adaptive mechanisms to control the granularity of learned substructures. Gong and Maday (2023) identify the design of better message passing algorithms as another possible direction for advancing molecular representation learning, noting that existing pre-training strategies already incorporate chemical environment matching and 3D geometric information such as bond lengths and angles. While their work does not directly propose a substructure-aware expert model, the call for improved message passing suggests a pathway for integrating adaptive, substructure-level reasoning into graph neural network architectures.

## 4.3 Molecular Property Prediction as Graph-Level Classification

Molecular property prediction is a fundamental task in cheminformatics and bioinformatics, with critical applications in drug discovery and toxicity assessment (Nguyen et al., 2020). When formulated as a graph-level classification problem, each molecule is represented as a graph where nodes correspond to atoms and edges to chemical bonds, and the goal is to predict a molecular property by aggregating information across the entire structure. Nguyen et al. (2020) note that traditional approaches to this task often rely on hand-crafted molecular descriptors paired with machine learning models such as random forests or support vector machines.

More recently, graph neural networks (GNNs) following the Message Passing Neural Network (MPNN) framework have been applied to molecular property prediction, where model performance is determined by the specific message passing algorithm employed (Gong & Maday, 2023). Gong and Maday (2023) develop a directed message passing approach inspired by Directed MPNN (D-MPNN), applying a bond-based message passing algorithm that operates over the molecular graph to produce a graph-level representation suitable for property prediction. Their work situates molecular property prediction alongside related tasks such as molecule graph generation, reflecting the broader role of GNNs in learning from molecular graph structures.

# 5 Future Directions

The current landscape of molecular representation learning is constrained by several interrelated limitations that prevent graph neural networks from fully capturing the complexity of chemical systems. Topology-aware graph encodings, while effective at representing atomic connectivity, inherently struggle to encode structural symmetry, molecular reversibility, and long-range dependencies that are essential for accurate property prediction. Bond-based message passing algorithms, despite their advantages over atom-centric approaches, typically treat all molecular regions with uniform granularity and lack mechanisms to adaptively modulate information flow based on substructure importance. The sensitivity of adaptive expert architectures to hyperparameter configurations further complicates practical deployment, as suboptimal partitioning of molecular fragments can introduce noise and degrade predictive performance. Moreover, the integration of three-dimensional geometric information, electrostatic potentials, and quantum mechanical properties into predominantly topology-focused representations remains an open challenge, with existing pre-training strategies only partially bridging this gap. These limitations collectively underscore the need for more flexible, geometry-aware, and adaptively attentive molecular modeling frameworks.

Future research should prioritize the development of hybrid representation learning architectures that seamlessly integrate topological graph encodings with three-dimensional conformational and electronic structure information. Such frameworks could employ multi-modal designs where graph neural networks process bond connectivity while parallel geometric deep learning modules capture spatial relationships, electrostatic surfaces, and quantum mechanical properties, with cross-modal attention mechanisms fusing these complementary representations. A particularly promising direction involves designing message passing algorithms with built-in gating functions that dynamically adjust their receptive fields based on local chemical context, enabling models to automatically learn which substructures warrant finer-grained processing without requiring manual hyperparameter tuning. The incorporation of equivariance constraints into these hybrid architectures would ensure that learned representations respect the physical symmetries of molecular systems, potentially improving generalization to novel chemical scaffolds. Additionally, self-supervised pre-training objectives that jointly optimize for topology reconstruction, geometry prediction, and electronic property estimation could encourage representations that capture complementary aspects of molecular identity. Rigorous empirical benchmarking across diverse prediction tasks, including quantitative structure-activity relationship modeling and activity-cliff prediction, will be essential to establish clear performance comparisons between graph-based featurization and classical molecular descriptors.

The proposed advances would substantially impact both the accuracy and practical applicability of artificial intelligence in drug discovery pipelines. More expressive molecular representations that capture geometry and electronic structure alongside topology could improve virtual screening hit rates by better distinguishing true activity cliffs from gradual structure-activity trends, directly addressing a persistent challenge in early-stage drug discovery. Adaptive substructure-aware architectures with learned gating mechanisms would reduce the engineering burden associated with model deployment across diverse prediction tasks, enabling more robust performance without task-specific hyperparameter optimization. In de novo drug design, geometry-aware generative models could propose synthetically accessible molecules with optimized binding poses and favorable pharmacokinetic profiles earlier in the design cycle. Ultimately, these methodological improvements would accelerate the translation of computational predictions into experimentally validated therapeutic candidates, narrowing the gap between in silico screening and clinical impact while reducing the time and cost associated with traditional drug development.


# 6 Conclusion

This survey has examined the evolving role of artificial intelligence in virtual screening and de novo drug design, with a particular emphasis on graph neural networks and molecular representation learning. The main findings indicate that AI-driven methodologies now enable predictive synthesis planning, reaction outcome prediction, and property estimation with remarkable precision, thereby accelerating the identification and optimization of novel therapeutics. Graph neural networks have emerged as a central paradigm, offering topology-aware molecular representations that explicitly encode atomic connectivity and bond structures. However, these representations face inherent limitations in capturing structural symmetry, reversibility, and long-range dependencies, revealing a persistent tension between preserving relational molecular structure and encoding higher-level geometric or electronic properties. Bond-based message passing algorithms, such as those employed in Directed Message Passing Neural Networks, represent a notable advancement by operating directly on bonds rather than atoms and incorporating attention mechanisms to refine both bond and atom states. Furthermore, the exploration of adaptive substructure-aware expert models highlights the potential for more granular molecular reasoning, though such architectures exhibit sensitivity to hyperparameter choices and require careful tuning to avoid performance degradation from redundant substructures.

The significance of this survey lies in its synthesis of two rapidly converging domains: artificial intelligence and computational drug discovery. By integrating perspectives on molecular representation, graph neural network architectures, and message passing strategies, this work provides a cohesive understanding of how AI is reshaping the molecular modeling landscape. The analysis clarifies the foundational role of graph-based representations while critically examining their limitations, thereby offering researchers a balanced view of current capabilities and persistent challenges. This synthesis is particularly timely given the accelerating adoption of AI in pharmaceutical research, where the ability to efficiently screen vast compound libraries and design molecules with desired properties has direct implications for reducing the time and cost of drug development. By highlighting both methodological advancements and unresolved tensions in representation design, this survey serves as a reference point for guiding future research directions and informing the development of more robust molecular learning frameworks.

Moving forward, the field must address the fundamental challenge of developing molecular representations that simultaneously capture topological connectivity, three-dimensional geometry, and electronic properties without sacrificing computational efficiency. Researchers should prioritize the design of message passing algorithms that can adaptively incorporate substructure-level reasoning while maintaining robustness to hyperparameter variations. Additionally, rigorous empirical comparisons between graph neural network-based featurization and classical molecular descriptors are needed to establish clear performance benchmarks across diverse prediction tasks, including quantitative structure–activity relationship modeling and activity-cliff prediction. The integration of chemical environment matching and geometric information into pre-training strategies offers a promising pathway, yet further innovation is required to fully realize the potential of AI in drug discovery. Ultimately, sustained interdisciplinary collaboration between computational chemists, machine learning researchers, and pharmaceutical scientists will be essential to translate these methodological advances into tangible therapeutic outcomes.
