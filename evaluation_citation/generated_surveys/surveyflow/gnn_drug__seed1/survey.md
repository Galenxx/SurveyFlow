# 1 Abstract

The integration of artificial intelligence into computational chemistry has fundamentally transformed drug discovery, with virtual screening and de novo molecular design emerging as central pillars of this revolution. Graph neural networks have demonstrated remarkable efficacy in modeling molecular structures by representing atoms and bonds as nodes and edges within graph-based architectures, enabling sophisticated property prediction and molecular generation tasks. This survey provides a comprehensive examination of graph neural network methodologies applied to virtual screening and molecular property prediction, synthesizing recent advances across directed message passing frameworks, attention mechanisms, meta-learning strategies, and comparative evaluations with classical featurization techniques. Key findings reveal that directed graph attention networks operating on bond-centric representations offer distinct advantages for capturing asymmetric chemical interactions, while meta-learning approaches show promise for improving generalization in low-resource molecular property prediction scenarios. The convergence of attention-based message passing with domain-aware molecular representations establishes a coherent foundation for both virtual screening pipelines and generative design workflows, though challenges remain in computational efficiency, substructure-aware modeling, and the integration of topological information. This survey consolidates the methodological landscape of graph neural networks in computational drug discovery, highlighting the critical interplay between architectural design choices and molecular representation strategies that underpins contemporary AI-driven therapeutic development.


# 2 Introduction

The integration of artificial intelligence into drug discovery has fundamentally transformed the landscape of computational chemistry, enabling researchers to navigate the vast chemical space with unprecedented speed and accuracy. Traditional approaches to identifying and optimizing therapeutic candidates have long been constrained by the sheer scale of combinatorial possibilities and the complexity of molecular interactions. In recent years, AI-driven methodologies have emerged as powerful tools that address these limitations, offering predictive capabilities that span synthesis planning, reaction outcome prediction, and molecular property estimation. These advances have proven particularly valuable in virtual screening and de novo drug design, where the ability to rapidly evaluate and generate compounds with desired pharmacological profiles can dramatically accelerate the drug development pipeline. The convergence of deep learning with molecular science has thus established a new paradigm in which computational models serve as indispensable partners in the quest for novel therapeutics.

Despite these promising developments, several fundamental challenges persist in the application of machine learning to molecular discovery. A central difficulty lies in the representation of molecular structures in forms that are both computationally tractable and chemically meaningful. Molecules possess inherent structural complexities—including directional bonds, stereochemistry, and long-range dependencies—that are not easily captured by conventional feature engineering approaches. Furthermore, the scarcity of labeled experimental data for many biological targets creates significant obstacles for supervised learning methods, which typically require large training sets to achieve robust generalization. The problem is compounded by the need to distinguish between general quantitative structure–activity relationships and the more nuanced phenomenon of activity cliffs, where structurally similar compounds exhibit dramatically different biological activities. These challenges demand sophisticated modeling frameworks that can extract rich, substructure-aware representations while remaining data-efficient and sensitive to the subtle geometric and topological features that govern molecular behavior.

This survey provides a comprehensive and structured examination of graph neural network architectures for drug discovery and molecular property prediction, synthesizing recent methodological advances into a coherent taxonomy. The primary contribution is a detailed analysis of directed message passing frameworks that operate on bond-level representations, with particular attention to attention-based mechanisms that capture asymmetric chemical interactions. We systematically review how these architectures adapt the general message-passing paradigm to the specific requirements of molecular graphs, including the mathematical formulations that underpin directed bond state updates and the integration of neighborhood information through attention weighting. By tracing the evolution from undirected graph models to directed attention networks, this survey illuminates the design principles that enable these systems to model the directional nature of chemical bonds and improve predictive accuracy on molecular property tasks.

A second key contribution is the critical evaluation of strategies for addressing data scarcity and generalization challenges in molecular learning. We examine meta-learning approaches that learn better initializations for graph neural networks, enabling effective fine-tuning on limited target data while promoting substructure-aware representations. Additionally, this survey presents a comparative analysis of graph neural network representations against classical featurization techniques for both general quantitative structure–activity relationship modeling and the specialized problem of activity cliff prediction. This comparative perspective highlights the relative strengths and limitations of deep learning-based and traditional feature extraction methods, offering practical guidance for practitioners selecting appropriate molecular representations for specific predictive tasks. The survey also identifies open challenges and promising research directions, including the development of topology-aware architectures capable of capturing structural symmetries and long-range dependencies that remain underexplored in current molecular graph models.

The aim of this survey is to equip researchers and practitioners with a clear understanding of the current state of graph neural network methodologies for molecular discovery, while providing a structured roadmap of the field. We begin by establishing the foundational concepts of molecular graph representation and the message-passing paradigm, then progress through directed and attention-based architectures, meta-learning strategies for data-efficient prediction, and comparative evaluations with classical approaches. Each section is designed to build upon the preceding material, culminating in a discussion of emerging challenges and future opportunities. By organizing the literature along these thematic lines, this survey serves both as an accessible entry point for newcomers and as a comprehensive reference for experienced researchers seeking to understand the methodological landscape of graph neural networks in drug discovery.

# 4 Message Passing and Meta-Learning for Molecular Graphs

## 4.1 Bond-Based Directed Message Passing Neural Networks

Gong and Maday (2023) introduce a directed message passing framework that operates on bonds rather than atoms, explicitly modeling the directional flow of information between bonded atoms. In this approach, each bond is represented as a directed edge, and messages are computed using an attention mechanism. The directed bond states are then updated through a multilayer perceptron (MLP) with a residual connection and layer normalization, following the form \(h^{t+1}_{\vec{p}(ij)} = W_e^2 (\sigma(W_e^1 (\text{LayerNorm}(h^t_{\vec{p}(ij)} + m^{t+1}_{\vec{p}(ij)}))))\), where \(m^{t+1}_{\vec{p}(ij)}\) denotes the incoming message and \(h^t_{\vec{p}(ij)}\) is the previous directed bond state (Gong & Maday, 2023). This update function allows the model to iteratively refine bond-level representations by integrating neighborhood information while preserving the directed nature of chemical bonds.

The broader context for such architectures lies in the message-passing paradigm of graph neural networks, where representations are computed layer-wise by aggregating information from neighboring nodes and edges (Nguyen et al., 2020). While Nguyen et al. (2020) describe this general mechanism for node-level updates, Gong and Maday (2023) adapt the principle to a bond-centric directed scheme, demonstrating how message passing can be tailored to capture asymmetric chemical interactions.

## 4.2 Attention Mechanisms in Molecular Graph Representation

Molecules are naturally represented as graphs, with atoms as nodes and bonds as edges, making graph neural networks a suitable framework for molecular property prediction (Gong & Maday, 2023; Nguyen et al., 2020). Building on the success of attention mechanisms in natural language processing, graph attention networks were introduced to weigh the importance of neighboring nodes during message passing (Gong & Maday, 2023). By modeling molecules as molecular graphs, these deep learning methods transform property prediction tasks into graph-level classification or regression problems (Nguyen et al., 2020).

## 4.3 Graph-Level Classification and Meta-Learning for Molecular Graphs

Graph-level classification in molecular property prediction has been advanced through architectures that explicitly account for the directional nature of chemical bonds. Gong and Maday (2023) introduced Directed Graph Attention Networks (D-GATs), a model that applies attention-based message passing on directed molecular graphs and couples the resulting representations with a linear classifier for downstream tasks. The authors note that D-GATs is specifically designed for the small-size graphs typical of most molecular property prediction problems, although they acknowledge it is less efficient than undirected graph models in certain settings.

Beyond architectural innovations, meta-learning has been explored as a strategy to improve generalization when labeled molecular data are scarce. Nguyen et al. (2020) observe that conventional GNN-based methods often treat molecular graphs as undifferentiated wholes, overlooking the distinct roles of different substructures, which limits their ability to capture rich molecular representations and hinders generalization. Their work targets low-resource molecular property prediction by learning better initializations for GNNs through meta-learning, aiming to address these substructure-awareness and data-efficiency challenges.

# 6 Future Directions

The current landscape of graph neural networks for molecular discovery is constrained by several fundamental limitations that impede progress toward robust and generalizable predictive models. Existing architectures predominantly treat molecular graphs as undirected or only partially directed structures, failing to capture the asymmetric electronic effects and directional nature of chemical bonding that govern molecular reactivity and binding behavior. Most message-passing schemes operate on atom-centric representations and lack explicit mechanisms for encoding higher-order structural motifs such as ring systems, symmetric scaffolds, and long-range spatial dependencies that critically influence molecular properties. The scarcity of labeled experimental data for many therapeutic targets remains a persistent bottleneck, with current meta-learning approaches offering only partial solutions that often sacrifice substructure-specific information during transfer learning. Furthermore, the absence of topology-aware inductive biases means that models cannot reliably distinguish between chemically equivalent and non-equivalent positions within symmetric molecules, limiting their capacity to identify activity cliffs and subtle structure-activity relationships that define successful drug candidates.

Future research should prioritize the development of topology-aware graph neural network architectures that explicitly incorporate molecular symmetry, ring topology, and long-range spatial dependencies into directed message-passing frameworks. Such architectures could extend bond-centric representations to operate on higher-order structural motifs, enabling models to propagate information across extended molecular graphs without degradation while maintaining awareness of chemically distinct substructural environments. Integrating physical inductive biases derived from quantum chemistry and molecular mechanics directly into attention mechanisms represents a promising direction for achieving more faithful representations of electronic structure and non-covalent interactions. Additionally, meta-learning and few-shot learning paradigms require refinement to address data scarcity through pretraining strategies that span diverse molecular tasks while explicitly preserving substructure-specific information during rapid adaptation to new targets. The development of hybrid models that combine the representational flexibility of graph neural networks with the interpretability of classical pharmacophore and shape-based methods could bridge the critical gap between predictive accuracy and mechanistic understanding, offering medicinal chemists actionable insights into the molecular features driving model predictions. Rigorous benchmarking of emerging architectures against established baselines across diverse prediction tasks and data regimes will be essential to validate these methodological advances and ensure that added architectural complexity yields commensurate performance gains.

Advancing topology-aware graph neural networks and meta-learning strategies for molecular property prediction would fundamentally transform the early-stage drug discovery pipeline by enabling more reliable identification of activity cliffs and subtle property differences that currently elude computational methods. More accurate models of structure-activity relationships would reduce reliance on costly high-throughput screening campaigns and empower researchers to explore broader chemical spaces with greater confidence in predicted binding affinities and pharmacological profiles. Improved generalization across chemical series and target classes would accelerate hit-to-lead optimization and facilitate drug repurposing efforts by revealing latent structure-property relationships invisible to current approaches. The integration of physical principles with data-driven learning would yield more interpretable models that foster trust among medicinal chemists and enable more effective human-AI collaboration in designing next-generation therapeutics. Ultimately, these advances would shorten the timeline from initial screen to clinical candidate while increasing the probability of success by focusing experimental resources on molecules with genuinely novel and optimizable binding modes, thereby addressing one of the most pressing challenges in contemporary pharmaceutical research.


# 7 Conclusion

This survey has systematically examined the integration of graph neural networks into computational drug discovery, with particular emphasis on virtual screening and molecular property prediction. The analysis reveals that directed message passing frameworks operating on bond-centric representations offer distinct advantages for capturing the asymmetric nature of chemical interactions, as exemplified by Directed Graph Attention Networks that employ attention-based updates to refine bond-level states. Meta-learning strategies have emerged as promising solutions to the persistent challenge of data scarcity, enabling improved generalization in low-resource molecular property prediction scenarios by learning transferable initializations that preserve substructure-specific information. The comparative evaluation of graph-based representations against classical featurization techniques for quantitative structure–activity relationship modeling and activity-cliff prediction highlights the nuanced trade-offs between deep learning approaches and traditional methods, demonstrating that the choice of molecular representation must be carefully aligned with the specific predictive task and available data resources. Across these methodological threads, a coherent narrative emerges: the progressive incorporation of domain-aware chemical knowledge into neural architectures is yielding models with greater fidelity to the underlying physics of molecular systems.

The significance of this survey resides in its structured synthesis of architectural innovations, learning paradigms, and empirical benchmarking studies that collectively define the contemporary landscape of AI-aided molecular discovery. By tracing the evolution from undirected graph models to directed attention networks, the survey illuminates the design principles that enable neural architectures to model directional bonding and asymmetric electronic effects, thereby advancing beyond generic graph convolutions toward chemically informed representations. The focused examination of meta-learning for low-resource prediction addresses a critical bottleneck in drug discovery, where labeled experimental data for novel therapeutic targets remain scarce and expensive to obtain. Furthermore, the comparative perspective on featurization strategies situates graph neural networks within the broader historical context of molecular representation, offering practitioners a balanced assessment of when deep learning provides tangible benefits over classical methods and when simpler approaches may suffice. This integrated perspective serves both as an accessible entry point for newcomers seeking to understand the methodological foundations of the field and as a comprehensive reference for experienced researchers navigating the rapidly expanding literature on graph-based molecular learning.

The path forward demands concerted effort across multiple fronts to translate methodological advances into practical impact on therapeutic development. Researchers should prioritize the development of topology-aware architectures that explicitly encode structural symmetries, ring systems, and long-range dependencies, extending directed message passing to higher-order motifs that capture the geometric and electronic complexity governing molecular behavior. Equally important is the rigorous benchmarking of emerging models against established baselines across diverse prediction tasks and data regimes, ensuring that reported improvements reflect genuine advances rather than artifacts of experimental design. The integration of physical inductive biases from quantum chemistry and molecular mechanics into attention mechanisms holds particular promise, though such efforts require careful validation to confirm that added complexity yields commensurate gains in predictive accuracy and interpretability. Finally, fostering a culture of open science through the sharing of code, data, and experimental protocols will be essential to accelerate progress, enable reproducible research, and build cumulative understanding of how best to harness artificial intelligence for the discovery of novel therapeutic agents. The convergence of methodological rigor, domain expertise, and collaborative infrastructure will determine the pace at which computational approaches fulfill their potential to transform early-stage drug discovery.
