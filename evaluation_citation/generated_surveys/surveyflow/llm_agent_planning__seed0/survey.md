# 1 Abstract

The deployment of large language model (LLM)-based autonomous agents in open-world environments has exposed fundamental limitations in planning reliability, as single-plan generation frequently proves suboptimal or infeasible due to task complexity and model uncertainty. This survey provides a structured examination of recent advances in LLM agent planning, with particular emphasis on multi-plan selection strategies, efficiency optimization through symbolic integration, and modular framework design for grounded, tool-augmented workflows. We analyze five representative categories of planning methods, evaluate their performance across diverse benchmarks, and investigate the growing role of environment simulation and active tool retrieval in improving long-horizon adaptability and error recovery. Key findings reveal that modular architectures separating planning logic from execution facilitate dynamic tool chaining and robust error handling, while reinforcement learning with intermediate rewards enhances faithful reasoning in agentic search paradigms. The survey further identifies critical gaps in multi-agent coordination benchmarks and underscores the importance of white-box optimization for computationally efficient agent workflows. By synthesizing developments across personal device agents, computational chemistry assistants, and retrieval-augmented reasoning systems, this work offers a comprehensive perspective on the current landscape and future trajectories of planning in LLM-based agents.


# 2 Introduction

The development of autonomous agents powered by large language models has emerged as a transformative paradigm in artificial intelligence, enabling systems that can perceive, reason, and act in complex environments. These agents leverage the remarkable generative and inferential capabilities of large language models to decompose high-level objectives, interact with external tools, and adapt their behavior across diverse domains ranging from personal assistance to scientific discovery. As the field rapidly evolves, the ability of these agents to plan effectively—constructing coherent, multi-step strategies and executing them reliably—has become a central focus of research. The integration of tool use further extends their capabilities, allowing agents to ground abstract reasoning in concrete actions such as querying databases, running simulations, or manipulating software interfaces. This convergence of planning, reasoning, and tool invocation defines the current frontier of agent design, where the goal is not merely to generate plausible text but to orchestrate purposeful, context-aware sequences of operations that achieve meaningful outcomes in the real world.

Despite significant progress, several fundamental challenges persist in the design and evaluation of large language model agents. A primary concern is the inherent unreliability of single-plan generation, where agents frequently produce suboptimal or infeasible plans due to task complexity and model uncertainty. This limitation is compounded by the neglect of plan efficiency, as agents typically generate actions greedily without explicit optimization for resource usage or execution time. In open-world settings, agents face large decision spaces, underspecified requirements, and numerous interacting constraints that make long-horizon planning especially brittle. Error recovery remains a critical weakness, with empirical studies revealing recurring failure modes such as incorrect tool arguments and flawed result summarization. Furthermore, the black-box nature of large language model inference introduces structural inefficiencies, preventing runtime systems from exploiting workflow-level dependencies and leading to redundant computation. These interconnected problems highlight the need for more robust planning methodologies, principled tool-use strategies, and comprehensive evaluation frameworks that can systematically assess agent performance under realistic conditions.

This survey makes two primary contributions to the understanding of planning and reasoning in large language model agents. First, it provides a structured taxonomy of planning methods, organizing existing approaches into distinct categories that reflect different strategies for addressing the challenges of plan generation, selection, and optimization. By examining multi-plan selection techniques that generate and evaluate candidate plans rather than relying on a single output, the survey illuminates how diversity-aware strategies can mitigate the risks of plan infeasibility. It further explores the integration of symbolic planning models with large language models as a promising direction for improving plan efficiency without modifying the underlying model architecture. The survey also investigates modular frameworks that decouple planning logic from execution and tool integration, enabling components to be updated independently as the underlying language model ecosystem advances. This architectural perspective is essential for building agents that can evolve alongside rapid improvements in foundation model capabilities, and it is exemplified by domain-specific frameworks in computational chemistry and personal device assistance that maintain adaptability through clean separation of concerns.

Second, the survey offers a comprehensive analysis of tool-use paradigms and evaluation benchmarks that shape the development and assessment of agent capabilities. It examines the shift from static tool assignment to active tool retrieval from open registries, where agents must dynamically reason about which tools to invoke based on abstract user intent. The discussion extends to agentic search, a paradigm in which models are trained via reinforcement learning to plan queries, interact with retrievers, and reason over evidence through multi-turn trajectories. This treatment connects tool-use accuracy with reward design, contrasting outcome-based optimization with intermediate reward strategies that encourage more deliberate and faithful reasoning. The survey also addresses the emerging role of environment creation for agent evaluation, where simulated tool-use dynamics and diverse planning scenarios enable systematic testing of error recovery and adaptation. By synthesizing these methodological and evaluative dimensions, the survey provides a unified perspective on the current state and future directions of planning and reasoning in large language model agents, highlighting both the progress achieved and the critical gaps that remain in multi-agent coordination benchmarks and white-box optimization techniques.

The aim of this survey is to provide researchers and practitioners with a structured overview of the methods, tool-use strategies, and evaluation benchmarks that define the landscape of planning and reasoning in large language model agents. The paper is organized as follows. It begins by establishing foundational concepts and categorizing planning methods into representative directions, followed by an in-depth examination of tool-use paradigms including active retrieval, agentic search, and modular framework design. The survey then turns to evaluation, discussing benchmarks for planning performance, tool-use accuracy, and error recovery, with attention to environment-based assessment approaches. Finally, it identifies open challenges and promising research directions, offering a roadmap for advancing the reliability, efficiency, and adaptability of autonomous agents in complex, real-world settings.

The survey's scope is further distinguished by its emphasis on the manipulation of personal data and interaction with personal devices, setting it apart from earlier treatments of agent planning.

## 3.2 Skill Selection and Efficiency in Personal LLM Agents

A central challenge in personal LLM agents is that a single plan generated by the LLM is often suboptimal or even infeasible due to task complexity and the inherent uncertainty of the model (Li et al., 2024). Li et al. (2024) propose that a more natural approach is multi-plan selection, which involves generating multiple candidate plans and then selecting among them. This strategy addresses the diversity of plans that an LLM agent can produce for a given task, moving beyond reliance on a single, potentially flawed output.

Beyond plan feasibility, the efficiency of generated plans is a critical but often overlooked issue. Li et al. (2024) observe that in existing LLM agents, planning is typically performed greedily based on the LLM’s immediate output, without explicit consideration of plan efficiency. The authors suggest that connecting LLMs with symbolic planning models—without altering the LLM itself—represents a promising future direction for generating more efficient plans. At a foundational level, the LLM serves as the core that supports the agent’s diverse skills, including responsive skills that directly execute user requests such as question answering (Li et al., 2024).

# 4 Modular Environments for Agentic Planning

## 4.1 Modular Frameworks and Declarative Layers for Grounded Planning

Recent work highlights the persistent challenges that autonomous agents face in grounded planning scenarios, including large decision spaces, underspecified requirements, and numerous interacting constraints. In open-world settings, agents are expected to decompose complex goals into multi-step plans, coordinate diverse sub-tasks, and adapt their strategies as situations evolve, with tool use serving as the primary interface for acting on the world (Xi et al., 2026). These demands motivate modular and declarative approaches that can structure planning while remaining adaptable to changing conditions.

To address these challenges, modular frameworks are being developed that separate planning logic from execution and tool integration, allowing components to be updated or replaced with minimal overhead. Pham et al. (2025) describe ChemGraph, an agentic framework designed for computational chemistry and materials science workflows, noting that its architecture is suited to incorporate new models and methods as the LLM ecosystem advances. This design principle—building frameworks that can evolve alongside underlying language model capabilities—exemplifies the modular philosophy that is increasingly central to grounded planning research.

## 4.2 Code-Empowered Agents for Tabular Reasoning and Chemistry Workflows

While the provided evidence does not detail tabular reasoning agents, it does support discussion of code-empowered agents in chemistry workflows. Pham et al. (2025) describe an agentic framework integrated with cheminformatics tools, enabling assistance with tasks such as molecular property prediction, similarity searching, and drug-likeness assessment. The authors also note that White and colleagues introduced MDCrow, an LLM agent assistant capable of performing molecular dynamics workflows. These examples illustrate how code-empowered agents are being applied to automate and support complex computational chemistry pipelines.

In the broader context of agent tool use, Xi et al. (2026) highlight a shift in recent efforts toward active tool retrieval from open registries, marking a transition from merely using pre-assigned tools to selecting and reasoning about which tools to invoke. Their work curates trajectories from seed tasks, isolating the cognitively demanding phase where agents must translate abstract user intent into concrete tool search and invocation, yielding a compact dataset of 1,170 samples. This emphasis on tool selection and reasoning is directly relevant to chemistry workflows, where agents must dynamically identify and chain appropriate cheminformatics or simulation tools to fulfill complex research objectives.

## 4.3 Environment Creation for Long-Horizon Planning and Error Recovery

Recent research highlights that agents operating in complex environments with large decision spaces, underspecified requirements, and many interacting constraints frequently fail to maintain coherent long-horizon plans, adapt to evolving situations, or recover from intermediate errors (Xi et al., 2026). To address these challenges, environment creation has emerged as a promising direction. Xi et al. (2026) extend the concept of world models—well-studied in reinforcement learning for learning environment dynamics to support planning and data generation—to tool-use settings, simulating realistic tool interactions for agent development and evaluation.

The practical importance of error recovery is underscored by empirical findings from computational chemistry workflows. Pham et al. (2025) report that across multiple models, the most common errors involve incorrect tool arguments and issues during result summarization, such as missing values or confusion between float and complex number formats in final reports. These recurring failure modes illustrate the need for environments that not only support long-horizon planning but also expose agents to realistic, recoverable error conditions. By constructing environments that model tool-use dynamics and generate diverse planning scenarios, researchers can systematically evaluate and improve an agent's ability to adapt when intermediate steps go wrong.

## 5.2 Reinforcement Learning for Query Planning and Multi-Turn Reasoning

Recent work has highlighted a shift toward agentic search, a paradigm in which large language models are trained via reinforcement learning to dynamically plan, issue queries to a retriever, and reason over returned evidence as part of an interactive trajectory (Xu et al., 2025). This approach moves beyond static retrieval-augmented generation by treating the search and reasoning process as a sequential decision-making problem, where the model learns to optimize its querying behavior over multiple turns.

Complex agentic workflows often involve modular, collaborative, and adaptive processes that can include mixture-of-experts reasoning, map-reduce summarization, ReAct-style reasoning, and cascade summarization (Shen et al., 2025). These patterns require the model to coordinate multiple invocations of itself or other components, making query planning and multi-turn reasoning central to effective execution. The evidence from Xu et al. (2025) frames reinforcement learning as the training mechanism that enables such dynamic planning and interactive reasoning, while Shen et al. (2025) illustrate the structural diversity of workflows that this training must support.

## 5.3 Agentic Workflow Optimization and Tool-Use Accuracy

Recent work on agentic workflows has drawn attention to the tension between treating large language model inference as a black-box API and the need for more efficient, structurally aware optimization. Shen et al. (2025) argue that when agent behaviors are defined on top of a black-box LLM API, the runtime cannot exploit structural dependencies within the workflow, resulting in redundant computations and suboptimal hardware utilization. Their proposed white-box approach, Halo, is designed to overcome this limitation by opening up the inference process to workflow-level optimization.

In parallel, research on tool-use accuracy has explored how reward design can shape more deliberate and faithful reasoning in retrieval-augmented generation. Xu et al. (2025) observe that while outcome-based methods optimize solely for final-answer correctness, a complementary line of work introduces intermediate rewards to encourage more careful search or reasoning processes. These intermediate reward strategies aim to improve the quality of tool interactions beyond surface-level answer accuracy, promoting behaviors that are more aligned with faithful tool use.

# 6 Future Directions

Current approaches to planning in large language model agents remain constrained by several fundamental limitations. Single-plan generation strategies are frequently suboptimal or infeasible, particularly in complex environments characterized by large decision spaces, underspecified requirements, and numerous interacting constraints. Although multi-plan selection has been proposed as a remedy, existing methods rarely incorporate explicit efficiency optimization, instead relying on greedy generation based on immediate model outputs. Furthermore, agents continue to struggle with maintaining coherent long-horizon plans, adapting to evolving situations, and recovering from intermediate errors, as evidenced by recurring failure modes such as incorrect tool arguments and result summarization errors. The prevailing black-box treatment of language model inference in agentic workflows compounds these issues by preventing runtime optimization of structural dependencies, leading to redundant computation and suboptimal hardware utilization. Additionally, while active tool retrieval from open registries represents an advance over static tool assignment, the cognitively demanding process of translating abstract user intent into concrete tool search and invocation remains a bottleneck that current systems address only partially.

Future research should pursue the integration of large language models with symbolic planning models to explicitly optimize plan efficiency without modifying the underlying language model. This hybrid approach would combine the flexibility and semantic understanding of neural models with the formal guarantees and optimization capabilities of symbolic planners, enabling agents to generate plans that are not only feasible but also resource-aware. Complementing this direction, modular and declarative frameworks should be developed that cleanly separate planning logic from execution and tool integration, allowing individual components to be updated or replaced as the language model ecosystem evolves. Such frameworks would facilitate systematic evaluation and iterative improvement of planning strategies across diverse domains. A second critical direction involves constructing rich simulated environments that model realistic tool-use dynamics and generate diverse planning scenarios with recoverable error conditions. By extending world-model concepts from reinforcement learning to tool-use settings, researchers can systematically expose agents to intermediate failures and evaluate their capacity for adaptive recovery. These environments should support the development of reinforcement learning approaches that train agents to dynamically plan, issue queries, and reason over evidence through multi-turn interactive trajectories. Within this training paradigm, intermediate reward mechanisms should be explored to promote deliberate and faithful tool use beyond surface-level answer correctness. Finally, white-box optimization techniques that expose workflow-level structural dependencies to the inference runtime should be advanced to eliminate redundant computation and improve hardware utilization in complex agentic workflows involving mixture-of-experts reasoning, map-reduce summarization, and cascade processes.

The proposed directions hold substantial potential to transform the reliability and efficiency of language model agents in real-world applications. By enabling explicit efficiency optimization and robust error recovery, future agents could be deployed with greater confidence in high-stakes domains such as computational chemistry, where incorrect tool arguments or summarization errors can compromise entire research workflows. Modular architectures that evolve alongside advancing language model capabilities would reduce maintenance overhead and accelerate adoption across scientific and industrial settings. Moreover, training agents through reinforcement learning in realistic simulated environments would yield systems that not only produce correct final answers but also exhibit faithful, deliberate reasoning throughout their planning and tool-use processes. The combination of symbolic integration, modular design, environment-driven training, and white-box optimization would collectively address the core challenges of long-horizon planning, adaptability, and computational efficiency, ultimately enabling autonomous agents to operate more effectively in the complex, open-world scenarios that characterize practical deployment.


# 7 Conclusion

In this survey, we have examined the evolving landscape of planning and reasoning in LLM-based agents, with a particular focus on the challenges and opportunities that arise in grounded, tool-use settings. Our analysis reveals that single-plan generation remains fundamentally limited by task complexity and model uncertainty, motivating a decisive shift toward multi-plan selection and modular, declarative frameworks that separate planning logic from execution. We further identify plan efficiency as a critically underexplored dimension, and highlight the promise of hybrid approaches that connect LLMs with symbolic planning models without modifying the underlying language model. Across computational chemistry, open-world tool use, and agentic search, recurring failure modes—such as incorrect tool arguments, result summarization errors, and an inability to recover from intermediate failures—underscore the necessity of environments that simulate realistic, recoverable error conditions. The emergence of white-box optimization for agentic workflows and the use of intermediate rewards to encourage faithful tool interactions further signal a maturation of the field, moving beyond surface-level accuracy toward structurally efficient and behaviorally aligned agent designs.

The significance of this survey lies in its synthesis of fragmented research threads into a coherent account of the central tensions shaping modern agent planning. By connecting foundational concerns—plan feasibility, efficiency, and error recovery—with concrete instantiations in domains such as chemistry automation and interactive retrieval, we provide a structured lens through which to assess progress and identify persistent gaps. Our emphasis on modularity, environment design, and the transition from black-box to white-box inference reflects a broader disciplinary shift toward building agents that are not only capable but also robust, adaptable, and computationally tractable. This integrated perspective is intended to serve both as a diagnostic tool for evaluating existing systems and as a conceptual foundation for designing next-generation agents that must operate reliably in complex, open-ended environments.

Looking forward, the path to more capable LLM-based agents demands a concerted effort across several fronts. Researchers must prioritize the development of environments that go beyond static benchmarks to model the dynamic, error-prone nature of real-world tool use, enabling systematic study of plan recovery and adaptation. The integration of symbolic planning with neural generation warrants deeper exploration, particularly in terms of formal guarantees and efficiency gains. Furthermore, the community should embrace white-box architectures and intermediate training signals that align agent behavior with faithful, deliberate reasoning rather than superficial answer correctness. We call for collaborative initiatives to establish shared evaluation protocols that capture not only task success but also plan quality, computational cost, and robustness to perturbation. Only through such multifaceted efforts can we realize the full potential of LLM-based agents as reliable partners in scientific discovery, personal assistance, and beyond.
