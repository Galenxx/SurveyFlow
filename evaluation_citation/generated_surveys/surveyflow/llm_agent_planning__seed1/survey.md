# 1 Abstract

The shift toward agentic search and tool-augmented reasoning has transformed how large language models interact with external information, moving from static retrieval to dynamic, multi-step planning and execution. This survey examines recent advances in agentic architectures, focusing on plan-level optimizations, input reformulation, declarative tool grounding, and evaluation paradigms for long-horizon reasoning. We review approaches that treat agentic workflows as optimizable directed acyclic graphs, compile tool interactions into primitive-based dataflow representations, and train faithfulness reward models to enforce declarative grounding constraints. Additionally, we analyze frameworks for orchestrating domain-specific scientific workflows and code-augmented reasoning in specialized contexts such as electronic health records. Our synthesis reveals that while single-turn tool use and schema compliance are approaching saturation, current agents still struggle with maintaining coherent long-horizon plans, adapting to dynamic environments, and recovering from errors under complex constraints. We highlight the emergence of multi-turn, interactive evaluation benchmarks and multi-agent coordination strategies as critical directions for assessing and improving agentic capabilities. These findings underscore a central tension in the field: the gap between near-solved narrow tool use and the open challenge of robust, adaptive planning in real-world settings.


# 2 Introduction

The emergence of large language models has fundamentally reshaped the landscape of artificial intelligence, extending their influence far beyond text generation into the realm of autonomous agency. These models now serve as the cognitive core of systems that can perceive, reason, plan, and act within complex environments. A defining characteristic of this shift is the transition from static, single-turn interactions to dynamic, multi-turn trajectories where agents must decompose high-level objectives, interact with external tools, and adapt their strategies based on environmental feedback. This agentic paradigm leverages the remarkable natural language understanding and reasoning capabilities of large language models to orchestrate workflows that span diverse domains, from scientific computing and healthcare to multi-agent game environments. As these systems grow in sophistication, the research community has increasingly focused on the mechanisms that enable reliable planning, the methods for grounding tool use in structured representations, and the benchmarks that rigorously assess these capabilities.

Despite the rapid progress, significant challenges remain in achieving robust and coherent planning and reasoning in large language model agents. When confronted with complex, real-world tasks that involve large decision spaces, underspecified requirements, and numerous interacting constraints, current agents frequently fail to maintain coherent long-horizon plans, adapt to evolving situations, or recover from intermediate errors. A notable gap persists between the near-saturated performance on narrow, single-turn tool-use benchmarks and the demands of dynamic, multi-step environments that require strategic coordination and adaptive reasoning. Furthermore, the grounding of tool invocations often remains an ad-hoc generative process rather than a structured, declarative execution plan, limiting the ability to optimize workflows at the plan level. In multi-agent settings, these difficulties are compounded by the need to reason about the behaviors and intentions of other actors, revealing substantial room for improvement in both teamwork and opponent modeling. These interconnected problems underscore the need for a systematic examination of the methods, tool-use paradigms, and evaluation frameworks that define the current frontier of agentic planning and reasoning.

This survey makes several contributions to the understanding of planning and reasoning in large language model agents. First, it provides a comprehensive synthesis of recent methodological advances, tracing the evolution from chain-of-thought prompting to reinforcement learning-based agentic search paradigms that treat multi-step reasoning as an interactive trajectory optimization problem. The survey examines how these methods enable agents to dynamically plan, issue queries, and reason over retrieved evidence, moving beyond static retrieval-augmented generation toward fully agentic workflows. By organizing these developments into a coherent taxonomy, the paper clarifies the relationships between trajectory-level reasoning, plan-level structural optimizations, and the declarative grounding of tool invocations through primitive-based dataflow representations. This integrated perspective reveals converging trends that have not been systematically connected in prior literature, offering researchers a consolidated reference point for navigating the rapidly expanding design space of agentic systems.

Second, this survey offers an in-depth analysis of tool-use mechanisms and evaluation benchmarks that are critical for assessing agent capabilities. It covers approaches that reformulate tool grounding by compiling agentic workflows into structured execution plans, as well as methods that train explicit reward models to evaluate the faithfulness of reasoning traces. The survey further investigates the design of dynamic, multi-turn evaluation environments that stress coordination, planning, and adaptive tool use, contrasting them with single-turn benchmarks that have approached saturation. By surveying domain-specific instantiations in computational chemistry and healthcare, alongside general-purpose multi-agent frameworks, the paper highlights both the breadth of current applications and the persistent challenges that define the path forward. This dual focus on methods and evaluation provides a balanced view of where the field stands and what obstacles must be overcome to achieve genuinely autonomous, trustworthy agents.

The aim of this survey is to provide researchers and practitioners with a structured overview of the current state of planning and reasoning in large language model agents, with a particular emphasis on methods, tool use, and evaluation benchmarks. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. We begin by examining the foundational reasoning paradigms that underpin agentic behavior, including chain-of-thought and its extensions into multi-step, trajectory-level planning. We then explore tool-use mechanisms, covering both architectural modifications and input reformulation strategies that enable structured, declarative grounding. Subsequently, we analyze evaluation benchmarks, distinguishing between single-turn and multi-turn settings, and discuss domain-specific applications that illustrate the practical deployment of these agents. Finally, we identify open challenges and promising research directions, outlining the path toward agents capable of robust, adaptive planning in complex, real-world environments.


# 3 Modular Agent Frameworks with Faithful Reasoning

## 3.1 Reinforcement Learning for Agentic Search and Multi-Turn Planning

Recent work has motivated a shift toward agentic search, a paradigm in which large language models are trained via reinforcement learning to dynamically plan, issue queries to a retriever, and reason over returned evidence as part of an interactive trajectory (Xu et al., 2025). This approach moves beyond static retrieval-augmented generation by treating the search process as a multi-turn planning problem that can be optimized through reinforcement learning.

## 3.2 Plan-Level Optimization in Agentic Directed Acyclic Graphs

Recent work has begun to explore plan-level optimizations that operate over the structure of agentic directed acyclic graphs (DAGs). Shen et al. (2025) introduce the problem of applying transformations such as node reordering and operator fusion across complex agentic DAGs, noting that existing multi-agent frameworks like LangGraph, AutoGen, and AgentScope provide abstractions for state management and message passing but do not perform these structural optimizations. Their system, Halo, is designed specifically to address this gap by optimizing the plan topology itself rather than only individual node execution.

The broader motivation for reasoning over such structured, multi-step plans comes from the shift toward agentic search paradigms. Xu et al. (2025) describe a setting in which large language models are trained via reinforcement learning to dynamically plan, issue queries to a retriever, and reason over returned evidence as part of an interactive trajectory. This trajectory-level reasoning naturally produces DAG-shaped plans whose efficiency and correctness can benefit from the kinds of plan-level optimizations that Shen et al. (2025) target.

## 3.3 Input Reformulation and Declarative Tool Grounding


A parallel line of work addresses tool grounding not by modifying the model architecture but by reformulating how inputs are structured and how tool invocations are represented. Shen et al. (2025) describe approaches that compile agentic workflows into primitive-based dataflow graphs, enabling primitive-level pipelining. This declarative representation shifts tool grounding from an ad-hoc generative process to a structured execution plan, where the relationships between tool calls are made explicit before execution. By representing workflows as graphs of primitives, these systems can optimize the scheduling and batching of tool interactions at a level below the query interface.

A related direction involves training explicit reward models to evaluate the faithfulness of reasoning traces that incorporate retrieved information. Xu et al. (2025) construct a large-scale dataset of search-augmented reasoning trajectories by subsampling 27,000 instances from NQ and HotpotQA and prompting Claude-3.7-Sonnet to label these trajectories for Information-Think faithfulness. This labeling process provides supervision for training a reward model that can distinguish between reasoning that faithfully grounds its conclusions in retrieved evidence and reasoning that superficially cites sources without genuine integration. Such faithfulness-oriented evaluation represents a form of declarative grounding constraint, where the system must demonstrate alignment between retrieved content and internal reasoning steps rather than merely producing syntactically valid tool calls.

# 4 LLM Planning in Scientific and Healthcare Domains

## 4.1 Computational Chemistry Workflow Planning with ChemGraph

Pham et al. (2025) introduced ChemGraph, an agentic framework designed to orchestrate computational chemistry workflows. The system provides tools implemented as Python functions that wrap widely used libraries—including RDKit, the Atomic Simulation Environment (ASE), and PubChemPy—into structured, agent-compatible interfaces. These tools span a broad range of functionalities, from basic cheminformatics operations such as molecular conversion to simulation backends (Pham et al., 2025). The authors also highlight the potential of combining ChemGraph with open-source models like Qwen2.5-14B to mitigate the computational cost associated with proprietary models, enabling cost-effective execution of complex scientific workflows. They position this initial version of ChemGraph as a groundwork for systems of intelligent scientific assistance (Pham et al., 2025).

## 4.2 Code-Empowered Tabular Reasoning in Electronic Health Records

The integration of code generation with large language models has emerged as a powerful paradigm for complex tabular reasoning in electronic health records. EHRAgent (2022) introduces an approach where code empowers LLMs for few-shot, multi-tabular reasoning within EHRs. The system's key design involves interactive coding with environment feedback, which enables the model to iteratively refine its reasoning. This design proved highly effective, as EHRAgent significantly outperformed all baselines across three datasets, achieving performance gains of 19.92%, 12.41%, and 29.60%, respectively (EHRAgent, 2022).

## 4.3 Skill Selection and Reasoning in LLM-based Personal Agents

Skill selection and reasoning in LLM-based personal agents are underpinned by the models' general capabilities in problem-solving. LLM-based autonomous agents have demonstrated remarkable abilities in reasoning, planning, and memorizing, with tool use emerging as a particularly notable capability (EHRAgent_ Code Empowers Large Language Models for Few-shot C, 2022). These foundational skills enable agents to guide complex workflows across specialized domains. Pham et al. (2025) note that LLMs have shown exceptional capabilities in natural language understanding, reasoning, and task execution, making them well-suited for directing intricate processes; indeed, several LLM-based agents have been developed to assist users with chemistry-related tasks.

The evaluation of such agents can proceed through different paradigms that probe their reasoning and skill execution. Pham et al. (2025) describe two distinct assessment approaches: single-turn evaluation, where the agentic framework receives a user query and generates a complete response without human intervention, and multi-turn evaluation. In the single-turn setting, if the LLM makes a mistake in a tool call, it can attempt to self-correct within that single interaction. These evaluation strategies provide a lens for examining how agents select and apply skills when reasoning through tasks, though the available evidence does not detail specific skill-selection mechanisms.

# 5 LLM Agent Planning in Dynamic Multi-Agent Settings

## 5.1 Environment Generation for Long-Horizon Computer Use Agents

The design of environments for long-horizon computer use agents has moved beyond static, single-turn benchmarks toward dynamic, multi-step settings that require agents to decompose complex goals, coordinate subtasks, and adapt to evolving situations (Xi et al., 2026). In such open-world contexts, tool use—encompassing querying services, operating software, and executing workflows—serves as the primary interface through which agents act (Xi et al., 2026). Xi et al. (2026) further note that recent work has explored self-correction and multi-agent role specialization as strategies for managing extended workflows. Complementing this direction, LLMArena (2023) introduced a dynamic evaluation benchmark specifically designed for multi-agent interaction, covering seven different types of game environments where multiple agents operate in a shared space. Together, these efforts illustrate a shift toward generating environments that stress the coordination, planning, and adaptive tool-use capabilities essential for long-horizon computer use agents.

## 5.2 Strategic Planning and Opponent Modeling in Multi-Agent Interactions

Strategic planning in multi-agent settings remains a significant challenge for current agents. Xi et al. (2026) observe that, despite the strong reasoning capabilities exhibited by large language models, agents frequently fail to maintain coherent long-horizon plans, adapt to evolving situations, or recover from intermediate errors when faced with large decision spaces, underspecified requirements, and many interacting constraints. This difficulty is compounded in dynamic multi-agent environments, where agents must also reason about the behaviors and intentions of other actors. Empirical assessments using the LLMARENA framework, which evaluates large language models under varied scenarios and opponent configurations, indicate that there is substantial potential for improvement in both teamwork and opponent modeling skills (LLMArena, 2023).

## 5.3 Categorization of Planning Capabilities in LLM Agents

Recent progress in tool use has been rapid, with frontier LLMs reaching near-saturated performance on single-turn function calling and schema compliance benchmarks (Xi et al., 2026). Despite these gains, a significant gap persists between LLM-based agents and humans in planning and reasoning for real-world tasks. When confronted with complex objectives involving large decision spaces, underspecified requirements, and many interacting constraints, current agents frequently fail to maintain coherent long-horizon plans, adapt to evolving situations, or recover from intermediate errors (Xi et al., 2026).

A foundational advance in amplifying the reasoning and planning abilities of LLM-based agents was the introduction of chain-of-thought (CoT) technology by Wei et al. (2022), which sparked a surge of subsequent research (LLMArena, 2023). This development has contributed to the broader adoption of LLMs as cognitive entities and controllers for agents (LLMArena, 2023). However, the evidence indicates that planning capabilities in LLM agents can be understood along a spectrum: while narrow, single-turn tool use is approaching a solved problem, the capacity for robust, adaptive planning in complex, dynamic environments remains an open challenge (Xi et al., 2026).

# 6 Future Directions

The current landscape of agentic planning and reasoning reveals a pronounced tension between narrow proficiency and robust, real-world capability. While frontier models have achieved near-saturated performance on single-turn tool use and schema compliance, they consistently fail when confronted with complex, long-horizon tasks that demand adaptive planning, dynamic coordination, and graceful error recovery. Plan-level structural optimizations over agentic workflows remain nascent, with existing multi-agent frameworks providing state management but lacking the ability to perform topological transformations such as node reordering or operator fusion. Furthermore, the grounding of tool invocations is often treated as an ad-hoc generative process rather than a structured, declarative execution plan, limiting the potential for fine-grained pipelining and optimization. In multi-agent settings, these limitations are amplified by a marked inability to model the intentions of other actors and maintain coherent strategies over extended interactions. The integration of faithfulness evaluation with trajectory-level reinforcement learning remains unexplored, leaving a critical gap between the generation of reasoning traces and the verification of their evidential grounding.

Future research should prioritize the unification of plan-level structural optimization with reinforcement learning-driven agentic search. By developing differentiable representations of agentic directed acyclic graphs, systems could jointly optimize both the actions taken and the topology of their execution during training, yielding more efficient and reliable multi-step trajectories. This would involve designing policy architectures that reason over graph structures, enabling agents to learn when and how to reorder, fuse, or parallelize tool calls as part of an end-to-end optimization process. A second critical direction involves extending declarative grounding and faithfulness reward models to multi-agent and long-horizon contexts. Current faithfulness evaluation focuses narrowly on single-agent reasoning traces, but collaborative and competitive settings demand that agents verify their interpretations of others’ behaviors against observable evidence. Developing multi-agent faithfulness metrics and training paradigms would directly address observed weaknesses in teamwork and opponent modeling. Additionally, constructing dynamic, generative evaluation environments that stress adaptive replanning, coordination under uncertainty, and error recovery is essential. These environments must move beyond static datasets to simulate evolving, open-world scenarios that mirror real-world computer use, scientific workflow orchestration, and healthcare reasoning.

Advancing these directions would substantially narrow the gap between current agentic systems and human-level strategic reasoning. Unifying plan optimization with policy learning promises agents that not only solve complex tasks but do so with greater computational efficiency and robustness, enabling practical deployment in resource-constrained or latency-sensitive settings. Extending faithfulness and grounding to multi-agent contexts would enhance the reliability and interpretability of collaborative agent teams, with direct implications for scientific assistance, clinical decision support, and autonomous system coordination. Together, these advances would transform agentic systems from brittle, single-turn tools into adaptive, trustworthy partners capable of sustained, coherent action in the open world, thereby realizing the full potential of language model agents in high-stakes, real-world applications.


# 7 Conclusion

This survey has examined the recent evolution of agentic architectures for large language models, tracing a trajectory from static retrieval-augmented generation toward dynamic, multi-turn planning and tool-augmented reasoning. A central finding is the emergence of agentic search as a distinct paradigm in which reinforcement learning is employed to optimize interactive trajectories that interleave planning, querying, and evidence integration. This trajectory-level perspective naturally gives rise to directed acyclic graph structures whose efficiency can be improved through plan-level transformations such as node reordering and operator fusion. In parallel, input reformulation strategies have advanced tool grounding by compiling agentic workflows into primitive-based dataflow representations, enabling declarative execution plans and faithfulness-oriented evaluation. The survey further documents the expansion of these capabilities into specialized domains, including computational chemistry workflow orchestration and code-empowered tabular reasoning in electronic health records, where structured interfaces and interactive feedback loops yield substantial performance improvements. Across these developments, a consistent tension persists: while single-turn function calling and schema compliance have approached saturation for frontier models, long-horizon planning, multi-agent coordination, and robust error recovery remain open challenges that expose fundamental gaps between current agent capabilities and the demands of real-world, dynamic environments.

The significance of this survey resides in its structured synthesis of the architectural and algorithmic shifts that are redefining how language model agents interact with external tools, search engines, and structured knowledge. By organizing recent advances into a coherent taxonomy that spans trajectory-level reasoning, plan-level structural optimization, and declarative grounding, the survey provides a conceptual framework for understanding agentic reasoning as a multi-turn planning problem rather than an isolated generation task. The juxtaposition of these methodological threads reveals converging trends that have not been systematically connected in prior literature, including the complementary roles of reinforcement learning for search policy optimization and graph-level transformations for execution efficiency. This integrated perspective is particularly timely given the proliferation of multi-agent frameworks and the accelerating deployment of language model agents in scientific, clinical, and open-world settings where reliability, faithfulness, and adaptive planning are paramount. The survey thus offers researchers and practitioners a consolidated reference point for navigating the rapidly expanding design space of agentic systems and for identifying the critical bottlenecks that define the current frontier.

Looking forward, closing the gap between narrow tool-use proficiency and robust, long-horizon reasoning demands concerted effort on several fronts. The field must prioritize the development of planning architectures capable of maintaining coherence over extended trajectories, recovering gracefully from intermediate failures, and reasoning about the intentions of other agents in collaborative or competitive settings. Unifying plan-level structural optimization with reinforcement-learning-based trajectory learning represents a promising direction for achieving both computational efficiency and adaptive behavior. Furthermore, as agentic systems are increasingly deployed in high-stakes domains such as healthcare and scientific research, the establishment of rigorous, multi-turn evaluation protocols that assess faithfulness, safety, and strategic reasoning—rather than single-turn accuracy alone—becomes an urgent priority. Progress along these dimensions will determine whether the current wave of agentic capabilities translates into genuinely autonomous systems that can be trusted to act competently, coherently, and responsibly in the open world.
