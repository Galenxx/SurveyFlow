# 1 Abstract

As large language models are increasingly deployed in open-world environments, they are expected to decompose complex goals into multi-step plans, coordinate diverse sub-tasks, and adapt strategies through tool use, yet a significant gap persists between these agents and human-level performance in real-world planning and reasoning. This survey systematically examines the current landscape of planning and tool-use capabilities in LLM-based agents, with a focus on multi-agent coordination, strategic reasoning, and agentic workflow optimization. We review recent advances across several dimensions, including dynamic evaluation benchmarks for multi-agent strategic planning, code-empowered planning paradigms that translate complex tasks into executable programs with iterative refinement, and declarative agentic architectures that structure planning through explicit multi-agent control flows. Our analysis reveals persistent deficiencies in long-horizon plan coherence, adaptation to evolving situations, and strategic reasoning in dynamic multi-agent settings, while also highlighting uneven and unreliable numerical reasoning capabilities even in formally defined interactive scenarios. Promising directions include reinforcement learning-driven agentic search that treats multi-turn retrieval as an optimizable planning problem, and plan-level optimizations over directed acyclic graphs that enable node reordering and operator fusion to improve execution efficiency. These findings collectively underscore the critical challenges that must be addressed to enable trustworthy and robust autonomous agents capable of reliable planning and reasoning in consequential real-world applications.


# 2 Introduction

The rapid advancement of large language models has fundamentally transformed the landscape of artificial intelligence, evolving systems that once merely generated text into autonomous agents capable of interacting with complex, open-world environments. These agents are increasingly expected to decompose intricate goals into multi-step plans, coordinate diverse sub-tasks, and adapt their strategies as circumstances change, with tool use—the ability to query external services, operate software, and execute computational workflows—serving as the primary interface through which they perceive and act upon the world. This evolution has been propelled by scaling laws that have endowed models with remarkable reasoning abilities, enabling them to tackle problems across domains as diverse as computational chemistry, healthcare, and strategic game-playing. As the ambition for these systems grows, so too does the need for robust planning and reasoning mechanisms that can sustain coherent, goal-directed behavior across extended time horizons and in the face of dynamic, uncertain conditions.

Despite these advances, a significant and persistent gap remains between the planning and reasoning capabilities of large language model agents and human-level performance in real-world tasks. When confronted with complex objectives involving vast decision spaces, underspecified requirements, and numerous interacting constraints, current agents frequently fail to maintain coherent long-horizon plans, adapt to evolving situations, or recover gracefully from intermediate errors. This limitation is particularly pronounced in strategic multi-agent environments, where models demonstrate an inability to accurately assess dynamic states and formulate effective strategies, often causing outcomes to favor opponents or leading to self-defeating behavior. Even in formally defined interactive scenarios with known optimal strategies, the reasoning capabilities of current models can be uneven and unreliable, exhibiting substantial performance variance across different systems. Furthermore, while multi-agent frameworks provide useful abstractions for state management and message passing, they lack the capability to perform plan-level optimizations over complex agentic workflows, missing critical opportunities to improve execution efficiency. Such brittleness underscores the fundamental challenges that must be addressed before autonomous agents can be entrusted with consequential, long-horizon planning in everyday life.

This survey makes several key contributions to the understanding and advancement of planning and reasoning in large language model agents. First, it provides a comprehensive taxonomy of planning paradigms that have emerged in recent research, ranging from code-empowered planning—where complex tasks are formulated as executable programs that undergo iterative refinement between the agent and a code executor—to declarative agentic layers that decompose tasks through structured multi-agent architectures with explicit control flows. The survey examines how these paradigms have been instantiated in domain-specific frameworks, such as computational chemistry systems where planner, executor, and aggregator agents collaborate through loop controllers, and healthcare applications where clinical reasoning is translated into action sequences of code operations. By systematically categorizing these approaches, the survey reveals both the common architectural patterns and the domain-specific adaptations that characterize the current state of the art, while also highlighting persistent failure modes including errors in tool arguments and issues during result summarization.

Second, this work offers a detailed analysis of tool-use mechanisms and evaluation methodologies that have been developed to assess agent capabilities. The survey traces the evolution from early single-turn function calling benchmarks to more sophisticated evaluation scenarios that test multi-agent coordination, strategic decision-making, and numerical reasoning in dynamic environments. It examines how agentic search—a paradigm in which models are trained via reinforcement learning to dynamically plan queries and reason over retrieved evidence—represents a fundamental shift toward treating the search process itself as an optimizable multi-turn planning problem. Furthermore, the survey investigates how agentic workflows structured as directed acyclic graphs enable plan-level optimizations, including node reordering and operator fusion, that improve execution efficiency while preserving logical semantics. Through this analysis, the survey identifies critical gaps in current evaluation practices, including the fragmentation of benchmarks and the lack of standardized methodologies for assessing planning fidelity and tool-use accuracy across diverse domains.

The aim of this survey is to provide researchers and practitioners with a structured overview of the methods, tool-use frameworks, and evaluation benchmarks that define the current landscape of planning and reasoning in large language model agents. The paper is organized as follows: it begins by establishing foundational concepts in agent planning and reasoning, then proceeds to examine major planning paradigms including code-empowered and declarative approaches, followed by an in-depth analysis of tool-use mechanisms and their integration into agentic workflows. Subsequent sections review evaluation benchmarks across single-agent and multi-agent settings, discuss the persistent gaps between model and human performance, and conclude by identifying open challenges and promising directions for future research that can help bridge the gap toward truly capable autonomous agents.


# 3 Benchmarks for LLM Agent Planning and Reasoning

## 3.1 Real-World Computer Use and Multi-Agent Strategic Planning Benchmarks

In open-world settings, agents are expected to decompose complex goals into multi-step plans, coordinate diverse sub-tasks, and adapt their strategies as situations evolve, with tool use—querying services, operating software, and executing workflows—serving as the primary interface through which agents act on the world (Xi et al., 2026). Recent work has extended these capabilities toward computer-using agents and multi-agent role specialization for long-horizon workflows (Xi et al., 2026). Early tool-use benchmarks focused on single-turn function calling, but the field has since progressed to more complex evaluation scenarios (Xi et al., 2026).

For multi-agent strategic planning, LLMARENA (2023) was proposed as a dynamic evaluation benchmark specifically designed for multi-agent interaction, covering seven different types of dynamic, multi-agent game environments. This benchmark assesses how multiple agents interact within a shared environment, providing a structured setting to evaluate coordination and strategic decision-making capabilities.

## 3.2 Categorization and Evaluation of LLM Agent Planning Capabilities

Despite the strong reasoning abilities that large language models (LLMs) have acquired through scaling laws, a significant gap persists between LLM-based agents and humans in planning and reasoning for real-world tasks (Xi et al., 2026). When agents are confronted with complex objectives that involve large decision spaces, underspecified requirements, and many interacting constraints, they frequently fail to maintain coherent long-horizon plans, adapt to evolving situations, or recover from intermediate errors (Xi et al., 2026). This limitation is further evidenced in strategic domains: in dynamic multi-agent environments such as chess, LLMs demonstrate an inability to accurately assess the current state of the board and to formulate effective strategies, which causes the game to progress in a direction that favors the opponent (LLMArena, 2023). The observed gap between LLM strategic planning and human performance was examined through manual play across multiple LLMs, consistently revealing deficiencies in high-level strategy formulation (LLMArena, 2023).

## 3.3 Planning and Reasoning in Personal LLM Agents

Planning and reasoning remain central challenges for personal LLM agents, particularly when they are deployed in open-ended, real-world settings. Xi et al. When faced with complex objectives that involve large decision spaces, underspecified requirements, and many interacting constraints, current agents frequently fail to maintain coherent long-horizon plans, adapt to evolving situations, or recover from intermediate errors. These limitations highlight the difficulty of sustaining goal-directed behavior beyond narrowly scoped or highly structured environments.

The reasoning demands placed on personal agents also extend to dynamic, multi-agent contexts where numerical and strategic thinking is required. The resulting scores varied substantially across models, with some achieving near-optimal performance while others produced markedly lower or even negative scores, indicating that even in formally defined interactive scenarios, the reasoning capabilities of current LLMs can be uneven and unreliable. Such brittleness in strategic and numerical reasoning further underscores the gap that must be closed before personal LLM agents can be trusted with consequential, long-horizon planning in everyday life.

# 4 Optimizing Tool Use in Agentic Workflows

## 4.1 Reinforcement Learning for Agentic Search and Multi-Turn Planning

Recent work has motivated a shift toward agentic search, a paradigm in which large language models are trained via reinforcement learning to dynamically plan, issue queries to a retriever, and reason over returned evidence as part of an interactive trajectory (Xu et al., 2025). This approach moves beyond static retrieval-augmented generation by treating the search process itself as a multi-turn planning problem that can be optimized through RL. In parallel, the computational orchestration of such agentic workflows has been formalized as a batch scheduling problem over directed acyclic graphs of LLM calls, where an optimal policy is sought to minimize execution cost across available workers (Shen et al., 2025).

## 4.2 Plan-Level Optimization in Agentic Directed Acyclic Graphs

Agentic search represents a paradigm in which large language models are trained via reinforcement learning to dynamically plan, issue queries to a retriever, and reason over returned evidence as part of an interactive trajectory (Xu et al., 2025). When these agentic workflows are structured as directed acyclic graphs (DAGs), there is an opportunity to perform plan-level optimizations across the entire graph structure. Shen et al. (2025) identify that such optimizations can include node reordering or operator fusion, techniques that restructure the execution plan to improve efficiency without altering the logical semantics of the workflow. Existing multi-agent frameworks, including LangGraph, AutoGen, and AgentScope, provide abstractions for state management and message passing that simplify the logical construction of agentic applications, but they do not perform these kinds of plan-level optimizations across complex agentic DAGs (Shen et al., 2025).

# 5 LLM-driven Autonomous Agent Frameworks

## 5.1 LLM Agents for Multi-Step Computational Chemistry Workflows


Pham et al. (2025) introduced ChemGraph, an LLM-powered agentic framework that automates molecular simulation workflows through structured tool calling and reasoning. The system was evaluated across a diverse set of 13 experiments, ranging from simple molecule-name-to-SMILES conversions to complex thermodynamic property calculations, and demonstrated that state-of-the-art LLMs can achieve high task completion accuracy (Pham et al., 2025). A key finding was that single-agent systems perform reliably on tasks involving a small number of steps, while the broader vision for such frameworks involves agents that can not only automate simulations but also adapt, reason, and collaborate, paving the way for more efficient and accessible scientific discoveries (Pham et al., 2025). This work aligns with the broader trend of augmenting LLMs with external tools, where models have rapidly evolved from text generators into core computational engines of autonomous agents with advanced planning and tool-use capabilities (EHRAgent, 2022).

## 5.2 Code-Empowered Planning and Reasoning in EHRAgent

A key strategy for enabling complex reasoning over electronic health records is to formulate the problem-solving process as an executable code plan. In EHRAgent, planning with a code interface translates a clinical task into an action sequence of code operations, which is then carried out by a code executor (EHRAgent_ Code Empowers Large Language Models for Few-shot C, 2022). This approach is further strengthened by an interactive coding mechanism: the LLM agent and the code executor engage in iterative refinement, where the agent examines environmental feedback in depth to optimize both plan generation and code execution (EHRAgent_ Code Empowers Large Language Models for Few-shot C, 2022).

A similar planning paradigm appears in the ChemGraph framework for computational chemistry, where a Planner Agent decomposes user requests into subtasks and generates a series of prompts that are sequentially forwarded to an Executor agent through a Loop Controller (Pham et al., 2025). Although ChemGraph operates in a different domain, its experience with agent errors offers relevant cautionary insights for code-empowered EHR reasoning. Pham et al. (2025) found that common failure modes across models included errors in tool arguments and issues during result summarization, such as missing values or confusion between float and complex number formats in the final report. These findings underscore the importance of robust code execution and careful output aggregation when relying on LLM agents to plan and reason over structured data.

## 5.3 Declarative Agentic Layers for Grounded Planning and Tool Use

Declarative agentic layers structure grounded planning and tool use by decomposing complex tasks into manageable subtasks and orchestrating tool invocations through explicit control flows. In the ChemGraph framework, Pham et al. (2025) introduce a three-agent architecture consisting of a Planner Agent, an Executor Agent, and an Aggregator Agent. The Planner decomposes a user request into subtasks and generates a sequence of prompts, which are then passed through a Loop Controller that sequentially forwards each prompt to an Executor. Each Executor is equipped with a predefined set of tools and, based on the user prompt, decides which tool to invoke. After each tool call completes, the LLM receives the result and determines whether additional tool calls are needed, with the message subsequently following one of two possible paths (Pham et al., 2025).

This approach reflects a broader paradigm in which LLM agents are augmented with planning capabilities to break down large, hard tasks into smaller, simpler steps for navigating complex real-world scenarios. By integrating with external tools, these agents access external APIs to obtain knowledge beyond their training data (EHRAgent, 2022). Such declarative layers thus provide a structured mechanism for grounding agent behavior in concrete tool interactions while maintaining explicit control over the planning and execution lifecycle.

# 6 Future Directions

The current landscape of LLM-based agents reveals persistent and fundamental limitations in planning and reasoning, particularly when these systems are deployed in open-ended, real-world environments. Despite advances in scaling laws and tool-use capabilities, agents consistently fail to maintain coherent long-horizon plans, adapt to dynamically evolving situations, or recover gracefully from intermediate errors when faced with large decision spaces and underspecified constraints. This brittleness is starkly evident in strategic multi-agent settings, where models exhibit an inability to accurately assess state and formulate effective strategies, leading to suboptimal or self-defeating behavior. Furthermore, numerical and strategic reasoning remains uneven and unreliable across different models, even in formally defined interactive scenarios. On the infrastructure side, while multi-agent frameworks provide useful abstractions for state management and message passing, they lack the capability to perform plan-level optimizations over complex agentic workflows, missing opportunities to restructure execution plans for improved efficiency without altering logical semantics.

Future research should prioritize the development of more robust planning architectures that can sustain goal-directed behavior over extended horizons. One promising direction involves integrating explicit world models and state-tracking mechanisms that allow agents to maintain accurate representations of their environment, enabling them to detect plan deviations early and trigger adaptive replanning. This could be coupled with hierarchical planning approaches that decompose long-horizon objectives into manageable sub-goals with verifiable intermediate milestones, allowing agents to recover locally from failures without abandoning the overall task. Another critical avenue is the advancement of plan-level optimization techniques for agentic workflows. By treating multi-agent execution graphs as optimization targets, future systems could automatically reorder operations, fuse redundant tool calls, and parallelize independent subtasks to minimize latency and computational cost. This would require developing formal cost models for LLM invocations and tool executions, along with algorithms that can reason about the trade-offs between execution speed, result quality, and resource consumption across complex directed acyclic graph structures.

Advancing these research directions would fundamentally transform the reliability and practicality of personal LLM agents. Agents capable of robust long-horizon planning and adaptive error recovery could be entrusted with consequential real-world tasks, from managing personal finances and coordinating healthcare logistics to automating complex scientific workflows. The ability to perform automated plan-level optimizations would dramatically reduce the computational overhead of deploying multi-agent systems, making sophisticated agentic applications economically viable at scale. Together, these advances would help close the gap between current brittle systems and the vision of truly capable autonomous agents that can reason, plan, and act reliably in the open world.


# 7 Conclusion

In this survey, we have systematically examined the evolving landscape of planning and reasoning in large language model agents, with particular emphasis on tool use, multi-agent coordination, and agentic workflow optimization. Our analysis reveals that despite the remarkable reasoning capabilities acquired through scaling laws, a persistent and substantial gap remains between current LLM-based agents and human-level performance in real-world planning tasks. This gap manifests most acutely in long-horizon plan coherence, where agents frequently fail to maintain goal-directed behavior when confronted with large decision spaces and underspecified constraints. The brittleness extends to dynamic multi-agent environments, where strategic reasoning proves uneven and unreliable, often causing outcomes to favor opponents even in formally defined scenarios with known optimal strategies. Through detailed examination of benchmarks such as LLMARENA and frameworks including ChemGraph and EHRAgent, we have demonstrated that current agents achieve reliable performance only in narrowly scoped tasks with limited complexity, while their capabilities degrade sharply under the demands of open-ended, consequential planning. The emergence of agentic search paradigms, declarative agentic layers, and plan-level optimizations over directed acyclic graphs represents meaningful progress, yet the absence of robust error recovery mechanisms and standardized evaluation methodologies underscores the immaturity of the field.

The significance of this survey lies in its comprehensive synthesis of recent advances and persistent limitations across multiple dimensions of agentic planning and reasoning. By connecting insights from tool-use benchmarks, multi-agent strategic evaluation, computational chemistry workflows, and code-empowered reasoning in healthcare, we provide a unified perspective on the critical challenges that must be addressed before autonomous agents can be entrusted with consequential, long-horizon tasks in everyday life. Our analysis reveals that the current fragmentation of evaluation paradigms and the lack of standardized methodologies for assessing planning fidelity, tool-use accuracy, and multi-step reasoning coherence hinder systematic progress toward robust autonomous agents. Furthermore, we highlight how the integration of reinforcement learning for agentic search and the formalization of agentic workflows as optimizable graph structures point toward promising directions, while also exposing the gap between existing multi-agent frameworks and the plan-level optimizations needed for efficient, scalable deployment. This survey thus serves as both a critical assessment of the state of the art and a structured roadmap for researchers seeking to bridge the divide between narrow task success and genuine autonomous competence.

Moving forward, the research community must prioritize the development of more rigorous and ecologically valid benchmarks that capture the full complexity of real-world planning, including underspecified goals, evolving constraints, and the imperative for graceful error recovery. Equally important is the advancement of training paradigms that can endow agents with the strategic depth and adaptability currently lacking, particularly through reinforcement learning over interactive search trajectories and automated plan-level optimizations over agentic directed acyclic graphs. We call for greater interdisciplinary collaboration to integrate insights from cognitive science, operations research, and software engineering into the design of next-generation personal agents. Only through concerted effort on these fronts can we realize the vision of LLM-based agents that plan, reason, and act with the reliability, efficiency, and sophistication required for meaningful deployment in open-world environments.
