# 1 Abstract

Recent advances in neural audio codecs have driven substantial progress in low-bitrate speech compression, yet challenges remain in balancing acoustic fidelity, semantic coherence, and generative flexibility. This survey provides a structured overview of emerging strategies in neural speech codec design, with a focus on hierarchical encoding, unified tokenization, and decoding paradigms that bridge semantic and acoustic representations. We examine multi-scale latent projections and stabilized quantization techniques that improve training stability and compression efficiency, alongside unified frameworks that integrate semantic, acoustic, and contextual signals into shared representation spaces. The review further investigates disentangled tokenization approaches that separate global linguistic content from local acoustic detail, as well as latent diffusion models and conditioning mechanisms that enhance the robustness and expressiveness of token-based waveform generation. Key findings indicate that hierarchical and semantically informed codec architectures consistently outperform purely acoustic or deterministic baselines, enabling improved intelligibility, speaker consistency, and perceptual quality at low bitrates. By synthesizing these developments, this survey highlights the convergence toward domain-adaptive, multi-granular codecs that support diverse downstream speech generation and understanding tasks.


# 2 Introduction

The field of speech processing has undergone a profound transformation with the advent of deep learning, reshaping how audio signals are compressed, represented, and synthesized. At the heart of this evolution lie neural audio codecs, which leverage end-to-end neural network training to convert continuous speech waveforms into compact sequences of discrete tokens. Unlike conventional codecs grounded in psychoacoustic principles and hand-engineered signal transformations, neural approaches learn optimal mappings directly from data, discovering latent representations that balance compression rate with reconstruction fidelity. These discrete representations have rapidly become indispensable in modern speech generation pipelines, serving as the crucial interface between raw acoustic signals and powerful generative models such as large language models and diffusion-based decoders. The flexibility inherent in learned codecs has opened new frontiers in low-bitrate transmission, zero-shot voice cloning, and expressive speech synthesis, while simultaneously raising fundamental questions about what information these discrete tokens should encode and how they should be structured to best serve diverse downstream tasks.

Despite remarkable progress, the design of neural audio codecs confronts several persistent and interconnected challenges. A fundamental tension pervades the field between semantic and acoustic representations: tokens derived from self-supervised learning models excel at capturing linguistic content and enabling intelligible generation, yet they often discard speaker identity, prosody, and paralinguistic nuance. Conversely, acoustic codec tokens preserve high-fidelity waveform detail but may lack the structured semantic coherence required for robust generative modeling and long-form consistency. This divide has motivated efforts toward unified tokenization frameworks that jointly model multimodal information streams, but achieving fine-grained alignment across semantic, acoustic, and contextual signals remains technically demanding. Further complications arise from the predominantly deterministic nature of conventional codec decoders, which limits generative diversity and naturalness in synthesized speech. Additional open problems include the preservation of emotional and prosodic information within compact discrete tokens, the robustness of decoding under noisy or diverse acoustic conditions, and the fundamental trade-offs between token compactness, compression efficiency, and reconstruction quality. These challenges underscore the necessity for a comprehensive examination of how architectural decisions, quantization strategies, and conditioning mechanisms collectively shape codec performance.

This survey provides a systematic and in-depth examination of neural audio codecs and discrete speech representations, offering a structured synthesis of recent architectural innovations, tokenization paradigms, and generative applications. The first major contribution is a detailed taxonomy of codec architectures, encompassing hierarchical encoding strategies that employ dimensionality reduction and stabilized quantization techniques to improve training stability and compression efficiency. We analyze unified frameworks that fuse semantic, acoustic, and contextual signals within shared latent spaces, examining how design choices such as vector quantization distance metrics, codebook learning strategies, and multi-scale representation schemes impact reconstruction quality. By organizing these architectural variants into a coherent framework, the survey clarifies the trade-offs and synergies among competing approaches, providing researchers with a structured roadmap for navigating the increasingly complex design space of modern neural codecs. Special attention is devoted to disentangled tokenization approaches that separate global linguistic content from local acoustic detail, a strategy that has proven effective for improving speaker consistency and semantic coherence in low-bitrate scenarios.

The second contribution is a comprehensive analysis of tokenization methodologies and their implications for downstream generative tasks. We systematically examine the spectrum from purely semantic tokens to purely acoustic tokens, highlighting hybrid and hierarchical schemes that bridge this divide. The survey further investigates how discrete tokens interface with generative models, including latent diffusion decoders that introduce beneficial stochasticity into waveform generation, language model-based synthesis frameworks that leverage hierarchical token structures for long-term consistency, and conditioning strategies that inject semantic guidance into acoustic reconstruction. Additionally, we address emerging application domains such as speech emotion recognition and speech enhancement, where discrete representations are being adapted to preserve paralinguistic information or to operate within continuous embedding spaces prior to quantization. By bridging the gap between codec design and generative modeling, this survey illuminates how representational choices cascade through to affect the fidelity, diversity, and controllability of synthesized speech, while also identifying the conditions under which continuous embeddings may offer advantages over discrete tokens for specific downstream tasks.

The aim of this survey is to provide a unified reference that consolidates recent progress, identifies open challenges, and outlines promising directions for future research in neural audio codecs and discrete speech representations. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. We begin by establishing foundational concepts in neural audio compression and discrete tokenization, providing the necessary technical background for understanding subsequent architectural discussions. We then present a detailed review of architectural innovations, structured around hierarchical encoding strategies, unified tokenization frameworks, and disentangled representation learning. Subsequent sections examine generative applications, including latent diffusion decoding, language model-based synthesis, and conditioning mechanisms, followed by a dedicated discussion of downstream tasks such as emotion recognition and speech enhancement. The survey concludes by synthesizing key insights, highlighting unresolved issues, and proposing concrete research trajectories that can advance the field toward more versatile, robust, and expressive speech codecs capable of serving both generative and discriminative objectives.


# 3 Neural Audio Codecs with Residual Vector Quantization

## 3.1 Multi-Scale Hierarchical Encoding for Low-Bitrate Compression

Recent work on neural audio codecs has explored multi-scale hierarchical encoding to improve compression at low bitrates. Li et al. (2024) describe a design in which a linear projection maps the encoder’s original latent space, which is 1024‑dimensional, into a lower‑dimensional quantization latent space of 8 or 32 dimensions. This projection is combined with a change in the vector quantization lookup distance from Euclidean distance to cosine similarity, a modification intended to increase training stability. In the same architecture, an explicit mean‑squared‑error codebook loss is used to learn the codebook, rather than relying on an exponential moving average update scheme.

In a complementary direction, FuseCodec (2024) integrates semantic and contextual signals directly into the encoder’s latent space. The resulting unified representation is reported to improve both intelligibility and perceptual quality, and the approach outperforms several compression‑focused models, including DAC, BigCodec, StableCodec, WavTokenizer, and X‑Codec2. The FuseCodec‑Distill variant further emphasizes naturalness and speaker consistency, excelling in perceptual quality. Together, these studies illustrate how hierarchical encoding strategies—whether through dimensionality reduction and stabilized quantization or through semantic‑contextual fusion—can enhance low‑bitrate neural compression.

## 3.2 Unified Cross-Domain Audio Tokenization with Domain-Adaptive Codebooks

Recent work has pursued unified tokenization frameworks that bridge the gap between semantic and acoustic representations. Li et al. (2024) note that discrete speech tokens have historically emerged from two distinct paradigms: semantic tokens derived from self‑supervised learning, and codec tokens produced by neural audio codec systems. While semantic tokens support intelligible generation, they lack the acoustic fidelity that codec tokens can provide. To address this divide, FuseCodec (FuseCodec, 2024) introduces a unified speech tokenization framework that jointly integrates acoustic, semantic, and contextual signals through multimodal representation fusion and supervision. The approach enables fine‑grained alignment across these information streams and achieves state‑of‑the‑art performance on speech reconstruction. By learning a shared representation space that adapts to both semantic content and acoustic detail, such unified codec designs move toward domain‑adaptive codebooks capable of serving diverse downstream speech generation tasks.

## 3.3 Disentangled Global and Local Tokens from Self-Supervised Representations

**3.3 Disentangled Global and Local Tokens from Self-Supervised Representations**

The distinction between semantic and acoustic information in speech has motivated efforts to derive disentangled token representations from self-supervised learning. Li et al. (2024) characterize this as two parallel lines of work: self-supervised learning produces semantic tokens, while neural audio codec systems yield codec tokens. Semantic tokens were initially adopted for speech language model-based generation and produce intelligible speech, but they fail to capture consistent acoustic traits such as speaker identity. This limitation highlights the need for representations that separate global semantic content from local acoustic detail.

Building on prior speech tokenization work that demonstrated the effectiveness of similarity matching for representation learning, FuseCodec (2024) introduces a global semantic-contextual supervision strategy as an alternative to latent fusion. The approach is motivated by the observation that existing methods typically constrain representations in ways that may not adequately preserve semantic coherence. By applying global supervision, the model aims to enforce semantic consistency across the learned tokens, complementing the local acoustic fidelity already captured by neural codec architectures.

# 4 Hierarchical Diffusion Decoding for Speech Tokenization

## 4.1 Latent Diffusion Models for Acoustic Token Decoding


Standard neural speech codecs typically rely on deterministic, GAN-trained decoders that lack external stochasticity in the waveform generation process. DiffSoundStream (n.d.) introduces an alternative by incorporating a latent diffusion model into the decoding stage, conditioned on both semantic and coarse acoustic tokens. This design enhances the generative capability of the decoder and is reported to overcome quality limitations inherent to purely deterministic approaches. The method also encourages the acoustic tokens to capture complementary information from the semantic tokens, enabling efficient reconstruction with fewer tokens.

## 4.2 Coarse-to-Fine and Hierarchical Discrete Token Generation

A common strategy in token-based speech generation is to adopt a hierarchical or coarse-to-fine tokenization scheme that separates linguistic content from acoustic detail. Most contemporary solutions employ a hybrid approach that combines semantic tokens, typically derived from a self-supervised learning (SSL) model, with acoustic tokens produced by a neural speech codec (DiffSoundStream, n.d.). In these pipelines, the semantic tokens serve as an intermediate modeling target that guides the generation of the acoustic tokens, rather than directly participating in waveform reconstruction.

Li et al. (2024) further formalize this hierarchical structure by proposing a codec network built on vector-quantized auto-encoders with adversarial training (VQ-GAN). Their approach first extracts continuous frame-level speech representations with fine-grained detail, which are then reconstructed by a coarse-grained codec operating on these representations. This design explicitly separates the generation process into stages of differing granularity, moving from linguistic or phoneme-level tokens toward full acoustic reconstruction.

## 4.3 Token Denoising and Robust Decoding for Zero-Shot TTS

Recent work has explored ways to improve the robustness of decoding from discrete speech tokens by injecting additional conditioning into the token-to-waveform generation process. In the DiffSoundStream framework, the authors propose conditioning both the encoder and decoder of a SoundStream model on semantic tokens through Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) layers (DiffSoundStream, n.d.). This conditioning is applied right before the last encoder block and right after the first decoder block, encouraging the autoencoder to leverage information already embedded in the semantic tokens during acoustic token decoding (DiffSoundStream, n.d.). The semantic tokens are derived from WavLM features that are downsampled and quantized to 2048 centroids via k-means clustering, yielding a token rate of 12.5 Hz, while the acoustic tokens operate at 50 Hz (DiffSoundStream, n.d.). This design represents an effort to denoise or regularize the decoding pathway by explicitly informing the acoustic reconstruction with higher-level semantic context.

In parallel, Li et al. (2024) introduce FoundationTTS, a framework that combines discrete speech tokens in a hierarchical fashion with a large language model to achieve long-term consistency and diverse output on large-scale and complex speech datasets. While the provided evidence does not detail specific denoising mechanisms within FoundationTTS, the hierarchical token combination suggests a structured decoding approach where different granularities of speech representation contribute to robust generation. Together, these efforts indicate a growing interest in conditioning strategies and hierarchical token architectures to improve the fidelity and consistency of zero-shot TTS decoding.

# 5 Discrete Speech Representations for Emotion and Enhancement

## 5.1 Multi-Layer Fusion of Discrete Tokens for Speech Emotion Recognition

Recent work by Zhao et al. (2026) addresses the challenge that discrete speech tokens, while efficient, may not adequately preserve the paralinguistic and prosodic information essential for speech emotion recognition (SER). To overcome this limitation, they employ an attention-based cross-layer fusion mechanism that architecturally combines information across WavLM layers and augment the discrete tokens with seven families of discretized OpenSMILE features at the feature level (Zhao et al., 2026). Their study compares mainstream neural codec tokenizers—SpeechTokenizer, DAC, and EnCodec—and analyzes their behaviors when fused with acoustic features. The findings demonstrate that through multi-layer fusion and acoustic feature integration, discrete tokens can close the performance gap with continuous representations in SER tasks (Zhao et al., 2026).

## 5.2 Generative Speech Enhancement via Continuous Codec Embeddings

Recent work has explored generative speech enhancement by operating on continuous embeddings produced by neural audio codecs. Li et al. (2025) propose a method that performs enhancement directly in the continuous representation space of a codec encoder. Specifically, the noisy input is first encoded by a DAC encoder to produce a continuous embedding \(y_e\). A speech enhancement model then predicts a clean continuous embedding \(y_h\) from \(y_e\), after which the codec’s quantizer module converts \(y_h\) into a discrete representation \(y_q^h\). This quantized representation is finally passed to the codec decoder to synthesize the enhanced speech waveform. By working at the continuous embedding level rather than on discrete tokens, the approach retains the generative capabilities of the codec while avoiding the information loss that can accompany early quantization.

In contrast, Zhao et al. (2026) focus on discrete speech representations for synthesis and spoof detection, employing neural codec tokenization via EnCodec, DAC, and SpeechTokenizer to obtain discrete tokens. Their framework uses multi-layer discrete units from a fine-tuned WavLM with layer-specific codebooks and augments these representations with quantized OpenSMILE paralinguistic features through hierarchical fusion. While this work does not address speech enhancement directly, it illustrates an alternative paradigm where discrete codec tokens serve as the primary representation for downstream tasks, highlighting the design choice between continuous and discrete codec-based processing pipelines.

# 6 Future Directions

Current neural audio codec systems face several interrelated limitations that constrain their broader applicability. Most architectures still process semantic and acoustic information through largely separate pipelines, even within unified frameworks, and the resulting discrete tokens frequently fail to preserve paralinguistic and prosodic cues essential for expressive speech tasks. The decoding stage remains dominated by deterministic or GAN-based generators, with only initial explorations of latent diffusion models, leaving the full potential of stochastic decoding underexplored. Furthermore, the trade-off between compression efficiency and the retention of speaker identity, emotional expressiveness, and environmental context has not been systematically addressed. The reliance on fixed codebook sizes and static quantization schemes limits adaptability across diverse downstream tasks, languages, and acoustic conditions. Evaluation protocols also lag behind architectural innovation, as conventional intelligibility and quality metrics do not adequately capture speaker consistency, emotional fidelity, or robustness to real-world noise and channel variability.

Future research should pursue fully integrated tokenization frameworks where semantic, acoustic, and paralinguistic information streams are jointly learned within a single latent space from the outset, rather than being fused post hoc. This could involve dynamic, task-adaptive codebooks that adjust their capacity and structure based on the target application, whether it be high-fidelity speech synthesis, emotion preservation, or low-bitrate transmission. Advancing beyond deterministic reconstruction toward more sophisticated generative models—such as diffusion-based or flow-matching decoders conditioned on hierarchical representations—promises to improve naturalness and robustness, particularly for zero-shot voice cloning and cross-lingual synthesis. Another important direction is the development of evaluation protocols that explicitly measure the preservation of paralinguistic attributes, speaker consistency, and environmental acoustics, moving beyond conventional intelligibility and quality metrics. Additionally, exploring lightweight and streaming-friendly variants of these unified codecs will be critical for real-time applications such as live translation, assistive communication devices, and on-device speech processing. Systematic investigation into the interpretability of disentangled token spaces could further enable fine-grained control over synthesis attributes, allowing users to independently manipulate linguistic content, speaker identity, and emotional tone.

Progress along these directions could substantially broaden the impact of neural audio codecs beyond traditional compression and synthesis. Unified representations that faithfully capture the full richness of speech—including who is speaking, how they are speaking, and in what context—would enable more natural and emotionally aware voice assistants, improved accessibility tools that preserve user identity, and robust speech processing in noisy or multilingual environments. By making these representations adaptable and computationally efficient, future codecs could become foundational components not only for media streaming and telecommunications but also for healthcare applications such as remote diagnosis through voice analysis, language learning platforms, and immersive virtual communication systems. The convergence of self-supervised learning, generative modeling, and adaptive quantization within a single codec paradigm holds the potential to bridge the remaining gaps between human-like speech naturalness and machine efficiency, ultimately making high-quality speech technologies more accessible and versatile across diverse real-world deployment scenarios.


# 7 Conclusion

Recent advances in neural audio codecs have established that multi-scale hierarchical encoding, unified tokenization frameworks, and disentangled representations collectively drive substantial improvements in low-bitrate speech compression and generation. The surveyed work demonstrates that projecting high-dimensional latent spaces into compact quantization subspaces, coupled with stabilized codebook learning via cosine similarity and explicit loss functions, enhances training stability and reconstruction fidelity. Parallel efforts in semantic–contextual fusion show that integrating linguistic and paralinguistic information directly into the encoder latent space yields unified representations that outperform purely compression-oriented models in both intelligibility and perceptual quality. The emergence of global and local token disentanglement further refines this landscape by separating semantic content from acoustic detail, enabling more coherent and speaker-consistent synthesis. In decoding, the incorporation of latent diffusion models and conditioning mechanisms, such as FiLM layers informed by semantic tokens, introduces beneficial stochasticity and denoising capacity that deterministic decoders lack. Moreover, the extension of codec representations to downstream tasks reveals that continuous embeddings can preserve generative capability while avoiding early quantization loss, whereas discrete tokens augmented with paralinguistic features can close the performance gap with continuous representations in emotion recognition.

This survey holds significance for the speech processing community by synthesizing a rapidly evolving body of work that redefines the role of neural codecs beyond traditional compression. By mapping the design space across hierarchical encoding, multimodal fusion, token disentanglement, and generative decoding, the survey provides a structured understanding of how these techniques interact to address the fundamental trade-off between bitrate, acoustic fidelity, and semantic coherence. The comparative analysis of continuous versus discrete processing pipelines offers practical guidance for selecting representations tailored to specific tasks, whether high-quality waveform reconstruction, zero-shot text-to-speech, or paralinguistic-sensitive applications. Furthermore, the survey highlights the convergence of self-supervised learning and neural codec paradigms, underscoring the potential for unified architectures that serve both generative and discriminative downstream objectives.

Looking forward, the field would benefit from systematic benchmarking of codec designs under consistent evaluation protocols that jointly measure compression efficiency, perceptual quality, speaker consistency, and task-specific robustness. Future research should investigate the scalability of unified tokenization frameworks to multilingual and noisy real-world conditions, as well as the interpretability of disentangled token spaces. The integration of codec-based representations with large language models for speech generation presents an open frontier, where hierarchical token structures may enable more controllable and expressive synthesis. Ultimately, continued innovation in neural audio codecs promises to bridge the remaining gaps between human-like speech naturalness and machine efficiency, making high-quality speech technologies more accessible and versatile.
