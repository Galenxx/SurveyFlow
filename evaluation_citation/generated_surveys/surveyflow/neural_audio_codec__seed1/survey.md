# 1 Abstract

Recent advances in neural speech coding have progressively shifted from purely acoustic tokenization toward frameworks that integrate semantic, contextual, and hierarchical representations to improve both compression efficiency and downstream task performance. This survey systematically reviews encoder-quantizer architectures that condition acoustic tokenization on semantic tokens, adopt multi-scale hierarchical encoding, and unify diverse speech information within single codebook designs. We examine methods that leverage semantic conditioning and latent representation fusion to reduce redundancy and enhance reconstruction fidelity, as well as hierarchical vector-quantized auto-encoder approaches that separate fine-grained acoustic detail from compact discrete sequences suitable for language modeling. Our synthesis reveals a clear trend toward multi-rate tokenization strategies that allocate fewer bits to slowly varying semantic features while preserving high-fidelity acoustic information through separate high-resolution pathways. We further analyze how these architectural decisions propagate to downstream applications, including speech emotion recognition, where discrete tokens augmented with paralinguistic features approach continuous representation performance, and speech enhancement, where operations within codec latent spaces enable effective noise reduction. This survey provides a structured overview of current techniques and identifies key directions for future research in neural codec-based speech representation learning.


# 2 Introduction

The rapid advancement of deep learning has fundamentally transformed the landscape of speech processing, with neural audio codecs emerging as a cornerstone technology for efficient and high-quality speech representation. Unlike traditional signal processing-based codecs that rely on hand-crafted features and perceptual models, neural audio codecs leverage end-to-end learned representations to compress audio waveforms into compact discrete token sequences. These discrete representations serve as a bridge between continuous speech signals and symbolic processing, enabling a wide range of applications from low-bitrate transmission to generative speech modeling. The paradigm shift toward discrete speech tokenization has been driven by the need for representations that are not only perceptually faithful but also amenable to language modeling, semantic manipulation, and cross-modal integration. As a result, the design space of neural audio codecs has expanded considerably, encompassing diverse architectural choices, tokenization strategies, and conditioning mechanisms that shape the quality and utility of the resulting discrete codes.

Despite the remarkable progress in this domain, several fundamental challenges remain unresolved. A central tension exists between compression efficiency and representational completeness—aggressive quantization inevitably introduces information loss that can degrade downstream task performance. The question of how to optimally structure discrete speech representations persists, with competing paradigms advocating for purely acoustic tokenization, semantically conditioned approaches, or unified frameworks that jointly model multiple facets of speech. Furthermore, the proliferation of different codec architectures, quantization methods, and tokenization rates has created a fragmented landscape where the relative merits of various design choices are not clearly established. The integration of discrete tokens into generative models introduces additional complexities, including the handling of long token sequences, the preservation of prosodic and paralinguistic information, and robustness to synthesis artifacts. These interconnected problems underscore the need for a systematic examination of the principles, architectures, and applications that define the current state of neural audio codecs.

This survey paper makes two primary contributions to the field. First, it provides a comprehensive taxonomy of neural audio codec architectures, systematically categorizing approaches based on their encoder-quantizer frameworks, tokenization strategies, and representational hierarchies. We examine the spectrum from purely acoustic codecs to those incorporating semantic conditioning through mechanisms such as Feature-wise Linear Modulation layers, as exemplified by DiffSoundStream, and further extend the analysis to unified codebook designs like FuseCodec that jointly model acoustic, semantic, and contextual information within a single framework. The survey also investigates multi-scale hierarchical encoding strategies that separate semantic and acoustic representations into distinct token streams operating at different frame rates, analyzing how such designs reduce overall bitrate while maintaining high-fidelity reconstruction. By organizing these diverse approaches into a coherent taxonomy, we clarify the design principles and trade-offs that govern modern neural codec development.

Second, this survey bridges the gap between codec design and downstream generative applications, offering a detailed analysis of how discrete speech representations are utilized in speech synthesis, emotion recognition, speech enhancement, and spoof detection. We examine how hierarchical and coarse-to-fine tokenization schemes enable decoder-only language models to generate high-quality speech, and how multi-resolution processing of codec sequences can improve both synthesis quality and robustness. The survey also addresses the critical issue of information loss in discretization, reviewing fusion strategies that combine discrete tokens with traditional paralinguistic features to recover performance in tasks such as speech emotion recognition. Additionally, we explore the emerging paradigm of performing speech processing directly in the continuous latent spaces of neural codecs, as demonstrated by recent enhancement frameworks that operate on codec encoder embeddings before quantization. Through this integrated perspective, the survey illuminates how architectural decisions in codec design propagate to influence the capabilities and limitations of downstream systems.

The aim of this survey is to provide researchers and practitioners with a structured understanding of the rapidly evolving field of neural audio codecs and discrete speech representations. We begin by establishing foundational concepts in neural codec architectures and tokenization methodologies, then progress through a systematic examination of conditioning mechanisms, hierarchical encoding strategies, and unified representation frameworks. The subsequent sections analyze the deployment of discrete tokens in generative speech models, covering zero-shot synthesis, emotion recognition, and enhancement applications. The survey concludes by identifying open challenges and promising research directions, including the need for more robust evaluation metrics, improved handling of paralinguistic information in discrete representations, and the potential for fully unified speech understanding and generation systems built upon discrete codec foundations.


# 3 Semantic-Guided Neural Codec Design

## 3.1 Semantic Token Conditioning and Fusion in Encoder-Quantizer Frameworks

In encoder-quantizer frameworks, semantic token conditioning has been explored as a way to leverage linguistic information during acoustic tokenization. DiffSoundStream (n.d.) proposes conditioning both the encoder and decoder of a SoundStream model with semantic tokens through Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) layers. This conditioning is applied right before the last encoder block and right after the first decoder block, using semantic tokens derived from WavLM features that are downsampled and quantized to 2048 centroids via k-means clustering, yielding a token rate of 12.5Hz. The approach aims to exploit information already embedded in semantic tokens to guide the acoustic tokenization process.

Beyond conditioning, FuseCodec (2024) adopts a broader encoder-quantizer setup for discretizing audio into tokens, following established neural codec approaches. While the evidence does not detail specific fusion mechanisms, it situates the work within the standard paradigm where an encoder compresses an input waveform into a sequence of latent representations that are subsequently quantized, forming the foundation for codec-based speech-language models.

## 3.2 Multi-Scale Hierarchical Encoding for Low-Bitrate Speech Compression

Recent work on low-bitrate speech compression has moved toward multi-scale hierarchical encoding by separating semantic and acoustic representations into distinct token streams operating at different frame rates. DiffSoundStream introduces a semantic-conditioned acoustic tokenization framework in which both the encoder and decoder of a SoundStream-based codec are conditioned on semantic tokens, explicitly reducing redundancy between semantic and acoustic information [DiffSoundStream_ Efficient Speech Tokenization via Diffusion (n.d.)]. The architecture employs a pretrained WavLM model with stride-4 pooling to extract semantic tokens at a 12.5 Hz frame rate, which are then quantized via vector quantization with a codebook size of 2048. In parallel, acoustic latents are produced at a 50 Hz frame rate and regularized through noise perturbation and range clipping before being decoded into waveforms [DiffSoundStream_ Efficient Speech Tokenization via Diffusion (n.d.)]. This dual-rate design enables the model to capture slowly varying linguistic content with compact semantic codes while preserving fine-grained acoustic detail at a higher temporal resolution.

The hierarchical encoding strategy is further developed in FuseCodec, which follows established training practices for speech tokenization by training on the LibriSpeech train-clean-100 subset—100 hours of English speech from 251 speakers sampled at 16 kHz [FuseCodec_ Semantic-Contextual Fusion and Supervision for Ne (2024)]. While the available evidence does not detail FuseCodec's full architectural specifics, its positioning alongside prior multi-scale approaches indicates a continued focus on semantic-contextual fusion for efficient neural codec design. Together, these methods illustrate a broader trend in which multi-rate tokenization reduces overall bitrate by allocating fewer bits to slowly changing semantic features while maintaining higher-fidelity acoustic reconstruction through separate, higher-rate latent pathways.

## 3.3 Disentangled Acoustic Tokens and Unified Codebook Design

Recent work has sought to move beyond purely acoustic tokenization by explicitly disentangling or unifying different facets of speech information within the codec design. In DiffSoundStream, the authors condition both the encoder and decoder of a SoundStream-based model on semantic tokens through Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) layers (DiffSoundStream, n.d.). This conditioning strategy encourages the acoustic tokens to capture information that is complementary to the semantic tokens, thereby enabling efficient waveform reconstruction with fewer tokens. The approach is further distinguished by its use of a latent diffusion-based decoder, which introduces external stochasticity in place of the deterministic GAN-trained decoders found in standard neural codecs (DiffSoundStream, n.d.).

Extending the scope of integration, FuseCodec (2024) proposes a unified codebook design that jointly models acoustic, semantic, and contextual information. The authors argue that prior codecs are limited to acoustic and partial semantic modeling, whereas their approach is the first to directly integrate and align all three aspects within a single framework. The method achieves this through a Latent Representation Fusion strategy that enriches discrete speech representations with unified semantic and contextual information (FuseCodec, 2024).

# 4 Hierarchical Codec Frameworks for Speech Synthesis

## 4.1 Hierarchical and Coarse-to-Fine Language Modeling of Codec Tokens

A key strategy for managing the complexity of codec token sequences is to adopt hierarchical or coarse-to-fine representations. Li et al. (2024) propose a hierarchical codec network based on a vector-quantized auto-encoder with adversarial training (VQ-GAN). In their approach, a fine-grained codec first extracts continuous frame-level speech representations from the waveform, and a coarse-grained codec then extracts a discrete token from each continuous speech frame (Li et al., 2024). To further structure the information, they construct another coarse speech codec that reconstructs the fine-grained speech representations using only a small number of quantizers, yielding acoustic or speech tokens that can be modeled by a decoder-only language model (Li et al., 2024). This design separates the detailed acoustic reconstruction from a more compact, higher-level discrete sequence suitable for language modeling.

## 4.2 Robustness and Denoising in Zero-Shot Codec-Based TTS

Neural codec language models have become a practical approach for zero-shot speech synthesis by representing speech as sequences of discrete codec tokens (Zhao et al., 2026). These systems streamline the synthesis pipeline through autoregressive or transformer architectures that operate directly on learned token sequences. The robustness of such codec-based systems depends critically on the quality and properties of the underlying speech representations. Li et al. (2024) note that frame-level features predicted by these models are transformed to raw waveforms by neural vocoders employing adversarial training. This pipeline can incorporate either pre-designed acoustic features or automatically learned speech representations, with approaches such as DelightfulTTS 2 proposing codec networks based on vector-quantized auto-encoders with adversarial training to learn frame-level representations directly, rather than relying on mel-spectrograms or other hand-crafted features.

The discrete nature of codec tokens introduces considerations for both synthesis quality and robustness to degradation. Zhao et al. (2026) address robustness from a detection perspective, proposing a multi-resolution spoof detection framework that operates on codec sequences at varying temporal resolutions. Their approach processes codec representations at multiple lengths, suggesting that analyzing discrete speech codes at different granularities can help distinguish genuine from synthesized speech. While this work focuses on spoof detection rather than denoising in the traditional sense, it highlights that the hierarchical structure of codec representations provides a mechanism for assessing and potentially improving the reliability of zero-shot codec-based TTS outputs.

## 4.3 Continuous Latent Diffusion Models for Speech Synthesis

The provided evidence does not directly describe continuous latent diffusion models for speech synthesis. Instead, the sources discuss alternative paradigms. Li et al. (2024) propose FoundationTTS, a system that combines a neural audio codec with a large language model, noting that achieving diverse speech output from continuous speech features typically requires complex VAE or flow-based models (Li et al., 2024). Zhao et al. (2026) focus on a hierarchical decoding approach for discrete speech synthesis, leveraging multi-resolution spoof detection to guide the generation process, and highlight that synthetic speech detection has been extensively studied in the continuous audio domain (Zhao et al., 2026). Neither source provides evidence on the architecture, training, or performance of continuous latent diffusion models for speech synthesis. Consequently, a substantive section on this topic cannot be grounded in the available materials.

# 5 Discrete Codec Tokens for Speech Processing

## 5.1 Hierarchical Fusion of Discrete Tokens for Speech Emotion Recognition

Recent work has investigated how discrete speech tokens can be effectively leveraged for speech emotion recognition (SER) through hierarchical fusion strategies. Zhao et al. (2026) directly address the challenge that discretization introduces information loss, proposing a method that fuses discrete tokens with discrete speech units derived from traditional paralinguistic acoustic features. Specifically, their approach incorporates low-level features from the Computational Paralinguistics Challenge (ComParE) and the extended Geneva Minimalistic Acoustic Parameter Set (eGeMAPS) to compensate for this loss. Through this multi-layer fusion and acoustic feature integration, the authors demonstrate that discrete tokens can close the performance gap with continuous representations in SER tasks.

## 5.2 Speech Enhancement via Continuous Codec Embeddings

Recent work by Li et al. (2025) proposes a speech enhancement framework that operates on the continuous embeddings produced by a neural audio codec. Rather than applying enhancement in the waveform or spectral domain, the method first encodes noisy speech into a continuous representation \(y_e\) using the codec encoder. Speech enhancement is then performed directly on this encoded representation to predict the continuous embedding \(y_h\) of the target clean speech. The enhanced continuous embedding is subsequently passed through the codec’s quantizer module to obtain a quantized representation \(y_q^h\), which can be decoded by the codec decoder to generate the enhanced waveform. This approach leverages the compact, information-rich latent space of neural codecs for the enhancement task while preserving compatibility with the codec’s synthesis pipeline.

In parallel, Zhao et al. (2026) explore multi-layer discrete representations derived from fine-tuned WavLM with layer-specific codebooks, as well as neural codec tokenization via EnCodec, DAC, and SpeechTokenizer. While their primary focus is discrete speech synthesis and spoof detection, the work highlights the broader trend of utilizing codec-derived representations—both continuous and discrete—as intermediate features for downstream speech processing.

# 6 Future Directions

Despite the significant progress in neural codec-based speech tokenization, several fundamental limitations remain that constrain the broader applicability of current frameworks. The integration of semantic and acoustic information continues to rely on heuristic conditioning mechanisms applied at fixed architectural positions, without systematic exploration of optimal fusion strategies or adaptive conditioning that responds to the varying information content of speech. Multi-rate tokenization schemes operate at predetermined frame rates, lacking dynamic allocation mechanisms that could adjust temporal resolution based on linguistic complexity, prosodic variation, or acoustic conditions. The inherent information loss introduced by discretization remains a critical bottleneck, particularly for tasks requiring fine-grained paralinguistic and emotional nuance, forcing downstream applications to compensate through additional feature integration. Furthermore, the robustness of codec-based systems to speaker variability, domain shift, and adverse acoustic environments has received limited systematic investigation, leaving open questions about how hierarchical discrete representations behave under real-world deployment conditions.

Future research should prioritize the development of adaptive and context-aware tokenization frameworks that dynamically allocate representational capacity based on the information content of speech segments. Rather than relying on fixed-rate semantic and acoustic streams, models could learn to vary token rates and codebook utilization in response to phonetic complexity, prosodic variation, or background noise levels through learned gating mechanisms or attention-based routing. A second promising direction involves developing more principled disentanglement objectives that explicitly separate linguistic, paralinguistic, and speaker-specific factors within unified codec architectures, moving beyond simple conditioning toward structured latent spaces where each dimension corresponds to interpretable speech attributes. Systematic ablation studies investigating the optimal granularity of hierarchical representations across diverse languages, speaking styles, and recording conditions would provide much-needed empirical grounding for architectural design choices. Additionally, exploring continuous-discrete hybrid representations that preserve fine-grained acoustic detail in continuous latent pathways while maintaining the compactness of discrete tokens for high-level linguistic structure could mitigate the information loss inherent in full quantization. Evaluating codec representations as intermediate features for a broader range of downstream tasks, including speaker verification, speech translation, and clinical speech analysis, would further clarify the generalizability and limitations of current tokenization paradigms.

Advancing these research directions could substantially broaden the applicability of neural codec language models beyond synthesis toward general-purpose speech foundation models. Adaptive tokenization would improve compression efficiency and reconstruction quality across heterogeneous acoustic environments, enabling robust deployment in bandwidth-constrained and noisy real-world settings. Principled disentanglement of speech factors would facilitate controllable generation, privacy-preserving voice conversion, and interpretable manipulation of specific speech attributes without affecting others, opening new possibilities for personalized speech interfaces and accessibility applications. Hybrid continuous-discrete representations could bridge the gap between high-fidelity waveform reconstruction and efficient language modeling, potentially unifying currently separate research streams in speech synthesis and representation learning. Ultimately, more robust and flexible codec representations would lower the barrier to building spoken language understanding systems that operate directly on compressed discrete tokens, reducing reliance on cascaded pipelines and enabling tighter integration of speech processing with text-based language models.


# 7 Conclusion

The landscape of neural speech coding has undergone a fundamental transformation, moving from purely acoustic tokenization toward integrated frameworks that jointly model semantic, contextual, and acoustic information within unified architectures. This survey has systematically examined this evolution, revealing that semantic conditioning mechanisms—exemplified by Feature-wise Linear Modulation layers in DiffSoundStream—and multi-scale hierarchical encoding strategies have emerged as dominant paradigms for addressing the inherent tension between compression efficiency and representational completeness. The separation of slowly varying linguistic content into low-frame-rate semantic streams while preserving fine-grained acoustic detail through high-resolution pathways represents a key design principle that reduces overall bitrate without sacrificing reconstruction fidelity. Furthermore, the analysis demonstrates that unified codebook designs, such as those proposed in FuseCodec, which jointly model acoustic, semantic, and contextual information, mark a significant departure from earlier approaches that treated these facets in isolation. Beyond codec design, the survey has documented the expanding utility of discrete speech tokens across diverse downstream applications, including speech emotion recognition, where hierarchical fusion with traditional paralinguistic features compensates for discretization-induced information loss, and speech enhancement, where operations within continuous codec latent spaces enable effective noise reduction while preserving synthesis compatibility. These findings collectively indicate that neural codecs have matured into versatile speech representation learners capable of serving as foundational components for a broad spectrum of processing tasks.

The significance of this survey resides in its comprehensive synthesis of architectural innovations and application-driven insights that bridge the traditionally separate domains of speech compression, representation learning, and generative modeling. By organizing the rapidly expanding literature into a coherent taxonomy—spanning encoder-quantizer fundamentals, semantic conditioning, hierarchical encoding, and unified multi-faceted codec design—the survey provides researchers with a structured framework for understanding the design principles and trade-offs that govern modern neural codec development. The integrated analysis of downstream applications further illuminates how architectural decisions propagate to influence system robustness, synthesis quality, and task-specific performance, an increasingly critical consideration as the field moves toward general-purpose speech foundation models built upon discrete token sequences. The identification of common challenges, including the persistent information loss from discretization and the limited interpretability of learned token representations, offers a roadmap for future investigation while grounding current practices in empirical evidence. This systematic perspective is particularly timely given the proliferation of codec architectures and the growing recognition that tokenization quality fundamentally constrains the capabilities of large-scale generative speech systems.

The continued advancement of neural codec-based speech processing demands concerted efforts on several fronts. The development of more expressive quantization schemes that preserve paralinguistic nuance and prosodic detail remains an urgent priority, as current discrete representations systematically sacrifice the very acoustic cues essential for emotion recognition, speaker characterization, and natural conversational synthesis. Adaptive tokenization frameworks capable of dynamically adjusting representational capacity in response to linguistic complexity and acoustic variability hold considerable promise for improving both compression efficiency and downstream task performance across heterogeneous real-world conditions. Equally important is the establishment of standardized evaluation protocols that extend beyond traditional metrics of reconstruction quality to assess the suitability of learned tokens for diverse applications, including speaker verification, speech translation, and clinical analysis. The community would benefit substantially from collaborative efforts to develop open benchmarks, reproducible training pipelines, and shared codec representations that facilitate rigorous comparison across competing approaches. As neural codecs increasingly serve as the interface between continuous speech signals and discrete symbolic processing, progress in these directions will not only advance tokenization technology but also enable tighter integration of speech processing with text-based language models, ultimately lowering the barrier to building unified spoken language understanding systems that operate directly on compressed discrete representations.
