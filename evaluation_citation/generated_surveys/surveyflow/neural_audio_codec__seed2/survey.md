# 1 Abstract

Recent advances in neural audio codecs have established discrete speech representations as foundational components across a wide range of speech processing tasks, from high-fidelity compression to generative synthesis and discriminative analysis. This survey provides a structured overview of current research on conditioning mechanisms, tokenization strategies, and generative modeling within codec-based systems, with an emphasis on the integration of semantic and acoustic information. We examine approaches that condition codec encoders and decoders on higher-level semantic representations through feature-wise modulation, as well as hierarchical and multi-resolution tokenization methods that capture complementary speech attributes at different granularities. Key findings indicate that semantic conditioning and hierarchical fusion of discrete tokens with paralinguistic features can substantially narrow the performance gap with continuous representations in tasks such as speech emotion recognition, while multi-resolution token-level detectors enable more robust and controllable generation. Additionally, performing speech enhancement directly in the continuous embedding space of neural codecs offers a compact and effective alternative to conventional waveform-domain processing. This survey synthesizes these developments to clarify emerging trends, highlight persistent challenges such as train–inference mismatch and discretization-induced information loss, and outline promising directions for building unified, robust, and versatile speech representations.


# 2 Introduction

Neural audio codecs have become a foundational technology in modern speech processing, fundamentally transforming how continuous audio signals are represented, transmitted, and manipulated. By employing encoder-decoder architectures augmented with vector quantization, these systems compress speech waveforms into compact sequences of discrete tokens that retain essential acoustic information while discarding imperceptible details. The advent of deep learning has propelled neural codecs to achieve remarkably high-fidelity reconstruction at extremely low bitrates, opening new frontiers in speech synthesis, voice conversion, and enhancement. Beyond their original purpose as compression tools, the discrete representations generated by these codecs have emerged as powerful intermediate features for generative modeling, serving as the backbone for language model-based text-to-speech systems, zero-shot voice cloning, and other speech generation paradigms. This dual identity—as both efficient compression mechanisms and semantically rich acoustic representations—has positioned neural audio codecs at the center of a rapidly evolving research ecosystem that spans tokenization strategies, architectural innovations, and diverse downstream applications.

Despite these advances, several fundamental challenges remain unresolved in the design and deployment of neural audio codecs. A central tension exists between compression efficiency and the faithful preservation of both acoustic fidelity and linguistic content. Standard codec architectures, which extract speech representations, quantize them through residual vector quantizers, and reconstruct waveforms via adversarial training, often excel on small, clean datasets but struggle to maintain quality when confronted with larger, more diverse, or noisy real-world data. Another critical problem concerns the integration of semantic information into the tokenization process: while conditioning codec components on higher-level representations can guide acoustic tokenization, the optimal strategy for combining semantic and acoustic cues within a unified architecture remains an open question. The discretization inherent in codec tokenization introduces additional information loss that degrades performance on downstream tasks such as speech emotion recognition and spoof detection. Furthermore, in generative applications, codec language models face a persistent discrepancy between training and inference conditions, as models must rely on their own previously generated tokens rather than ground-truth sequences during decoding. These interconnected challenges underscore the need for a systematic understanding of how architectural choices, tokenization strategies, and conditioning mechanisms collectively influence the quality and utility of discrete speech representations.

This survey provides a comprehensive examination of neural audio codecs and discrete speech representations, with a structured focus on three interrelated dimensions: architectures, tokenization methods, and generative applications. The first contribution is a systematic synthesis of recent advances in codec design, encompassing both conventional encoder-decoder frameworks and emerging approaches that unify semantic and acoustic encoding within single architectures. We analyze how conditioning mechanisms—such as Feature-wise Linear Modulation layers guided by semantic tokens—can steer the tokenization process toward more efficient and structured representations, and we examine architectural paradigms that separate text-to-feature prediction from waveform generation. By comparing causal and non-causal tokenization strategies, as well as methods that distill information from self-supervised learning models, we illuminate the design space of modern neural codecs and their inherent trade-offs in terms of compression rate, reconstruction quality, and semantic richness. This synthesis reveals how architectural decisions cascade through the entire speech processing pipeline, affecting not only compression performance but also the suitability of learned representations for downstream tasks.

The second contribution is an in-depth exploration of how discrete codec representations are leveraged across a spectrum of generative and discriminative applications. We review coarse-to-fine token prediction strategies that exploit the hierarchical structure of residual vector quantization layers, multi-resolution token-level frameworks for spoof detection that operate directly on codec sequences during decoding, and discrete diffusion models that offer alternatives to autoregressive generation. The survey also addresses the challenge of information loss from discretization, discussing fusion methods that integrate discrete tokens with complementary paralinguistic acoustic features to recover emotional and prosodic information. Additionally, we cover the emerging use of codec representations in speech enhancement, where denoising is performed directly on continuous codec embeddings before quantization and decoding, offering a compact and effective alternative to waveform-domain processing. By connecting tokenization design choices to their tangible impact on applications—including zero-shot text-to-speech, emotional face-to-speech synthesis, and speech emotion recognition—this survey bridges the gap between codec architecture research and practical deployment considerations.

The aim of this survey is to provide researchers and practitioners with a structured overview of the current state of neural audio codecs and discrete speech representations, clarifying the relationships between architectural decisions, tokenization paradigms, and application-level performance. The paper is organized as follows. We begin by establishing foundational concepts in neural audio codec architectures and vector quantization techniques. We then examine tokenization strategies, including semantic conditioning and hierarchical approaches, before turning to generative applications that build upon discrete representations, such as codec language models and diffusion-based synthesis. Subsequent sections address task-specific adaptations, including spoof detection, speech emotion recognition, and speech enhancement. Throughout, we highlight open challenges and promising directions for future research, aiming to foster continued innovation in this dynamic and rapidly expanding field.


# 3 Diffusion-Based Neural Codecs with Hierarchical VQ

## 3.1 Hierarchical and Disentangled Acoustic Tokenization

Recent work has explored conditioning neural audio codecs on higher-level representations to encourage more efficient or structured acoustic tokenization. DiffSoundStream proposes to improve the efficiency of speech tokenization by conditioning both the encoder and decoder of a SoundStream model on semantic tokens through Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) layers (DiffSoundStream, n.d.). This design leverages the information already embedded in the semantic tokens to guide the acoustic tokenization process.

In a related direction, Li et al. (2024) investigate a two-stage framework that separates text-to-feature prediction from waveform generation, using a self-supervised vector-quantized acoustic feature as an intermediate representation. Their approach employs a classification-based txt2vec model and a vec2wav vocoder that incorporates an additional feature encoder for smoothing the acoustic features. This disentanglement between linguistic content and acoustic detail aligns with the broader goal of hierarchical tokenization, where different token types capture complementary aspects of speech.

## 3.2 Semantic-Acoustic Fusion in Neural Codecs

Recent work has explored directly conditioning neural codec components on semantic representations to improve acoustic tokenization. In DiffSoundStream, the authors propose to condition both the encoder and decoder of a SoundStream model with semantic tokens through Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) layers, aiming to leverage information already embedded in the semantic tokens for acoustic tokenization (DiffSoundStream, n.d.). This approach focuses on non-causal tokenization and directly uses SSL-derived semantic tokens without distillation, in contrast to methods that unify semantic and acoustic encoding via a single encoder by distilling information from a self-supervised learning model (DiffSoundStream, n.d.). While this line of work demonstrates the potential of semantic conditioning, Li et al. (2024) note that standard codec architectures—which extract speech representations, quantize them with residual vector quantizers, and reconstruct waveforms with adversarial training—achieve high quality primarily on small, clean datasets and face challenges when scaling to larger or more diverse, noisy data.

## 3.3 Unified and Multi-Domain Codebook Architectures

Recent work has explored unifying semantic and acoustic information within a single codec architecture. DiffSoundStream adopts a non-causal tokenization approach that directly leverages semantic tokens derived from self-supervised learning (SSL) models without requiring distillation, in contrast to prior methods that distill non-causal semantic information into a causal encoder (DiffSoundStream, n.d.). This design enables the model to combine semantic and acoustic encoding within one encoder, while also reducing the overall token rate through a hybrid autoregressive and non-autoregressive framework (DiffSoundStream, n.d.).

In parallel, Li et al. (2024) investigate codec architectures that extract speech representations via a codec encoder, quantize them with residual vector quantizers, and reconstruct the waveform using a decoder trained with adversarial objectives. While these systems achieve high reconstruction quality on small, clean datasets, scaling them to larger and more diverse data remains a challenge (Li et al., 2024).

# 4 Discrete Token TTS with Spoof Detection

## 4.1 Token-Level Spoof Detection and Denoising for Codec TTS

Existing spoof detection systems are predominantly formulated on reconstructed audio signals and designed for post-hoc classification, without operating on discrete codec token sequences or guiding generation during decoding (Zhao et al., 2026). To address this gap, Zhao et al. (2026) propose MSpoof-TTS, a framework that integrates a separately trained multi-resolution token-level spoof detector into the decoding process for codec-based speech synthesis, while keeping the underlying language model fixed. This approach extends spoof detection to the token level, enabling the detector to influence generation directly during decoding rather than serving as a downstream classifier.

## 4.2 Coarse-to-Fine and Compressed Token Prediction

Recent work has explored coarse-to-fine and compressed token prediction strategies to improve the quality and controllability of discrete speech generation. Zhao et al. (2026) propose a multi-resolution token-based framework for spoof detection in synthesized speech, motivated by the observation that generated token sequences can progressively deviate from the natural codec distribution. Their approach aggregates token embeddings across multiple temporal resolutions using mean pooling over the temporal dimension, enabling the model to capture hierarchical information at different granularities. In a related vein, Ye et al. (2025) achieve hierarchical modeling of diverse information across different residual vector quantization (RVQ) layers in their emotional face-to-speech system, though further architectural details of this coarse-to-fine design are not elaborated in the provided evidence.

## 4.3 Latent Diffusion Models for Zero-Shot TTS


Neural codec language models have recently emerged as a practical and effective paradigm for zero-shot speech synthesis, operating by modeling speech as sequences of discrete codec tokens through autoregressive or transformer architectures (Zhao et al., 2026). These systems streamline the synthesis pipeline by predicting subsequent tokens conditioned on ground-truth codec sequences during training; however, at inference time the model must rely on its own previously generated tokens, introducing a discrepancy between training and decoding conditions (Zhao et al., 2026). Zhao et al. (2026) adopt a pretrained codec-based TTS system as a fixed base generator within their framework, highlighting the growing role of discrete representations in zero-shot synthesis.

In parallel, discrete diffusion models offer an alternative generative formulation for speech tasks. Ye et al. (2025) provide a preliminary treatment of discrete diffusion models, situating them alongside continuous diffusion models as a foundation for TTS applications. While their work focuses on emotional face-to-speech generation without vocal cues, the preliminary establishes discrete diffusion as a relevant modeling choice for speech synthesis more broadly.

# 5 Discrete Speech Representations for Enhancement

## 5.1 Hierarchical Fusion of Discrete Tokens for Emotion Recognition

Zhao et al. (2026) address the challenge of information loss caused by discretization in speech emotion recognition by proposing a hierarchical fusion approach that integrates discrete tokens with complementary acoustic features. To compensate for this loss, they design a method that fuses discrete tokens with discrete speech units derived from traditional paralinguistic acoustic features, specifically utilizing low-level features from the Computational Paralinguistics Challenge (ComParE) and the extended Geneva Minimalistic Acoustic Parameter Set (Zhao et al., 2026). Their work compares mainstream neural codec tokenizers—SpeechTokenizer, DAC, and EnCodec—and analyzes their behaviors when fused with these acoustic features. The findings demonstrate that through multi-layer fusion and acoustic feature integration, discrete tokens can close the performance gap with continuous representations in SER tasks (Zhao et al., 2026).

## 5.2 Continuous Codec Embeddings for Speech Enhancement

Recent work has explored the use of neural audio codec representations for speech enhancement (SE). Li et al. (2025) propose performing enhancement directly on the continuous embeddings produced by a neural audio codec. In their approach, a noisy input is first encoded to obtain a continuous representation. The SE model then predicts the continuous embedding of the target clean speech from this encoded representation. The enhanced continuous embedding is subsequently passed through the codec’s quantizer module to produce a quantized representation, which can be decoded by the codec’s decoder to generate the final enhanced waveform. This pipeline leverages the compact, information-rich continuous space of the codec for the enhancement task, while still allowing reconstruction through the standard codec decoding path.

# 6 Future Directions

The current landscape of neural audio codec research reveals several important limitations that warrant further investigation. Most existing codec architectures achieve high reconstruction quality primarily on small, clean datasets and struggle when scaled to larger, more diverse, or noisy real-world data. The integration of semantic conditioning into codec design, while promising, remains fragmented—approaches that condition on semantic tokens through feature-wise modulation have shown potential, yet a unified framework that seamlessly combines semantic and acoustic information without information loss has not been fully realized. Additionally, the discretization process inherent in codec tokenization introduces information bottlenecks, as evidenced by the performance gaps observed in speech emotion recognition tasks when relying solely on discrete tokens. The mismatch between training with ground-truth codec sequences and inference with self-generated tokens in codec language models further degrades generation quality. Moreover, token-level spoof detection and generation-time controllability remain nascent areas, with current systems typically operating as post-hoc classifiers rather than actively guiding the decoding process. Finally, the application of codec representations to tasks beyond synthesis, such as speech enhancement, has only been preliminarily explored.

Future research should prioritize developing robust codec architectures that maintain high fidelity across diverse acoustic conditions, potentially through adversarial training strategies augmented with data augmentation, multi-condition training, or noise-robust quantization schemes. A critical direction involves designing truly unified semantic-acoustic codecs where linguistic and paralinguistic information is jointly optimized within a single encoder-decoder framework, rather than relying on external conditioning modules. This could be achieved through hierarchical vector quantization that allocates different codebook levels to complementary aspects of speech, or through learned information bottlenecks that explicitly separate and recombine semantic and acoustic factors. Another promising avenue is bridging the train-inference discrepancy in codec language models by incorporating scheduled sampling, reinforcement learning, or discrete diffusion formulations that are inherently robust to autoregressive error accumulation. Extending token-level controllability mechanisms—such as integrating spoof detectors, emotion classifiers, or speaker verifiers directly into the decoding loop—could enable real-time guidance of generation quality and attribute consistency. Furthermore, the continuous embedding spaces of neural codecs represent an underexplored resource for speech enhancement, voice conversion, and bandwidth extension, where enhancement or transformation can be performed in the compact latent domain before decoding.

Advancing these research directions would substantially broaden the applicability and reliability of neural audio codecs. Robust codec architectures capable of handling diverse, noisy data would enable deployment in real-world communication systems, hearing assistance devices, and user-generated content platforms. Unified semantic-acoustic tokenization could streamline speech synthesis pipelines by reducing the need for separate linguistic frontends and acoustic models, while simultaneously improving the naturalness and expressiveness of generated speech. Closing the train-inference gap in codec language models would enhance zero-shot voice cloning and spontaneous speech generation, making these technologies more practical for personalized assistants and content creation. Token-level controllability during decoding would provide fine-grained safeguards against spoofing and malicious use, addressing growing concerns around deepfake audio. Finally, performing speech enhancement directly in codec latent spaces could lead to more computationally efficient and higher-quality denoising systems, as the compact representations naturally filter out irrelevant acoustic variation while preserving essential speech content. Collectively, these advances would move neural codecs from laboratory benchmarks toward robust, controllable, and trustworthy speech processing components suitable for widespread deployment.


# 7 Conclusion

Recent advances in neural audio codecs have substantially reshaped the landscape of speech generation, enhancement, and analysis. This survey has examined a range of emerging directions that move beyond conventional waveform reconstruction toward more structured, interpretable, and controllable tokenization paradigms. A central theme is the growing integration of semantic and acoustic information within codec architectures, as demonstrated by conditioning mechanisms that leverage self-supervised representations to guide tokenization without extensive distillation. Parallel efforts have explored hierarchical and multi-resolution token representations, enabling models to capture complementary aspects of speech at different granularities and to address challenges such as distributional drift during autoregressive generation. The survey further highlights the adoption of discrete codec tokens in zero-shot text-to-speech synthesis, where language model-based and diffusion-based formulations offer flexible generative frameworks, albeit with persistent train–test mismatches. In speech emotion recognition and enhancement, fusing discrete tokens with continuous acoustic features or operating directly within codec embedding spaces has proven effective in mitigating information loss and improving robustness. Collectively, these developments underscore a shift toward codecs as general-purpose speech representations that support a wide variety of downstream tasks beyond compression.

The significance of this survey lies in its systematic synthesis of convergent research threads that are often treated in isolation. By juxtaposing work on semantic conditioning, hierarchical tokenization, discrete diffusion, and task-specific adaptation, the survey clarifies how neural codecs are evolving from low-level compression tools into foundational components of speech processing pipelines. It identifies critical design choices—such as the trade-offs between causal and non-causal architectures, the integration of multi-resolution information, and the handling of discretization-induced information loss—that have broad implications for model scalability, data efficiency, and generalization. For practitioners, the survey provides a structured overview of current capabilities and limitations, highlighting where standard codec designs excel and where they falter under diverse or noisy conditions. For researchers, it maps out underexplored intersections, such as the joint optimization of tokenization and generation objectives, and the potential for unified architectures that seamlessly bridge semantic understanding and acoustic fidelity.

Moving forward, the field would benefit from deeper investigation into codec representations that are inherently robust to acoustic variability and scalable to large, heterogeneous datasets. Future work should prioritize the development of unified evaluation protocols that assess tokenization quality not only through reconstruction metrics but also through downstream task performance, controllability, and resilience to domain shift. There is also a pressing need to bridge the gap between token-level generation and real-world deployment, particularly in security-critical applications where spoof detection and generation must co-evolve. By fostering closer collaboration between the codec design, generative modeling, and speech application communities, the next generation of neural codecs can achieve the versatility and reliability required for widespread adoption.
