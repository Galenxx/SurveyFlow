# 1 Abstract

The detection of claims and misinformation in video content has traditionally relied on linguistic analysis through transcription and text-based methods, yet the proliferation of short-form video platforms and increasingly sophisticated AI-generated media demands approaches that can leverage non-linguistic visual and acoustic cues. This survey systematically examines the emerging landscape of claim detection, checkworthiness assessment, and forensic analysis across both short-form and long-form video modalities, with particular attention to methods that operate independently of full textual understanding. We review and synthesize recent advances spanning three interconnected domains: non-linguistic claim detection using visual and audio features, multilingual checkworthiness detection in short-form videos on platforms such as TikTok, and explainable forensic detection of manipulated segments in untrimmed video content. Our analysis reveals a significant gap between the motivating intuition that non-linguistic cues carry claim-relevant signals and the predominantly text-centric methods documented in the literature, while also identifying promising directions in cross-modal consistency analysis and adversarial learning frameworks that expose inconsistencies between modalities. We further characterize the architectural families of existing fake news detectors for short-form videos, ranging from feature-based multimodal models with late fusion to deep fusion architectures that capture richer cross-modal relationships. This survey highlights open challenges and future research directions for developing robust, interpretable, and multilingual claim detection systems that can scale across diverse video formats and manipulation techniques.


# 2 Introduction

The rapid proliferation of short-form video platforms such as TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts has fundamentally transformed how information is created, consumed, and disseminated across global audiences. These platforms prioritize engaging, easily digestible content, often leveraging sophisticated recommendation algorithms that amplify viral material regardless of its veracity. This ecosystem has created fertile ground for the spread of misinformation, disinformation, and various forms of harmful content, ranging from manipulated media and misleading claims to coordinated influence operations. Unlike traditional text-based social media, short-form videos present unique challenges for content moderation due to their multimodal nature, combining visual imagery, audio signals, on-screen text, and dynamic editing techniques within compressed timeframes. The sheer volume of content uploaded daily, coupled with the linguistic diversity of global platforms and the increasing sophistication of AI-generated manipulations, has rendered conventional moderation approaches insufficient. Consequently, there is an urgent need for automated detection systems capable of analyzing these multimodal signals at scale to identify false claims, manipulated media, and potentially harmful material before it reaches vulnerable audiences.

Despite growing research interest in this domain, several critical challenges remain inadequately addressed in the current literature. First, the detection of claims and checkworthiness in short-form videos has predominantly relied on linguistic analysis through automatic speech recognition and text-based processing, largely neglecting the rich signals present in non-linguistic visual and acoustic modalities. This text-centric bias creates significant limitations for multilingual and cross-lingual applications, where language-specific models may fail to generalize across the diverse linguistic landscape of global platforms. Second, while various multimodal fusion architectures have been proposed for fake news detection in short-form videos, there exists considerable fragmentation in methodological approaches, with limited systematic understanding of how different fusion strategies perform across varying content types and manipulation techniques. Third, the emerging challenge of explainable forensic analysis for manipulated video segments, particularly in longer-form content that may circulate on short-form platforms in truncated versions, remains underexplored. Finally, the research community lacks comprehensive benchmarking resources and standardized evaluation protocols that span the full spectrum of misinformation types, modalities, and languages relevant to short-form video platforms.

This survey makes several substantial contributions to address these gaps in the literature. First, we provide a comprehensive and structured taxonomy of detection methods for misinformation and harmful content on short-form video platforms, systematically categorizing approaches based on their architectural design, modality utilization, and fusion strategies. We examine three principal families of methods: feature-based multimodal models that employ strong single-modality encoders with late fusion, deep fusion architectures that capture richer cross-modal interactions through joint representation learning, and emerging approaches that incorporate adversarial training and uncertainty estimation for more robust and interpretable predictions. Our taxonomy further distinguishes methods based on their handling of linguistic versus non-linguistic signals, highlighting the critical but often overlooked role of visual and acoustic cues that can generalize across language boundaries. This organizational framework enables researchers to identify methodological trends, understand the evolution of detection capabilities, and recognize underexplored areas warranting further investigation.

Second, we conduct an extensive analysis of available datasets and evaluation resources, documenting their characteristics, limitations, and suitability for different detection tasks. This includes datasets focused on claim detection, checkworthiness assessment, fake news identification, and manipulated media forensics across multiple languages and platform contexts. We pay particular attention to the challenges of dataset construction for short-form video content, including temporal constraints, multimodal annotation complexity, and the rapid evolution of platform-specific features and trends. Third, we synthesize insights from recent advances in non-linguistic claim detection, multilingual checkworthiness assessment, cross-modal consistency analysis, and explainable forensic techniques, identifying promising directions for future research. We highlight the potential of approaches that leverage visual and auditory cues for language-agnostic claim detection, the importance of interpretable models that can localize and explain manipulated segments, and the need for systems that can adapt to emerging manipulation techniques including realistic AI-generated content.

The aim of this survey is to provide researchers and practitioners with a comprehensive understanding of the current landscape of misinformation and harmful content detection on short-form video platforms, while identifying critical gaps and promising research directions. The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. We begin by establishing foundational concepts and defining the scope of misinformation and harmful content in the context of short-form video platforms. We then present our taxonomy of detection methods, examining feature-based multimodal models, deep fusion architectures, and adversarial approaches in detail. Subsequently, we survey available datasets and evaluation resources, analyzing their characteristics and limitations. We then discuss emerging research directions, including non-linguistic claim detection, multilingual checkworthiness assessment, explainable forensics, and cross-modal consistency analysis. Finally, we conclude with a synthesis of key findings and recommendations for advancing the field toward more robust, generalizable, and interpretable detection systems capable of operating at the scale and diversity required by contemporary short-form video platforms.


# 3 Cross-Modal Consistency for Short-Form Video Fake News Detection

## 3.1 Claim Detection in Visual and Non-Linguistic Audio Modalities

The challenge of detecting claims in visual and non-linguistic audio modalities has received limited direct attention in the literature, though related work provides a conceptual foundation. Kan et al. (2024) illustrate the intuition behind this task through the example of a video in an unfamiliar language: even without recognizing any spoken words, a viewer can often distinguish between content that is unlikely to contain an informative claim—such as a nursery rhyme or dance video—and content that likely does, such as a podcast or news clip. This observation suggests that non-linguistic visual and acoustic cues carry signals relevant to claim presence, motivating approaches that do not depend on full textual understanding.

While Kan et al. (2024) frame the problem and propose a system that incorporates masked autoencoders for claim detection, the provided evidence does not detail specific architectural choices, training procedures, or evaluation results for the visual and non-linguistic audio components. The broader research landscape has primarily addressed claim detection through linguistic channels. Vatndal and Setty (2025) survey several systems—including BRENDA for real-time claim detection in long-form text, FACTCHECKEDITOR and PODFC for multilingual verification with the latter tailored to long-form audio, and LIVEFC for real-time fact-checking of audio streams—but these approaches rely on transcription and text-based claim detection rather than directly modeling visual or non-linguistic audio features. The gap between the motivating intuition articulated by Kan et al. (2024) and the predominantly text-centric methods documented in the literature highlights an open area for future work in claim detection from non-linguistic modalities.

## 3.2 Multilingual Checkworthiness Detection in TikTok Videos

Multilingual checkworthiness detection in short-form videos, particularly on platforms like TikTok, presents unique challenges that have recently begun to attract research attention. Vatndal and Setty (2025) introduced ShortCheck, a system designed for this task, and evaluated it on both Norwegian and English TikTok videos. Their model demonstrated strong performance, notably achieving a recall of 0.85 for checkworthy instances in Norwegian content. This work highlights the viability of automated checkworthiness detection across languages in the short-form video domain.

While not focused on TikTok specifically, Kan et al. (2024) offer a broader perspective on the role of multimodality in claim-related video analysis. They argue that even without understanding a video’s spoken language, visual and auditory cues can help distinguish content likely to contain an informative claim, such as a podcast or news clip, from content that does not, like a nursery rhyme or dance video. This observation underscores the potential for multimodal approaches to generalize across languages, a principle that is directly relevant to the development of robust, multilingual checkworthiness detection systems for platforms like TikTok.

# 4 Multi-Modal Guarding Systems Against Video Misinformation

## 4.1 Explainable Forensic Detection of Manipulated Segments in Long-Form Videos

Recent work has begun to address the challenge of explainable forensic detection of manipulated segments specifically within untrimmed, long-form videos. Feng et al. (2026) introduced a dedicated benchmark and a baseline method aimed at promoting research into robust and interpretable video authenticity analysis. Their work emphasizes the importance of developing forensic techniques that can localize and explain manipulations in lengthy video content, which is increasingly crucial for combating misinformation involving realistic AI-generated media. To support reproducibility and further investigation, the authors have committed to open-sourcing their dataset and code.

In parallel, the detection of fake news in short-form videos has been approached through cross-modal consistency analysis. Tian et al. (2026) proposed a framework that exposes inconsistencies between modalities by employing adversarial learning and joint optimization. Their method involves intra-modal refinement, cross-modal fusion, and contrastive adversarial training to produce a classification prediction along with a confidence score and uncertainty estimation. While this work targets short-form content, its focus on interpretable cross-modal consistency signals offers relevant insights for explainable forensic approaches that must scale to longer video formats.

## 4.2 Exposing Cross-Modal Consistency in Short-Form Video Fake News Detection

Tian et al. (2026) observe that existing fake news detectors for short-form videos can be organized into three families. Feature-based multimodal models, such as FakeSV, employ strong single-modality encoders with late fusion but only weakly exploit cross-modal interactions. In contrast, deep fusion architectures—including TikTec, FANVM, SVFEND, FakingRecipe, and KDSGAT-FNVD—aim to capture richer relationships across modalities (Tian et al., 2026). The broader motivation for advancing such detection capabilities is underscored by the growing risk that targeted video manipulations pose to the reliability of visual media (Feng et al., 2026).

# 5 Future Directions

Despite growing interest in multimodal video verification, the current research landscape exhibits several critical limitations that constrain progress toward robust and generalizable detection systems. The most salient gap is the persistent reliance on transcription-based pipelines for claim detection, which fundamentally limits applicability across the hundreds of languages spoken on global short-form video platforms. While conceptual work has demonstrated that non-linguistic visual and acoustic cues can distinguish claim-bearing content from non-informative material, this intuition remains largely untested through rigorously evaluated systems with clearly documented architectures and training procedures. Furthermore, multilingual checkworthiness detection has been validated on only a narrow set of languages, leaving generalizability to low-resource and typologically diverse languages uncertain. The integration of explainable forensic localization with cross-modal consistency analysis also remains underdeveloped, particularly for short-form content where temporal constraints and rapid editing patterns pose unique challenges. Additionally, the field lacks unified benchmarks that span the full spectrum of video lengths, manipulation types, and linguistic contexts, making systematic comparison across methods difficult.

Future research should prioritize the development of end-to-end multimodal claim detection systems that operate directly on raw visual and non-linguistic audio streams without requiring transcription as an intermediate step. Such systems would need carefully designed architectures capable of extracting claim-relevant patterns from facial expressions, gestures, scene composition, speaking rate, intonation contours, and background acoustic environments. Comprehensive benchmarking efforts should establish standardized evaluation protocols that measure performance across diverse languages, video durations, and manipulation techniques, enabling rigorous comparison between linguistic and non-linguistic approaches. Researchers should also investigate hybrid fusion strategies that combine non-linguistic claim detection with existing text-based pipelines to create robust ensembles that maintain effectiveness when any single modality is degraded or unavailable. In parallel, explainable forensic methods must be extended to handle the unique characteristics of short-form content while preserving the localization precision developed for longer videos. This includes designing adversarial training frameworks that not only detect cross-modal inconsistencies but also produce human-interpretable explanations identifying which specific segments were manipulated and what visual or acoustic artifacts revealed the manipulation. Special attention should be devoted to cross-lingual transfer learning and domain adaptation techniques that enable systems trained on high-resource languages to perform effectively on low-resource languages without extensive retraining, potentially through shared non-linguistic feature representations.

Advancing these research directions would substantially strengthen societal defenses against visual misinformation at a global scale. Language-agnostic claim detection systems would enable fact-checking organizations and platform moderators to triage content across linguistic boundaries, dramatically expanding their reach to communities currently underserved by automated verification tools. Explainable forensic capabilities that precisely localize manipulations in both short-form and long-form videos would provide journalists, policymakers, and ordinary users with actionable evidence rather than opaque algorithmic decisions, fostering greater trust in automated systems and supporting more informed content moderation appeals. The development of robust multilingual checkworthiness detection would be particularly impactful for underrepresented language communities, reducing information inequities and helping curb the spread of misleading content on globally accessible platforms where linguistic diversity far outpaces the availability of language-specific verification resources. By bridging the persistent gap between linguistic and non-linguistic approaches, the next generation of video verification systems could offer comprehensive, interpretable, and genuinely language-agnostic protection against the evolving landscape of visual misinformation.


# 6 Conclusion

This survey has systematically examined the emerging landscape of claim detection, checkworthiness assessment, and forensic analysis across short-form and long-form video modalities, with particular emphasis on methods that operate independently of full textual understanding. Our analysis reveals a pronounced gap between the well-founded intuition that non-linguistic visual and acoustic cues carry claim-relevant signals and the predominantly text-centric approaches that characterize the current state of the art. While systems such as ShortCheck have demonstrated the viability of multilingual checkworthiness detection on platforms like TikTok, and cross-modal consistency frameworks have advanced interpretable fake news detection through adversarial learning and joint optimization, the direct modeling of visual and non-linguistic audio features for claim detection remains largely unexplored in terms of rigorously evaluated architectures and training methodologies. We have identified three principal methodological families—feature-based multimodal models with limited cross-modal interaction, deep fusion architectures that capture richer inter-modal relationships, and emerging approaches that prioritize explainability alongside detection performance—each offering distinct trade-offs between computational efficiency, detection accuracy, and interpretability. Furthermore, our examination of explainable forensic detection for manipulated segments reveals that while benchmarks have recently been established for untrimmed long-form videos, the integration of forensic localization with cross-modal consistency analysis for short-form content remains underdeveloped, and the field lacks unified evaluation protocols that span the full spectrum of video formats, manipulation types, and linguistic contexts.

The significance of this survey lies in its comprehensive synthesis of a fragmented research landscape at the intersection of multimodality, multilinguality, and misinformation detection. By systematically categorizing detection methods based on their architectural design, modality utilization, and fusion strategies, and by documenting the characteristics and limitations of available datasets and evaluation resources, this work provides researchers with a structured foundation for understanding both the progress achieved and the critical gaps that persist. The survey highlights a fundamental tension that defines the current state of the field: although compelling conceptual motivation exists for leveraging visual and acoustic signals in a language-agnostic manner, the empirical and architectural foundations for doing so remain nascent, with most systems defaulting to transcription-dependent pipelines. Moreover, the concentration of research on a narrow set of languages and platform contexts constrains the generalizability of existing solutions, leaving low-resource language communities and diverse cultural contexts underserved. By identifying these boundaries and articulating the relationships between disparate research threads, this survey offers a roadmap for advancing the field toward more robust, interpretable, and inclusive verification systems.

The path forward demands a concerted effort to bridge the gap between linguistic and non-linguistic approaches to video verification. Researchers must prioritize the construction of large-scale, diverse datasets annotated for claim presence across languages and cultural contexts, enabling the development of systems that do not depend on transcription as a prerequisite for claim detection. Architectural innovations are needed to extract claim-relevant patterns directly from facial expressions, gestures, scene composition, speaking rate, intonation contours, and background acoustic environments, and to fuse these signals with text-based pipelines in robust ensembles that maintain effectiveness when any single modality is degraded or unavailable. Equally critical is the extension of explainable forensic methods to handle the unique temporal constraints and editing patterns of short-form content while preserving the localization precision developed for longer videos, thereby enabling not only the identification of potentially misleading material but also the precise and interpretable attribution of manipulated segments. As synthetic media generation technologies continue to advance, the ability to verify content across modalities and languages without full textual understanding will become an indispensable component of the information integrity ecosystem. Achieving this vision will require interdisciplinary collaboration spanning computer vision, speech processing, natural language understanding, and human-computer interaction, alongside open-source tooling and rigorous benchmarking efforts that ensure systems are as adaptive and multimodal as the media they seek to scrutinize.
