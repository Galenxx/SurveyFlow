# 1 Abstract

The rapid growth of short-form video content on social media platforms has intensified the spread of multimodal misinformation, yet current detection systems often suffer from high false positive rates and insufficient exploitation of cross-modal relationships. This survey provides a structured overview of recent advances in multimodal video misinformation detection, with particular emphasis on claim detection, checkworthiness assessment, and cross-modal consistency modeling. We examine how dedicated claim detection modules serve as critical gating mechanisms that filter out non-factual content before resource-intensive verification, thereby reducing erroneous flagging of harmless videos, and review the emergence of specialized datasets curated from platforms such as TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts alongside benchmarks for detecting manipulated segments in untrimmed long-form videos. A central finding is that existing multimodal detectors, whether employing late fusion or deep fusion architectures, consistently underutilize cross-modal interactions, motivating a new generation of methods that explicitly evaluate alignment or contradiction between visual and textual streams. By synthesizing these developments, this survey identifies persistent gaps and charts future directions for building more accurate and interpretable video verification systems.


# 2 Introduction

The rapid rise of short-form video platforms has reshaped the global information landscape, with services such as TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts now serving as primary channels for content creation and consumption for billions of users. These platforms are engineered to maximize engagement through brief, visually compelling formats and algorithmic recommendation systems that can propel content to viral status within hours. While this ecosystem has lowered barriers to creative expression and enabled diverse voices to reach wide audiences, it has also introduced profound risks for the spread of misinformation and harmful content. Short-form videos present a uniquely challenging medium for content moderation because they combine moving imagery, spoken narration, on-screen text, background music, and rapid editing into a dense multimodal package that can convey false or misleading claims with exceptional persuasive power. The speed at which such content circulates, coupled with the emotional resonance of video as a format, means that corrections and fact-checks often arrive too late to counteract the initial impact. As public health crises, electoral processes, and financial systems increasingly become targets of coordinated disinformation campaigns operating through short-form video, the development of automated, scalable, and reliable detection mechanisms has become an urgent research priority with direct societal implications.

Despite growing research attention, the detection of misinformation in short-form videos remains beset by several fundamental problems that distinguish it from more established areas such as text-based or image-based misinformation analysis. A persistent and practically significant issue is the high rate of false positives, where systems incorrectly flag satirical, entertainment, or personal content as misinformative, leading to over-censorship and erosion of user trust in moderation processes. This problem is intimately connected to the challenge of determining whether a video even contains a factual claim in the first place, a prerequisite step that many existing pipelines overlook or treat only superficially. Beyond claim detection, the multimodal nature of short-form video demands analytical frameworks that can reason about the relationships between visual, auditory, and textual streams, yet current architectures consistently underutilize cross-modal interactions. Late fusion models that process each modality independently and combine representations only at a final stage fail to capture subtle contradictions or misalignments between what is shown and what is stated. Even deep fusion architectures, which integrate modalities more tightly during processing, often lack explicit mechanisms for evaluating cross-modal consistency. Additional complexity arises from the need to detect manipulated segments within videos, particularly as AI-generated alterations become increasingly sophisticated and difficult to distinguish from authentic footage, and from the scarcity of large-scale, ecologically valid benchmark datasets that reflect the true diversity of short-form video content across platforms, languages, and cultural contexts.

This survey makes several significant contributions to the field of misinformation detection on short-form video platforms. First, it provides a comprehensive and structured taxonomy of detection methods, systematically categorizing approaches based on their treatment of multimodal data, including feature-based fusion models, deep fusion architectures, and emerging paradigms that explicitly model cross-modal consistency and contradiction. By mapping the landscape of existing techniques, the survey identifies persistent gaps in current research, particularly the insufficient exploitation of cross-modal interactions and the need for interpretable forensic analysis as AI-generated content grows more sophisticated. Second, the survey offers an in-depth examination of the critical yet often overlooked claim detection stage, which serves as a gating mechanism to filter out non-factual content before resource-intensive verification processes are applied. This focus on reducing false positives represents a pragmatic advancement toward deployable, real-world moderation systems that balance accuracy with scalability.

In addition to methodological contributions, this survey consolidates and analyzes the emerging body of datasets and benchmark resources developed specifically for short-form video misinformation research. It examines handcrafted datasets sourced from TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts, as well as those curated from fact-checking platforms, highlighting the diversity of annotation strategies, linguistic and cultural coverage, and task formulations. The survey further addresses the challenge of detecting manipulated video segments, reviewing dedicated benchmarks and baseline methods designed for forensic analysis of untrimmed content. By synthesizing these resources, the survey provides researchers with a clear understanding of available data, their limitations, and directions for future dataset development. The aim of this survey is to serve as a foundational reference for researchers and practitioners working at the intersection of multimedia forensics, natural language processing, and content moderation. The paper is structured as follows: we begin by establishing the background and defining the scope of misinformation and harmful content on short-form video platforms, then present a detailed taxonomy of detection methods, followed by an analysis of datasets and evaluation benchmarks, and conclude with a discussion of open challenges and promising directions for future research.


# 3 Multi-Modal Claim Detection for Video Misinformation

## 3.1 Multimodal Claim Detection for False Positive Reduction

A key challenge in video misinformation detection is the risk of false positives, where harmless videos are incorrectly flagged as misinformative. Kan et al. (2024) address this issue directly through a dedicated Claim Detection task designed to reduce such errors. Their approach analyzes the visual and non-linguistic audio portions of a video to filter out content that likely does not present any factual information, effectively weeding out videos before they undergo further misinformation assessment. The authors characterize this process as analogous to watching a video to determine whether it makes a claim at all.

More broadly, the shift toward multimodal analysis reflects a growing recognition that text alone is insufficient for evaluating video content. Vatndal and Setty (2025) note that multimodal misinformation has spurred the development of techniques that fuse text, audio, and visual cues. Within this landscape, the claim detection step serves a critical gating function, ensuring that only videos containing substantive claims proceed to more resource-intensive verification stages, thereby reducing the overall false positive rate in automated fact-checking pipelines.

## 3.2 Checkworthy TikTok Video Detection for Fact-Checkers

The development of resources for detecting checkworthy claims in short-form video is an emerging area of work. Kan et al. (2024) introduced ViMGuard, a multi-modal system for guarding against video misinformation, and evaluated it on a handcrafted dataset of approximately 700 short-form videos scraped from TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts, supplemented with homemade recordings, all manually labeled for their task. In a related line of work, Vatndal and Setty (2025) curated a dataset of TikTok videos sourced from fact-checking platforms including Snopes, PolitiFact, FactCheck.org, and Health Feedback, which they manually annotated for checkworthiness using a defined set of guidelines.

# 4 Short-Video Forensics Adapted for Long-Form Manipulated Segments

## 4.1 Sliding-Window Forensics for Long-Form Manipulated Segments

Feng et al. (2026) address the challenge of detecting manipulated segments within untrimmed, long-form videos by introducing a dedicated benchmark and a baseline method designed for this task. Their work emphasizes the need for robust and interpretable forensic analysis, which is critical for combating misinformation as AI-generated content becomes increasingly realistic. The authors commit to open-sourcing both the dataset and code to facilitate reproducibility and encourage future research in this area.

# 5 Cross-Modal Consistency for Short-Form Fake News Detection

## 5.1 Exploiting Cross-Modal Consistency for Fake News Detection

A key limitation in current multimodal fake news detection for short videos is the insufficient exploitation of cross-modal interactions. Tian et al. (2026) categorize existing detectors into three families, noting that feature-based multimodal models, such as FakeSV, rely on strong single-modality encoders with late fusion but only weakly capture cross-modal relationships. Deep fusion architectures, including TikTec, FANVM, SVFEND, FakingRecipe, and KDSGAT-FNVD, attempt more integrated representations, yet Tian et al. (2026) position their own work as a response to the persistent gap in explicitly modeling cross-modal consistency. This observation motivates approaches that directly examine whether the visual and textual streams of a short video convey mutually reinforcing or contradictory information, thereby providing a stronger signal for veracity assessment.

# 6 Future Directions

Current detection pipelines remain constrained by a narrow focus on individual modalities and isolated tasks, limiting their real-world applicability. Most existing work targets either short-form or long-form video in isolation, without addressing the fluid ecosystem in which clips are extracted, recontextualized, and redistributed across platforms. The heavy reliance on curated, handcrafted datasets further restricts generalization, as models trained on platform-specific or topic-specific data struggle when exposed to the diverse, rapidly evolving content found in the wild. Moreover, while claim detection has emerged as a valuable gating mechanism for reducing false positives, its integration with downstream verification and manipulated segment localization remains largely unexplored, leaving a fragmented landscape where each component operates in a silo. The insufficient modeling of cross-modal consistency, particularly the nuanced ways in which visual and textual streams can subtly contradict rather than overtly clash, represents another persistent gap that undermines detection accuracy. Current architectures, whether employing late fusion or deep fusion strategies, consistently underutilize the rich semantic relationships between modalities, treating cross-modal alignment as a byproduct rather than a core computational objective.

Future research should pursue unified, end-to-end architectures that jointly perform claim detection, cross-modal consistency reasoning, and fine-grained manipulation localization within a single framework. Such systems would move beyond treating these as sequential, independent stages and instead allow signals from each task to inform and refine the others, mimicking how human fact-checkers simultaneously evaluate whether a video asserts something factual, whether its modalities align, and whether specific segments have been altered. A second critical direction involves developing temporally aware models capable of handling videos of arbitrary length without relying on trimmed or pre-segmented inputs. This would enable detection systems to operate natively on the full, unedited footage that increasingly populates social media feeds, eliminating the blind spots that arise when manipulated segments are embedded within otherwise authentic long-form videos. Complementing these architectural advances, future work must prioritize the creation of large-scale, ecologically valid benchmarks that span multiple platforms, capture diverse manipulation types, and reflect the natural distribution of both misleading and benign content. Such datasets should be constructed through systematic sampling from live platforms and annotated with rich, multi-faceted labels that support the joint training of compound detection objectives, including explicit annotations for cross-modal contradictions and subtle forms of semantic misalignment.

Pursuing these directions would fundamentally reshape the practical utility of automated video misinformation detection. Unified architectures would reduce computational overhead and latency, making real-time or near-real-time triage feasible at the scale required by major platforms, thereby enabling proactive filtering that intercepts misleading content before it achieves viral reach. Temporally aware models would extend the applicability of detection systems to the full range of video content encountered in practice, closing a critical vulnerability that current systems fail to address. Ecologically valid benchmarks would drive the development of models that generalize beyond laboratory conditions, directly addressing the false positive problem that remains a central barrier to deployment. Collectively, these advances would enable a shift from reactive, post-hoc fact-checking toward preventative content moderation that reduces societal harm while preserving legitimate non-factual expression such as satire, opinion, and entertainment. Furthermore, the development of interpretable forensic methods capable of localizing and explaining manipulations would enhance transparency and user trust, essential requirements for any moderation system operating at scale in democratic societies.


# 7 Conclusion

The proliferation of short-form video platforms has fundamentally altered the information ecosystem, creating an urgent need for automated detection systems that can operate at scale while minimizing false positives. This survey has systematically examined the state of the art in multimodal video misinformation detection, revealing a field in active transition from unimodal text analysis toward integrated architectures that fuse visual, audio, and textual cues. A central finding is the emergence of claim detection as a critical gating mechanism that filters out non-factual content before resource-intensive verification, directly addressing the persistent problem of erroneously flagging harmless videos such as satire, entertainment, and personal anecdotes. The review further documents the progression from feature-based late fusion models, which only weakly capture cross-modal relationships, to deep fusion architectures that attempt more integrated representations, yet consistently fall short of explicitly modeling whether visual and textual streams convey mutually reinforcing or contradictory information. Recent work targeting cross-modal consistency and the forensic localization of manipulated segments in untrimmed long-form video represents a promising corrective to these limitations, signaling a shift toward more interpretable and fine-grained analysis. The survey also consolidates the emerging landscape of specialized datasets curated from TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts, highlighting both the diversity of annotation strategies and the persistent scarcity of large-scale, ecologically valid benchmarks that span multiple platforms and manipulation types.

The significance of this survey lies in its structured synthesis of technical trajectories, resource landscapes, and persistent gaps within a rapidly maturing research domain. By mapping the operational logic of modern detection pipelines and identifying where they remain vulnerable, the paper provides a clear diagnostic of current capabilities and limitations. The emphasis on claim detection as a gating function offers a pragmatic pathway toward deployable systems that balance accuracy with scalability, while the identification of cross-modal consistency as an underexploited signal reveals a fundamental architectural shortcoming that cuts across existing model families. The consolidation of dataset resources and forensic benchmarks further equips researchers with a clear understanding of available tools and their constraints, enabling more targeted future contributions. This synthesis underscores that the field stands at a critical juncture where incremental improvements in fusion techniques are insufficient; what is needed are models that treat cross-modal reasoning as a core computational objective rather than an emergent property of feature concatenation.

Moving forward, the research community must prioritize the development of unified, temporally aware architectures that jointly perform claim detection, cross-modal consistency reasoning, and fine-grained manipulation localization within a single framework. Such systems would mirror the integrative reasoning of human fact-checkers and enable real-time triage at the scale demanded by major platforms. This architectural ambition must be matched by concerted efforts to construct large-scale, ecologically valid benchmarks that capture the fluid, cross-platform nature of contemporary video misinformation and support the joint training of compound detection objectives. Furthermore, as AI-generated manipulations grow increasingly indistinguishable from authentic recordings, interpretable forensic methods capable of localizing altered segments will become indispensable for maintaining user trust and enabling transparent moderation decisions. Collaborative open-sourcing of datasets, benchmarks, and detection tools will be essential for accelerating progress and ensuring reproducibility across the field. The fight against video misinformation ultimately demands an interdisciplinary effort that bridges computer vision, natural language processing, and forensic analysis to build systems that are not only accurate but also transparent, trustworthy, and capable of intercepting harmful content before it achieves viral reach.
