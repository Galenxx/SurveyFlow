# 1 Abstract

The rapid growth of short-form video platforms has intensified the spread of multimodal misinformation, where visual, auditory, and textual elements converge to create persuasive but potentially deceptive content. Automated detection systems remain predominantly reliant on linguistic analysis, leaving a critical gap in the exploitation of non-linguistic audio-visual cues and cross-modal interactions. This survey systematically examines recent advances in multimodal claim detection and checkworthiness assessment, with a particular focus on short-form video content and the unique challenges posed by non-linguistic modalities. We review modular detection pipelines, masked autoencoder-based approaches for language-independent claim identification, and emerging techniques for explainable manipulation detection in untrimmed long videos. Our analysis reveals that current systems predominantly employ late-fusion strategies that only weakly capture cross-modal interactions, failing to verify whether visual, textual, and auditory streams convey mutually consistent information. We identify consistency-based reasoning and deep cross-modal fusion as promising directions for advancing the robustness of video claim detection systems, particularly for detecting subtle inter-modal discrepancies that signal manipulation. This survey provides a comprehensive foundation for researchers seeking to develop more sophisticated multimodal approaches that move beyond transcription-centric paradigms to combat video-based misinformation.


# 2 Introduction

The rapid proliferation of short-form video platforms such as TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts has fundamentally transformed the digital information landscape. These platforms prioritize highly engaging, easily consumable content, often lasting less than a minute, and leverage sophisticated recommendation algorithms that can amplify both legitimate information and harmful falsehoods with unprecedented speed and scale. Unlike traditional text-based or long-form video media, short-form videos present a uniquely dense multimodal experience, where visual imagery, spoken language, on-screen text, music, and sound effects converge to create a persuasive and often emotionally resonant message. This convergence makes the format exceptionally effective for communication but equally potent for the spread of misinformation, disinformation, and other harmful content. The automated detection of such content is therefore a critical challenge, demanding novel computational methods that can reason across modalities in a manner that mirrors the richness of the medium itself.

Despite growing research interest, the field faces several interconnected and unresolved problems. A primary challenge is the effective integration of multimodal signals. Many existing detection systems function as feature-based multimodal models that rely on strong but independent encoders for text, audio, and video, fused only at a late stage, which results in a weak exploitation of cross-modal interactions. This limitation is particularly acute in short-form content, where a deceptive claim in the audio track may be seamlessly contradicted by the visual stream, a discrepancy that late-fusion systems are ill-equipped to detect. A second, related problem is the detection of checkworthy claims directly from non-linguistic audio-visual cues. Most current systems depend on transcribed speech for claim detection, leaving them ineffective when the spoken language is unavailable, unfamiliar, or deliberately obscured. Furthermore, the development and evaluation of robust detection models are hampered by a scarcity of specialized, high-quality datasets. While some manually labeled datasets of short-form videos exist, the field lacks standardized benchmarks that capture the diverse and subtle forms of manipulation found on these platforms, particularly those requiring fine-grained, cross-modal reasoning.

This survey paper makes two principal contributions to the study of misinformation and harmful content detection on short-form video platforms. First, it provides a comprehensive and structured taxonomy of current detection methods, categorizing them by their underlying architectures and the degree to which they model cross-modal interactions. We systematically review the evolution from unimodal linguistic analysis and late-fusion multimodal models to more advanced deep fusion architectures and emerging approaches that explicitly model cross-modal consistency. By charting this landscape, we identify a critical gap: the need for systems that can verify whether visual, textual, and auditory streams convey mutually consistent information, moving beyond the assumption that each input clip is a uniform whole. This analysis highlights a promising paradigm shift toward consistency-based reasoning, where subtle boundary discrepancies and inter-modal contradictions serve as key indicators of manipulation, a principle that is gaining traction in related fields such as long-form video forensics and is directly applicable to the short-form domain.

The second contribution is a detailed synthesis of the resources that underpin this research, including an analysis of existing datasets, evaluation protocols, and the unique challenges posed by non-linguistic modalities. We examine the construction and limitations of current benchmarks, from those designed for general checkworthiness assessment to those targeting specific phenomena like manipulated video segments. A particular focus is placed on the nascent area of claim detection from visual and acoustic cues alone, a capability motivated by the human ability to discern the presence of an informative claim without understanding the spoken language. By consolidating these resources and highlighting their methodological underpinnings, this survey not only serves as a guide for practitioners navigating the current state of the art but also illuminates the most pressing open problems. We aim to catalyze future work that bridges the gap between monolithic, late-fusion models and the nuanced, cross-modal reasoning required to effectively safeguard the integrity of the short-form video ecosystem.

The aim of this survey is to provide researchers and practitioners with a clear, structured overview of the methods, datasets, and multimodal approaches for detecting misinformation and harmful content on short-form video platforms. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. We begin by defining the scope of the problem and establishing a taxonomy of harmful content types and detection tasks. We then systematically review detection methods, progressing from unimodal and late-fusion approaches to deep fusion and cross-modal consistency models. A dedicated section surveys the available datasets and benchmarks, analyzing their characteristics and limitations. Subsequently, we discuss the unique challenges of non-linguistic claim detection and interpretability. Finally, we conclude by synthesizing key open problems and outlining promising directions for future research in this rapidly evolving field.


# 3 Multi-Modal Misinformation Detection and Checkworthiness Assessment

## 3.1 Claim Detection in Visual and Non-Linguistic Audio

The detection of claims in visual and non-linguistic audio modalities presents unique challenges, as informative content must be identified without reliance on transcribed speech. Kan et al. (2024) illustrate this scenario with the example of encountering a video in an unfamiliar language: a viewer may be unable to recognize any spoken words yet can still distinguish between genres such as a nursery rhyme or dance video, which likely lack an informative claim, and a podcast or news clip, which likely contain one. This observation motivates the use of visual and acoustic cues for claim detection in video content.

Prior systems have addressed claim detection primarily through linguistic channels, with extensions into audio processing. Vatndal and Setty (2025) survey several tools in this space, noting that BRENDA provides real-time claim detection for long-form text, while PODFC is tailored for long-form audio verification in multilingual contexts. LIVEFC handles real-time fact-checking of audio streams by combining transcription, claim detection, and evidence retrieval. These systems, however, depend on transcribed speech, leaving open the challenge of detecting claims directly from non-linguistic audio-visual signals, as suggested by the masked autoencoder approach referenced in Kan et al. (2024).

## 3.2 Checkworthiness Assessment for TikTok Videos

Checkworthiness assessment for short-form videos, particularly those on TikTok, has been approached through modular system designs. Vatndal and Setty (2025) propose ShortCheck, a modular, inference-only pipeline specifically designed to detect potentially misleading or checkworthy content in short-form videos such as those published on TikTok. The system assigns each video a binary categorical label—Checkworthy or Not Checkworthy—and its design emphasizes modularity over monolithic or end-to-end models (Vatndal & Setty, 2025).

The evaluation of such systems relies on datasets that include TikTok content. Kan et al. (2024) constructed a handmade dataset for evaluating their ViMGuard system, which comprised short-form videos scraped from TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts, as well as homemade videos recorded on a phone. The final dataset contained around 700 short-form videos, each manually labeled (Kan et al., 2024). While the evidence confirms the dataset included TikTok videos and was manually labeled, the specific labeling scheme used for checkworthiness assessment in that work is not detailed in the provided excerpts.

# 4 Explainable Cross-Modal Consistency for Fake News Forensics

## 4.1 Explainable Forensics for Long-Form Video Manipulations

The detection of manipulated segments in long-form videos presents distinct challenges compared to short-form content, particularly regarding interpretability. Feng et al. (2026) address this gap by introducing a dedicated benchmark and a baseline method for explainable forensic techniques that detect manipulated segments in untrimmed long videos. Their work promotes research into robust and interpretable video authenticity analysis, which they describe as crucial for combating misinformation in an era of increasingly realistic AI-generated content (Feng et al., 2026). To facilitate reproducibility and encourage future research on this task, the authors plan to release the associated dataset and code (Feng et al., 2026).

## 4.2 Cross-Modal Consistency for Short-Form Fake News Detection

Short-form video platforms have intensified the need for robust fake news detection, yet existing approaches exhibit a notable gap in modeling cross-modal interactions. Tian et al. (2026) categorize current short news video detectors into three families, observing that feature-based multimodal models rely on strong single-modality encoders with late fusion but only weakly exploit cross-modal interactions. While deep fusion architectures have been proposed to address this limitation, the core challenge of aligning and verifying information across modalities remains underexplored in short-form content.

A promising direction for strengthening detection lies in explicitly modeling cross-modal consistency. Although the evidence from Feng et al. (2026) focuses on long-form video forensics, their observation that manipulated segments often manifest as subtle boundary discrepancies and that conventional models are insensitive to such cues because they assume each input clip is entirely real or fake highlights a broader principle: detectors must move beyond treating multimodal inputs as uniform wholes. For short-form fake news, this suggests that verifying whether visual, textual, and auditory streams convey mutually consistent information could expose manipulations that single-modality or late-fusion systems miss. Tian et al. (2026) further underscore the need for approaches that go beyond weak cross-modal interaction, pointing toward consistency-based reasoning as a natural evolution for the field.

# 5 Future Directions

Current approaches to claim detection and checkworthiness assessment in video content remain heavily reliant on transcribed speech and linguistic channels, leaving a significant gap in the exploitation of non-linguistic visual and acoustic signals. Systems designed for short-form videos, while modular in design, predominantly employ late fusion strategies that only weakly capture cross-modal interactions, failing to verify whether visual, textual, and auditory streams convey mutually consistent information. Furthermore, the detection of manipulated segments in long-form videos suffers from limited interpretability, as conventional models often treat input clips as uniform wholes and are insensitive to subtle boundary discrepancies. The lack of robust, interpretable methods that can reason across modalities without depending on language transcription represents a critical limitation in combating misinformation across diverse video formats.

Future research should prioritize the development of cross-modal consistency reasoning frameworks that explicitly align and verify information across visual, auditory, and textual modalities. Such frameworks would move beyond late fusion by detecting inconsistencies between what is shown, what is heard, and what is stated in overlaid text or captions, thereby exposing manipulations that single-modality systems overlook. For short-form content, this could involve designing lightweight, inference-only architectures that compare semantic embeddings across modalities to flag contradictory signals, even in the absence of transcribed speech. For long-form videos, future work should extend these consistency checks to temporal boundary analysis, enabling models to pinpoint manipulated segments with interpretable explanations by identifying abrupt shifts in cross-modal coherence. Additionally, constructing larger, publicly available benchmark datasets with fine-grained labels for cross-modal consistency and manipulation boundaries will be essential for training and evaluating these next-generation systems.

Advancing cross-modal consistency reasoning would fundamentally strengthen the robustness of misinformation detection in an era of increasingly realistic AI-generated content. By enabling systems to detect claims and manipulations without relying on language transcription, these methods would extend fact-checking capabilities to multilingual and low-resource settings where speech recognition is unreliable or unavailable. Interpretable manipulation detection would also empower human fact-checkers and content moderators with actionable explanations, increasing trust in automated tools and accelerating the verification process. Ultimately, this line of research would contribute to a more resilient information ecosystem by making video authenticity analysis more comprehensive, accessible, and resistant to the evolving tactics of disinformation campaigns.


# 6 Conclusion

This survey has systematically examined the evolving landscape of multimodal claim detection and checkworthiness assessment for video-based misinformation, revealing a field in transition from linguistic dominance toward more balanced multimodal reasoning. The analysis of current detection pipelines demonstrates that while mature systems such as BRENDA, PODFC, and LIVEFC achieve reliable performance through transcribed speech, they remain fundamentally constrained by their dependence on textual representations of spoken content. The emergence of approaches that leverage visual and acoustic cues directly, including masked autoencoder strategies and the modular ShortCheck pipeline for short-form videos, represents a critical expansion beyond transcription-centric paradigms. In parallel, research on manipulated segment detection in long-form videos has elevated interpretability as a core requirement, with explainable forensic techniques addressing the subtle boundary discrepancies that conventional models systematically overlook. Across both short-form and long-form content, a consistent finding emerges: current multimodal detectors, particularly those employing feature-based late fusion, only weakly exploit cross-modal interactions, leaving a significant gap in the ability to verify whether visual, textual, and auditory streams convey mutually consistent information.

The significance of this survey lies in its synthesis of two research trajectories—claim detection and video forensics—that have largely developed in isolation despite addressing fundamentally overlapping challenges in misinformation defense. By examining systems designed for short-form platforms such as TikTok alongside those targeting untrimmed long-form videos, the survey identifies cross-modal consistency as a unifying concept capable of advancing both domains simultaneously. The finding that manipulated segments frequently manifest as boundary discrepancies, combined with the observation that short-form fake news detectors underutilize cross-modal alignment, exposes a structural weakness in prevailing architectures that treat multimodal inputs as uniform wholes. This survey further highlights the practical importance of modular, inference-only designs and interpretable outputs for real-world deployment, where fact-checkers and end users require transparent reasoning rather than opaque predictions. The manual construction of evaluation datasets drawn from TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts also underscores the community's recognition that platform-specific content demands tailored resources, yet the current scarcity of standardized benchmarks with fine-grained cross-modal labels remains a barrier to progress.

The path forward demands a concerted shift from weak cross-modal interaction toward consistency-based reasoning frameworks that actively verify alignment across modalities. Researchers must prioritize the development of architectures capable of detecting mutually contradictory information in visual, auditory, and textual streams, as such contradictions frequently signal manipulation or misleading framing that single-modality systems cannot perceive. The release of benchmark datasets and code, as planned for explainable long-form video forensics, must become standard practice to enable rigorous comparison and reproducibility across the field. Equally important is the expansion of checkworthiness assessment to incorporate non-linguistic cues, particularly for content in unfamiliar languages or without spoken narration, where visual and acoustic signals provide the only actionable evidence. By bridging the gaps between claim detection, checkworthiness assessment, and forensic analysis, and by embracing interpretability and cross-modal consistency as foundational design principles, the research community can construct more robust and generalizable defenses against the rapidly evolving tactics of video-based disinformation campaigns.
